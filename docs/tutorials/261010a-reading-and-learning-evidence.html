What the research says about how Spideryarn helps people read

Twenty-one questions about reading and learning, from retrieval practice to voice to AI summaries. Each mode is checked against the evidence, and the counter-evidence gets as much room as the support.

10 October 2026 · skim: 10 minutes (TL;DR, mental models, Pass 1, the judgment section) · deep read: about an hour · the library behind it: docs/project/reading-research-overview.md

TL;DR

The strongest ground Spideryarn stands on is the part where the reader produces something and is then shown the text: Recall, Quiz, explaining it to yourself. The "augment, don't replace" stance now has real support from three randomised studies in 2025, though none of them is about experts reading papers. Most of what the model makes for the reader (summaries, the tree, the Sketch) is supported only as an orienting aid. That help shrinks for readers who already know the field, and our readers often do.

The biggest gap is the reader we actually have. Nobody has measured what an expert remembers from a full paper days later, with or without a tool like ours. In the three small reading-tool studies that checked, the tool made reading faster or easier without a detected gain in correct answers.

Four ways to think about it

1. A gym, not a lift

Vision.md's forklift analogy, turned into a prediction. The memory literature says the work you do yourself is what stays: recalling, explaining, guessing before checking. A tool that lifts the weight for you makes the session easier and the muscle smaller. So for any mode, ask whether the reader or the model is doing the lifting.

2. A map helps a stranger more than a local

Help that suits a newcomer fades, and sometimes backfires, for someone who knows the ground. This is the expertise reversal effect, and it runs through half the library. A gap-free summary, a dense web of cross-links, a hand-holding tutor: each is worth most to the reader who needs it least in our audience.

3. Feeling fluent is not knowing

In three small controlled studies, reading tools made reading faster or feel easier, and readers got no more answers right. Readers are poor judges of what they understood even without help (correlation about 0.24), and AI help makes them more confident still. A mode that feels great is not yet evidence that it works.

4. A citation is a badge, not a check

A link to the source raises trust whether or not anyone opens it. In one study fewer than one in ten answers had a reference checked at all, and checking went with lower trust. "Every sentence is a door into the text" is only as good as how often the door is used.

Pass 1: the shape of the evidence

Twenty-one web researchers each took one question, such as "does retrieval practice work for prose?" or "does easy look-up stop you learning the word?". Each wrote a file of graded, cited findings. A digest then took the product's claims one at a time and gave each a verdict. There are five verdicts, and they are kept apart on purpose:

  • Supported good evidence in people and material close to ours.
  • Supported indirectly good evidence, but in students, short passages or a lab task. We are assuming it carries over.
  • Untested nobody has tested it anywhere close enough to count. Not the same as wrong.
  • No reliable effect / suggests otherwise tested, and absent, too small, or pointing the other way.
  • Evidence of harm tested, and it made something that matters worse.

Every finding is also graded twice. Certainty asks how solid the result is (High, Moderate, Low, Contested). Directness asks how close the people and material were to an adult reading a research paper (Direct, Indirect, Analogy). Figure 1 puts the product's main claims on those two axes. Almost everything sits in the middle column, because almost nothing was studied on our readers.

Two things to hold onto from here on

The population gap. Most of this research is on undergraduates, school pupils or second-language learners, reading short passages. An expert reading a forty-page paper in their own field is almost never the person studied.

The reading gap. Publishers blocked automated access, so most sources were read at abstract level. Every DOI was checked against Crossref and each resolves to the paper it is cited as. But the numbers are the topic files' numbers, and they should be checked against the paper before they leave the repo.

Pass 2: the twenty-one questions

Grouped into five themes. Each line is the answer in brief; open it for the evidence, the grades and what it means for the modes it touches. The file name is where the citations are.

Learning from what you read

Does recalling what you read beat re-reading it? Supported indirectly

Yes, from about a day on, and only with feedback. Pulling an article's content out of your own head beats looking at it again, for what you remember days or weeks later. Across 222 classroom studies the average benefit is moderate (g ≈ 0.5). It has been shown on roughly 2,500-word journal papers, not only word lists. Two catches: if the final test comes within minutes, re-reading looks better, so the benefit shows up later; and without the right answer shown afterwards, a hard test on journal papers did little. Recalling a whole paragraph transferred to new questions; fill-in-the-blank mostly did not.

Contrary: whether the effect survives complex, tightly interlinked material is disputed. The famous "retrieval beats concept-mapping" result partly vanished in a 2023 preregistered replication. No study looked at researchers reading papers in their field.

For Spideryarn: Recall and Quiz are built on this, and their "show the passage after the attempt" design is the right kind of feedback. What is missing is a later check (see the timing problem). File 01.

Do readers know what they understood? Do questions before reading help? Suggests otherwise

Readers are poor judges of their own understanding: the correlation between what they predict and what they score is about 0.24, across 94 studies. Writing a summary or keywords after a delay improves that (g = 0.46). Comparing your answer against a standard helps too.

Questions asked before reading help the content they ask about (g = 0.66), and almost nothing else (g = 0.01). So a pre-question steers attention; it is not general priming. ChatGPT-written pre-questions worked about as well as human ones in four experiments.

For Spideryarn: nothing in the product shows a reader their actual level except Quiz's marks. A confidence rating before the mark, and a "guess before you reveal" state on the top FAQ questions, are the obvious experiments. File 02.

What makes tutoring work, and is the "zone of proximal development" real? Supported indirectly

Tutoring helps by about 0.8 standard deviations, not Bloom's famous 2. Step-by-step computer tutors come close to humans. Help in small contingent steps is where the benefit lives. The zone of proximal development is a framing rather than a tested quantity. What is testable is "contingent help that fades", and even fading has thinner support than its fame suggests.

A difficulty the learner cannot overcome stops being desirable. This is the rationale for Recall's "tell after two rungs". Help that suits novices fades or reverses for experts (expertise reversal). The "85% rule" for how often to get things wrong comes from machine-learning classifiers, not people.

Contrary: no peer-reviewed study tests whether pitching a text easier or harder suits expert adults. File 03.

Do AI tutors and LLM help make people learn, or just perform? Supported indirectly

The clearest result, from 2025: about 1,000 high-school pupils doing maths practice with an unguarded GPT-4 scored 48% higher in practice. With the tool removed, they scored 17% lower than pupils who never had it. A guarded tutor package (teacher-written solutions, common mistakes, step-by-step hints, no answers handed over) avoided the drop but did not beat the control either (Bastani et al., PNAS; a published correction exists that nobody could open).

The one reading study: 405 pupils aged 14 to 15 read history passages. Three days later, LLM-only study did worse on comprehension and memory than note-taking alone or note-taking plus the LLM, and most pupils still preferred the LLM (Kreijkes et al. 2025).

Counterweight: a carefully scripted AI tutor beat in-class active learning in Harvard physics, on an immediate test only. And a much-cited meta-analysis showing a huge ChatGPT effect has been retracted. File 04.

Is doing it yourself better than being given it? Supported indirectly, less than the slogan says

Self-explanation helps: prompting people to explain material to themselves gives g = 0.55, and 0.79 for text. But the extra over simply being handed a good explanation is smaller: g = 0.35 against instructor-supplied explanations, from only six comparisons. Much of the benefit comes from being given an explanation at all.

The famous "interactive beats constructive beats active beats passive" ordering (ICAP) was never properly tested; its own authors call it a "gross first cut". Summarising is rated low-utility for students. Retrieval beat building a concept map. "Constructive" is not automatically best; what wins is whatever makes you do inferential work.

For Spideryarn: this is the cross-cutting question for every artefact the model makes. Being given a summary or a map is fine as orientation, especially for a newcomer. The reader would learn more by attempting something first, but by less than the slogans claim. File 08.

Does asking why someone is reading help? Supported, with a cost

A reader's goal changes what they learn: more of the relevant parts, less of the rest. Taking a different perspective later recovers ideas that were "unrecallable" under the first. Curiosity improves memory, even for incidental material. Giving learners control over sequencing and pacing has an overall effect near zero (g = 0.05). Interest-flavoured personalisation risks adding interesting-but-irrelevant detail, which hurts.

For Spideryarn: asking for a purpose is well founded. The cost is that it narrows what is remembered, and nothing in the product prompts for what the purpose did not ask about. File 20.

AI text and the source text

Does a summary beside the article help? Untested as built Harm if it replaces reading

An orienting passage ahead of the detail (an "advance organiser") helps, but only "if used in appropriate situations and measured properly". The one broad meta-analysis is from 1980. The same help fades or reverses for experts: in a 2025 meta-analysis, high-assistance instruction helped low-knowledge learners (d = +0.51), while low-assistance instruction was better for high-knowledge learners (d = −0.43).

When a summary replaced the passage, it helped weaker readers and hurt stronger ones (Etkin et al. 2025, 195 young adults). The size of the drop depends on how readers were split: d = 0.83 one way, 0.35 with an independent split, and the first may be partly regression to the mean.

Contested: the classic claim that experts learn more from gappier text failed to replicate inside its own paper and was reversed for skilled readers later. No study tests a summary read alongside a long article. File 05.

Does offloading to a tool cost you memory or skill? Depends on intent

Offloading is normal and often sensible. Its memory cost nearly vanishes when people know a test is coming, and saving something can improve memory for what you study next. The "Google effect" is shaky: its priming experiment failed to replicate twice. Surveys link heavier AI use to less self-reported critical thinking, but they are correlations.

AI inflates confidence: people solving problems with AI scored about 3 points higher and overestimated by about 4. "Cognitive surrender" and "metacognitive laziness" are named ideas with little or no data behind them yet; treat them as hypotheses. File 06.

Should the reader see the article's words or an AI's account of them? The article's words

Wrong automated advice drags people below what they achieve alone, replicated across many fields, and training does not reliably prevent it. LLM summaries of science abstracts overgeneralise at about five times the odds of expert-written ones (4,900 summaries), and asking for accuracy made it worse. That rate depends on the model and the prompt.

Citations raise trust even when they are random. Fewer than one in ten was hovered, and checking went with lower trust. AI explanations mostly raise agreement, right or wrong. A forced step (commit to your own answer first) cut over-reliance but was liked less.

For Spideryarn: verbatim Quotes and passage-only FAQ answers are well chosen. The exposed parts are the model-written bits around them: Skim's role lines, the reason a quote was chosen, the FAQ's questions. File 07.

Does checking a paper against the outside world help judge it? Supported indirectly

Lateral reading (leaving the page to learn about its source, as fact-checkers do) and teaching students to judge sources both work, but for students judging websites, not experts judging papers. Peer reviewers miss most errors: 607 BMJ reviewers found about 2.6 of 9 planted major errors, and brief training barely helped. That is the library's one Direct result about expert readers' own performance.

An LLM fact-check that correctly labelled 90% of the false headlines did not help people tell true from false, and where it was unsure or wrong it moved belief the wrong way. Human fact-checks did help. This backs Referee's "no verdict" rule. File 18.

On the page

Do highlighting and notes help? Memory yes, comprehension not reliably

The reader's own highlighting gives a small gain in memory (about 0.36) and no reliable gain in comprehension. Highlighting chosen by an instructor did better, about 0.44 for both. Taking notes beats not taking them, modestly. "Longhand beats laptop" did not survive a direct replication; it is contested, not settled.

For Spideryarn: Comments' bare mark is the weakest-evidenced act. A few words in your own words do more. File 09.

Does easy look-up stop you learning the word? No, against no help Untested against guessing first

Greg's question: "maybe the glossary, because it's constantly making it easy for you to look up the answer, means that you're not actually learning it." Glossed reading beats unglossed for learning words: 45% vs 27% of target words immediately, 33% vs 20% later (42 studies, second-language learners). So look-up does not cancel learning compared with no help.

But formats that make you choose before seeing beat plain definitions, and the memory literature says producing an answer before seeing it, even wrongly, helps when feedback follows. Nobody has compared "show it" with "guess first, then show it" for adults reading expository text. File 10.

Do links, previews and side panels help or distract? Help, less for experts

Putting related material next to what it explains helps (g ≈ 0.6), but the effect shrinks or reverses for readers who know the material, and "as close as possible" is not always best. Links cost little to read past; the cost is in deciding whether to follow them. The one controlled hover-preview study found speed gains and no change in effectiveness. File 11.

Does skimming in passes work? Does speed reading? Skim for triage Speed reading: no

Adults read non-fiction silently at about 238 words a minute, and about 100 when reading to remember. Skimming keeps the important ideas and loses detail and inference, and its benefit depended on easy navigation. Scim, the nearest published system to Skim, made readers faster with no accuracy gain. No rigorous trial of SQ3R, PQ4R or the three-pass method exists. RSVP (one word at a time) gives no reliable gain and removes looking back, which helps comprehension. The 2026-08-28 verdict to leave it unbuilt stands. File 12.

Do headings and an outline help? Supported indirectly

Headings and overviews improve memory for a text's topics and their organisation. Two cautions. When only some topics are signalled, the unsignalled ones are remembered worse than in a text with no signals. Signals also trade detail for breadth. No study compares model-made headings with an author's. File 13.

Typography, decoration, and the decorated-mode experiment Several settled no

"Bionic reading" does nothing (four studies, 2024–26). Hard-to-read fonts as a "desirable difficulty" failed to replicate (a 17-lab pooling). Reading on screen costs a little comprehension of expository text (g ≈ −0.2). A correction to our own earlier note: pre-highlighting by someone else is not "the worst" decoration. Accurate marks chosen by someone else helped a little in one meta-analysis and did nothing in a later experiment. Wrong marks hurt comprehension, and readers' confidence ran backwards against their scores. The real danger is model-quality marks being wrong. Marking author, model and reader voices in different typefaces has never been tested; within normal ranges a typeface does not change comprehension, so the cost is small. File 17.

Does search inside a piece change how you read the rest? Places, not answers, is the safe shape

Telling readers what is relevant improves learning of those parts and lowers it for the rest, so a reader who follows search hits remembers those passages better and the rest worse. Answering with the text open helps now and not after a day. Receiving a synthesised answer instead of the sources gives shallower learning. Search inside one document has never been tested. File 19.

Talking, and pictures

Is speaking better than typing? Faster and fuller Learning: not shown

Greg's belief: "voice is lower effort, it's faster. And … people probably say more and probably provide more context." Faster: about 2.9 times phone typing. More: spoken answers had more words and topics (1,001 people, randomised). Lower effort: only perceived effort has support. Better for learning: mixed. Spoken explaining elaborates more and written explaining organises better. In the one voice-versus-text AI tutor study (a preprint), voice nearly doubled interaction, cost 2.8 times as much, and gave no learning gain. File 14.

Do pictures help? What about AI-made ones? Decorative: about neutral

Explanatory pictures beside words help, mostly for newcomers. Purely decorative pictures are about neutral. Interesting but irrelevant material ("seductive details") hurts, more so when it comes first. Attractive design gives small gains, mostly in motivation. Nothing tests AI-made pictures, or shows that a wrong picture plants a false belief. That is exactly the risk Illustrated's own doc names. File 15.

Does a diagram of the argument help, if the reader did not draw it? Weakly Machine-drawn: untested

Concept maps help on average, and building one is associated with more benefit than studying one (g = 0.72 vs 0.43, across studies). But in a direct test, author-made organisers beat learner-made ones on transfer. Argument mapping's large effects shrink from g = 1.59 to 0.87 after correcting for publication bias, and they come from training people to build maps. There is no evidence on machine-drawn maps or on timelines for adults. File 16.

Prior art

What happened when HCI researchers built AI reading tools? Feel better, rarely truer

ScholarPhi (definitions in place), Scim (highlighted sentences in passes), Paper Plain (gists and plain-language definitions) and others. Where answer correctness was measured, the tool made no significant difference: 16/27 vs 16/27, 0.80 vs 0.76, 52% vs 50%. What moved was speed, ease and confidence. One Paper Plain reader who used only the gists rated their understanding highly and scored 2/7. No study measured delayed retention by expert adults. Spideryarn's own reader-study protocol (written reconstruction, blinded scoring, confidence against it) is stronger than most of this literature. File 21.

Pass 3: one reader, one paper, followed through

The worked example is the paper Greg was reading when he asked for this, arXiv 2609.01481, "Harness-of-Harness". Picture a reader who builds agent tooling. They open it in Spideryarn to compare the paper's planner, developer and tester loop with their own. Here is what they do, and what the evidence says at each step.

1

They say why they are reading

"Compare their loop with our Overseer." The profile and purpose go into every prompt. Supported A goal improves recall of what is relevant to it. The cost: it lowers recall of the rest, and this reader may never notice the paper's section on benchmarks because it was not what they came for.

2

They open the Brief summary first

Untested as built An orienting summary is the advance-organiser idea, which helps conditionally. But this reader knows the field, and help tuned to newcomers fades for experts. If they read the Brief instead of the paper, the 2025 evidence points to harm for strong readers. The Brief links every paragraph to its passages; whether they press those links is the open question.

3

They take Skim's first pass

Supported indirectly, for triage Skimming keeps the important ideas and loses detail and inference, and Skim's stepping makes navigation easy, which is what made skimming pay. The stops on pass one are what they will remember best, because a preview works only for what it points at. Scim's verdict applies: faster, not more correct.

4

They point at "harness" and the glossary card explains it

Supported against no help A gloss beats no gloss. Whether guessing first would make the term stick better is untested. ScholarPhi's lesson matters here: the paper uses "harness" in more than one sense, and readers took an incomplete definition list as complete.

5

They highlight a sentence about the tester, and add a note

Memory yes, comprehension not reliably The mark alone helps memory a little. The note in their own words does more, especially if they come back to it.

6

They ask chat whether the tester catches regressions

Nearest evidence is a caution Chat answers with citation chips. The 2025 trials say an AI that hands over answers lowers later unaided performance and raises confidence, and a citation raises trust whether or not it is opened. Asking "where does the piece say…" is the better kind of question than "what is the answer".

7

They look at Sources, then Reception

Supported indirectly Checking outside the document is what fact-checkers do. The rows are what others wrote, not a model's verdict, and the evidence says a verdict would not have helped.

8

They finish, and Learn asks them what they took from it

Supported indirectly This is the best-supported thing in the session: recall, then the passage. Recalling straight after reading is what the retention experiments did, so the timing is fine for memory. What is missing is a second look later: readers judge their own understanding better after a delay, and nothing brings them back.

What the evidence would change in that session

  • After step 1, a prompt later on for "what does the piece say that you didn't come for?".
  • At step 2, for a reader who says they know the field, "your one-line gist first" before the Brief.
  • At step 4, an optional "what do you think it means?" before the card. The card would also say when a term has more than one sense.
  • At step 5, "a few words" offered as the easy next step after the mark.
  • At step 6, sometimes "what do you think?" before answering, and a count of how often chips are pressed.
  • At step 8, the same Recall offered again tomorrow as a second check, and a confidence rating before Quiz marks.

Pass 4: the hard parts

The timing problem

Two different delays are easy to confuse, and GPT Sol's review caught this page's first draft confusing them.

  • The delay before the final test. Retrieval's advantage over re-reading shows up on a test a day or more later; on a test minutes later, re-reading can look better. That is about when you measure. Recalling straight after reading, as Learn does, is exactly what the retention experiments did.
  • The delay before a self-check. Readers judge their own understanding better when they summarise after a gap rather than at once. That is a reason for a second, later check.

So the timing of Recall is fine; what is missing is the later check. Whether a second recall a day later adds retention on top of the first is untested in this library, though spacing is named in learning-vision.md as "the strongest effect in the literature". It is not built, partly because the app has no scheduler.

The expert problem

Expertise reversal says help that suits a newcomer fades, or reverses, for someone who knows the field. It appears in the summary evidence, in the split-attention evidence, in signalling, and in multimedia. Our readers are often the experts, and several modes (a gap-free summary, dense cross-links, the tree, a tutor) are novice-shaped help. The reader profile is a self-description, while the effect depends on actual knowledge.

Where the files disagreed, and how the digest settled it

Five disagreements
  • Etkin et al. 2025 was graded Moderate in one file and Low in another. The one that read the paper found the "hurts strong readers" split may be partly regression to the mean, and the drop is d = 0.35 under an independent split. The digest uses Low–Moderate and the smaller figure.
  • Pre-highlighting: our 2026-08-28 note called it "the worst" decoration. Two files found accurate highlights help a little and wrong ones harm. The earlier note now carries a correction.
  • The reverse-cohesion effect (experts learn more from gappier text) is contested. The broader expertise-reversal meta-analysis is the safer ground.
  • Constructing versus viewing: both files agree that generating adds something on top of a good artefact, but less than the slogans claim.
  • A retracted meta-analysis of ChatGPT and learning (g = 0.87) is excluded. Bastani et al. has a correction nobody could open, so its figures are provisional.

How much to trust the library itself

Method, and its limits

Each researcher was told to cite only papers opened in that session, to check every DOI, to grade certainty and directness separately, and to give contrary evidence equal room. Publisher sites mostly blocked automated reading, so abstracts came through Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe PMC and Semantic Scholar. Each file says which sources were read in full. All 370 DOIs were then resolved against Crossref. 307 matched automatically. The rest were checked by hand and each resolves to the paper it is cited as. The researchers caught at least ten wrong DOIs among the leads they were given. GPT Sol reviewed the plan before the research and the digest after it, checking each conclusion against the topic files.

Judgment: where we disagree with the evidence, and what to try

Where the product and the evidence pull apart

The missing later check. Learn and Quiz come at the end of reading, which suits memory, but nothing brings the reader back for the delayed self-check that improves calibration.

Expert readers. Several modes are novice-shaped help, and the evidence says such help fades for experts.

Seeing is not doing. Most of what the model makes is looked at, not used.

Feeling is not understanding. Nothing shows a reader their actual level except Quiz's marks.

Links are not checks. "A door into the text" works only if the door is used, and we do not measure it.

Narrowing by goal. Purpose and search focus the reader and cost them the rest.

What could honestly be said

Only what the evidence carries, phrased no stronger. Check each number against the paper before it is printed.

  • Testing yourself on what you read beats re-reading it for keeping it, one of the best-replicated findings in learning research. Spideryarn's Learn mode is built on it.
  • In one 2025 randomised trial (Turkish high-school maths, in PNAS), unrestricted GPT-4 help left pupils worse off once it was gone. Spideryarn is designed to keep you reading the article itself. (The paper has a correction this library has not yet read.)
  • In one 2025 study of 4,900 science summaries, several LLMs overgeneralised, at rates that varied by model and prompt. Spideryarn keeps the author's text beside its AI help, and marks verified quotes as word for word.

Not to be said

That Spideryarn improves comprehension or retention. Nobody, including us, has measured that yet.

Questions you might ask

So is the glossary bad for learning?

No evidence says so. A gloss beats no gloss for learning words. What is untested is whether "guess first, then check" would beat "check", and the memory literature predicts it would. That is a cheap, optional experiment, not a reason to change the glossary.

Should we stop making summaries?

No. A summary is a reasonable orientation, especially for a newcomer. The risk is a summary replacing the reading, and the evidence that it can hurt strong readers. Keep it a press, never a default, keep its links to passages, and find out whether anyone presses them.

Is voice worth the cost?

For speed and how much people say: yes, that is the best-supported part of Greg's belief. For learning: not shown. Dictation is cheap and well founded. Live's case is fluency and preference, and the one comparison found it cost 2.8 times as much for no learning gain.

Why is so little of this about expert readers?

Because research is done where participants are: students in classes and labs. That is why "Indirect" is the most common grade, and why the reader study matters more than another literature search would.

Can we cite a number from this in a talk?

Open the paper first. Most sources were read at abstract level, and at least one key study has a correction nobody could open.

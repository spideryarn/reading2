Revisions and the schema

An article can be read again by the machine — re-fetched, re-extracted, re-summarised — without disturbing what the reader has done with it. This is the design that makes that true, what it costs, and the places it is thinner than it looks.

9 minutes to skim · ~30 to read everything · written 2026-09-03, against dev at 7db8b57c

TL;DR

Spideryarn never edits an article in place. Each run of the pipeline builds a whole new revision off to one side, and the article swaps to it in a single motion at the end — so a reader sees the complete old version or the complete new one, never a mixture, and a failed run costs them nothing.

A new revision starts as a copy of the published one, so asking for one more thing — a glossary, a timeline — does not re-do or re-pay for the rest. And everything the reader owns (their notes, their archive, their own title, their sharing setting) is deliberately stored somewhere a re-extraction cannot reach.

Four ways to picture it

1 · A photocopy on the desk

The article on the desk is the one everybody reads. To change it, you photocopy it, mark up the copy — for as long as that takes, minutes sometimes — and only at the very end swap the copy for the original in one motion. Nobody ever sees a half-marked page. The original goes in the drawer, not the bin.

2 · Seat numbers, not the people in them

Every paragraph gets a permanent seat number (spya-m4a7tn). Your note is filed against the seat, not against the words, so emptying seat 14 does not throw the note away — the seat number is never reused and never deleted. The catch is how seats are re-assigned after a re-extraction: by matching the text. A paragraph whose words are unchanged keeps its seat however far it has moved; a paragraph that was materially rewritten is treated as a new one, and the note about the old wording is kept but no longer attached to anything on the page.

3 · A shared document you took offline

You open a draft at 3:00 and save at 3:20. If somebody else saved at 3:10, your save would quietly bury theirs. So a draft writes down which version it started from, and the save is refused if the world has moved on. Spideryarn calls that column based_on_revision_id.

4 · A cast list with three columns

When the photocopy is made, each of the 46 fields on a revision has to be told what to do: mint a fresh value, derive it later from the content, or carry the old one across. A field nobody has classified is a compile error, not a default — because both possible defaults are wrong in a way nothing would notice.

Pass 1 What happens, end to end

Everything below follows one real article: Meditations On Moloch from Slate Star Codex, which lives in the corpus under the slug meditations-on-moloch and comes out as 289 blocks — paragraphs, headings, quotes — the first of which has the id spya-m4a7tn. Assume a reader added it weeks ago, left four comments on it, and has just asked for a glossary.

Asking for that creates a job: a short list of steps — fetch, extract, blocks, hierarchy, glossary, and so on — run one at a time. Pasting a new URL creates the same thing with a longer list.

Before the first step runs, the job opens a draft revision. Every step writes into that draft. When the last step finishes, the draft is checked and then published, which means one thing only: articles.current_revision_id is pointed at it.

Three consequences fall straight out of that, and they are most of what you need:

  • A failed job costs the reader nothing. The draft is simply never published; the article they had is the article they still have.
  • A partial job is impossible to see. There is no window in which the paragraphs are new and the outline is old.
  • Re-running one step is cheap, because the draft starts as a copy. Moloch's glossary job does not re-fetch the page, re-extract it, or re-summarise its 289 paragraphs.

Pass 2 The five nouns

There are 26 tables in the schema. Five of them carry this story, and the interesting thing is which side of the line each sits on. For Moloch: one articles row, a handful of article_revisions, 289 revision_blocks rows per revision, at least 289 block_identities rows shared by all of them, and four comments.

What each of the five actually holds

articles — one row per article, for as long as the article exists. The slug (the thing in the URL), the owner, a short permanent id, and the pointer at the current revision. It also carries the reader's relationship with the piece: archived_at, title_override, opens, last_opened_at, purpose ("why you're reading this one"), and the sharing switch. All of those are here on purpose — a re-extraction must not un-archive an article, forget the reader's own title, reset the count, or silently change who can see it.

article_revisions — one reading of the piece. It holds the title and byline as this extraction found them, what was fetched and when, and then the artefacts themselves: the outline tree, the glossary, the quotes, the ideas, the timeline, the quiz, the diagram. 46 columns in all. Several of them are JSON blobs, and § 7 says why that is allowed here and nowhere else.

"One reading" is the intent rather than a rule the data enforces: a glossary-only job mints and publishes a revision without re-reading anything. A more literal name would be one draft-and- publish cycle.

block_identities — the spine. Keyed on (article_id, block_id), and never deleted from. The comment in the schema is blunt about it: "THE SPINE. A block id, once minted, is never deleted from this table."

revision_blocks — the paragraphs as one revision saw them: the text, the HTML, the word count, whether it is a heading or a footnote or sits inside an authored callout, and ordinal, which is document order and is written explicitly from the array index rather than inferred from anything. Block ids are random and carry no position, so if that column is wrong there is nothing left to recover the order from.

It also holds a generated full-text-search column, per paragraph rather than per article, because a search hit is supposed to deep-link to the paragraph. It is generated rather than written by the pipeline, deliberately: a column somebody has to remember to fill is a column that ends up empty for exactly the article that was just re-run, and an empty search vector matches nothing without complaining.

revision_step_runs — one row per step per revision: which step, what its inputs hashed to, which model and prompt version, and whether it finished, is still running, or errored. This is what lets the publication check ask "was the outline actually built from these paragraphs?" rather than assuming it.

Why comments point at an identity rather than at a paragraph row

If a comment pointed at revision_blocks, then the next re-extraction would either have to keep every old paragraph row for ever, or delete the reader's question along with the paragraph. Neither is acceptable — the second obviously, the first because it makes "what does this article say now" a question with several answers.

So the comment's foreign key is on (article_id, block_id) into block_identities. If a re-extraction drops the paragraph, the comment is still a valid row pointing at a real identity; the reading view sorts it to the end rather than dropping it, because it is still the reader's question. It is worth being precise about what that does and does not buy: the comment survives and remains reachable, but a comment also stores the exact quote it was placed on, so the highlight in the prose has nothing left to attach to. Kept and reachable, not still anchored.

The same key is composite for a second reason worth knowing: block ids are unique only within an article. A global unique index would start rejecting perfectly valid rows at roughly a hundred articles, and a global upsert would silently overwrite one article's paragraph with another's. Nobody may "tidy" these into single-column keys.

And the foreign key from revision_blocks to block_identities has neither cascade nor restrict, which is unusual enough to have been written down: identities are never deleted, so that key can only ever fire on an id that was never minted at all. Do not read it as a guard against re-minting, though — the writer adds any newly minted identity before inserting the blocks that use it, so an extraction that decided to give a paragraph a fresh id passes this key cleanly. The key catches a corrupt write, not a bad judgment.

Pass 3 One article, three journeys

The same article, now followed through the three things that can happen to it.

Journey A — the first ingest

  1. A job is created with the default step list. It calls beginRevision, which locks the articles row — creating it first if this slug has never been seen, so a brand-new URL and a re-run take the same code path.
  2. There is nothing published yet, so there is nothing to copy. The draft is minted with based_on_revision_id = null, which means copied from nothing.
  3. Each step runs and writes its product into the draft. blocks writes 289 rows into revision_blocks and mints 289 rows in block_identities. hierarchy writes the tree. glossary writes its column.
  4. The last step's transaction publishes the draft and finishes the job together. The pointer moves. The article appears on the shelf.

Journey B — the reader asks for a glossary six weeks later

This is the journey the whole design is for.

  1. A job of { steps: ["glossary"] } calls beginRevision. Revision r1 is current, so the new draft r2 gets based_on_revision_id = r1, and copies: 36 of the 46 columns, all 289 revision_blocks rows, and every revision_step_runs row.
  2. The glossary step runs. It reads the blocks and the tree — which are there, in its own draft, precisely because the copy happened at the beginning rather than at the end. It writes the glossary column.
  3. At publish, five columns are derived fresh from the blocks and tree: word_count, block_count, part_count, section_count, root_gist.
  4. based_on_revision_id is still r1 and the article is still serving r1, so the check passes and the pointer moves to r2.

The reader now has a glossary. Their comments, their archive state, their own title for the piece — untouched, because none of that was ever on the revision.

Why the copy happens at the beginning, not at the end

The obvious design is to leave the new revision's columns empty and fill in the gaps from the old one at publish time. It fails on the first line: steps read each other. The glossary reads the blocks and the tree. Fill the gaps at the end, and a glossary-only job spends its whole life looking at a revision with no paragraphs in it.

There is a second reason that only shows up later. Filling gaps at publish time cannot tell "no step produced a glossary" from "the reader deleted the glossary". Copying whatever is actually there, null included, means the question never comes up. (Worth knowing: on Postgres, deleting a glossary currently does not work — the glossary panel's "start over" is refused, because it would mutate a revision that has already been published and nobody has decided yet whether that is allowed. See open questions.)

Journey C — the publisher edits the piece, and we re-extract

Now the article's text has genuinely changed. Stage 2 finds new paragraphs; some old ones are gone.

Re-extraction throws away the old markup entirely — the extractor re-parses the page and emits a fresh document with none of our ids in it — so ids are re-assigned by matching the new paragraphs against the previous revision's by their text. That is why the copy at the beginning matters twice over: the previous paragraphs are sitting in the draft, ready to match against, before the step runs.

  • A paragraph whose words are unchanged keeps its id, however far it has moved. Each old id is consumed once, so several identical short paragraphs cannot be handed the same one.
  • A materially edited paragraph gets a new id and loses whatever was anchored to it. This is a deliberate choice, not a gap: fuzzy-matching a rewritten paragraph would silently reattach a reader's note to a sentence that no longer says what they annotated. Losing the anchor is the safer failure — but it is a loss, and it is the honest limit of the seat-number model.
  • Old ids are never deleted from block_identities, so a comment on a paragraph that has gone is still a valid row and still reachable in the comments list.
  • The outline has to be rebuilt too. If the paragraphs actually changed and the job did not also run hierarchy, publication is refused — see the next section.

The measured cost, for the case that is worst

Re-running extraction over the same PDF with the same model and the same prompt keeps 32 of 43 ids and mints 11 — because a model asked twice produces one more record than last time and a comma in a different place, where a web page re-parsed twice produces the same paragraphs. That is a real product cost and it wants deciding before anything a reader owns is anchored to an id.

The carry policy — 46 columns, three fates

The design note worth taking away is that carry-by-default is the dangerous default, not the safe one. An early version built the carry set as "everything except these two short lists". A review pushed back, and it was right: a future column recording when we certified this revision, or which worker owned it, would be actively harmful copied — new content inheriting an old certification.

The sharpest case turned up later and is real rather than hypothetical. based_on_revision_id arrived in migration 0047. If it had been classified as carry, every draft would have inherited its grandparent as its base, and the publication guard would have compared the wrong pair — waving through exactly the race it exists to refuse, for ever.

The three ways this map is held together, and the gap between them

Compile time. The Record is keyed on keyof typeof articleRevisions.$inferSelect. A column added in TypeScript fails npm run typecheck until classified. This is the one that does the work.

A runtime throw — which is weaker than its own comment says. There is a function that throws if a column has no policy, and its comment claims it catches a column added by a hand-written SQL migration, which TypeScript cannot see. It does not: it asks the same TypeScript declaration the typecheck already read, so it can only ever restate what the compiler said, and it runs when a draft begins rather than at import. Worth knowing as an instance of the class this repo keeps writing postmortems about — a comment describing a guard the code does not implement — and the reason it is not dangerous here is the third mechanism.

A test that talks to the database. tests/store-revision-policy.test.ts compares the map against the live table's columns, which is the only one of the three that can see a SQL-only migration. It needs a database, and it skips without one.

And the gap. The block columns are a different story: CARRIED_BLOCK_COLUMNS is a list of 16 plain strings, and nothing typechecks them. A column left out is silently dropped from every re-extraction draft — which for role/treatment/note_id would mean a re-extracted article quietly putting its bibliography back into the argument, and for the callout columns would mean an authored box re-reading as ordinary prose. tests/store-carried-columns.test.ts asserts the list against the table, which is the check that used to be a comment asking a reader to do it by eye.

The four refusals

A draft does not become the article just because the job finished. These are the four checks on whether the draft is fit to be an article, all answered as a 409 Conflict rather than a 500, because a draft that is not fit to publish is not a server fault.

They are not the only way a publication can be turned down — a draft that has already been published, or belongs to another article, or is being pushed by a worker whose claim has lapsed, is each refused on its own. What is worth knowing about these four is that the tree ones are collected: a draft with three things wrong says all three, because "the tree does not cover paragraph 41" and "the tree was built from different paragraphs" are one fix, and reporting them one run at a time teaches whoever is fixing it that the check is unreliable.

#The refusalWhat it is protecting
1 No paragraphs, or no outline Neither is a readable article. The same bar the filesystem reader set by requiring both files.
2 An outline that does not describe these paragraphs Not merely "every id in the outline exists" — that only proves the outline's ids are a subset, and two ordinary mistakes pass it. It is the full structural check: root extent, singleton leaves, exact coverage, order, child partitioning.
3 An outline built from different paragraphs The hierarchy step's recorded input hash must equal the hash of this draft's paragraphs. Without it, a text-only re-extraction that keeps every id publishes the old summaries with no stale banner anywhere — an outline that is structurally perfect and describes an article nobody can read any more. A missing hierarchy row is refused too, because "I cannot tell" is not "it is fine".
4 A draft whose base has moved Something else published while this draft was being written. Publishing now would discard it, silently. See below.

Refusal 3 is a behaviour change worth knowing about

A job of { steps: ["blocks"] } on its own fails whenever it actually changed anything — which is the case anybody cares about. (Re-extract a page that has not moved, get identical paragraphs, and the hash still matches, so it publishes.) That is deliberate: without the check, re-extracting the text while keeping the old outline succeeds and quietly diverges. The fix for anyone who hits it is to run hierarchy as well, which the default step list and the cascade rules already do.

Why the base is a recorded column rather than a question asked at publish time

The tempting alternative is to ask the article what it is currently serving. That answers with whatever landed in the meantime — which is exactly the value the guard is meant to catch. A job runs one step per HTTP request and reopens its draft on the next claim, so a draft can be minutes old by the time it publishes. The base has to be written on the row when the draft is minted, or it is not recoverable at all.

null means two things and both are handled the same way: this is the article's first draft (fine, if the article is serving nothing) and this draft was minted before the column existed (refused). Fail-closed, at the cost of re-running whatever was in flight during that one deploy.

There is deliberately no opt-out flag, and that is a decision rather than an omission. The guard used to live in one caller — the pipeline's own session — and standalone publication walked straight past it. A guard that lives in one caller is a guard the next caller forgets. A field to skip it would be the same hole with a name on it: the caller who most needs the check is the one who has not thought about it, and a flag is what a passing agent reaches for when a fixture goes red.

Pass 4 The genuinely hard parts

The lock order, and a lock that locks nothing

Two locks are involved — the articles row and the jobs row — and the rule is article before job, everywhere. Being inside one transaction does not stop two transactions taking them in opposite orders and deadlocking, so the publication primitive takes the article lock itself whether or not the caller already holds it. Re-locking a row the same transaction already has is free.

The subtler trap is written up separately: SELECT … FOR UPDATE takes a lock on the rows the statement returns, so a FOR UPDATE that matches nothing locks nothing. "Lock the row, and insert it if it wasn't there" therefore protects nothing at all in the one case it was written for, and two concurrent callers both insert. The pattern that works is INSERT … ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING targeted at the key, then read the row back and work from what came back rather than from what you tried to write — which is what lockOrCreateArticle does. It only works at read committed, and every transaction in the store now names its isolation level out loud rather than inheriting whatever the database role happens to give it.

The job fence — a stale worker must not publish

A job's claim has a lease. When the lease lapses, the queue may hand the job to somebody else — so the original worker, which is still running and does not know, must be stopped from publishing what it built.

The fence is one UPDATE whose WHERE carries the whole condition at once: this job, this attempt token, status still running, and a lease that has not lapsed. Checking the token and then writing separately would recreate exactly the race the token exists to prevent. It is checked for exactly one row — never "at least one", never ignored — before anything a reader can see changes, and a zero-row result reaches the caller as a thrown error. A quiet return there would mean the next line reports a publication that did not happen.

A real incident: 2026-09-02

Worth reading because it shows exactly how far the lineage guard reaches, and where it stops.

On a shared development database, a reader watched a quotes job publish — "4 quotes" appeared in the panel. A timeline job then ran, published, and the quotes were gone. No error anywhere. Separately, an ideas job made a real 50-second model call, succeeded, logged lost the claim mid-step, and then sat there.

The cause of both was a second writer — a peer's checkout or a hand-run script on the same local database — deleting the article's revisions and republishing. The server's own log gives it away: revisions became current that this process never published, and one draft opened with basedOn null, blocksCopied 0 against an article that had been on the shelf for three days, which can only mean the article had no revisions at all at that instant.

Now the part that matters, and it is not the comfortable reading:

  • The lineage guard did not fire, and was right not to. The foreign revision became current 42 seconds before the timeline job opened its draft — so the draft recorded that revision as its base, the base and the pointer agreed at publish time, and the publication was correct by every rule we have. The quotes were lost by the second writer; the timeline job simply carried on from the article as it found it.
  • So know what the guard actually covers. It refuses a publication that would bury one landing during your draft. It cannot do anything about the state of the world when your draft opens — a draft copied from a damaged article publishes a faithful copy of the damage.
  • The real defect was the recovery. One error class was being raised for two different events — "somebody else owns this job" (walk away) and "nobody else owns it, but the draft was pulled out from under you" (do not walk away) — and the caller believed only the first reading. The job sat running for 12 minutes 40 seconds, a queued job behind it could not start, and Stop was accepted with a 200 and did nothing until the lease lapsed. Split into two error types the same day.

The database can pull a pointer out from under running code

jobs.draft_revision_id is on delete set null. Delete the draft revision — a fixture reset, a hand-run script, a future "start this article again" button — and Postgres silently removes the pointer from the job row, with nothing in application code involved. This is the shape of thing that is invisible in a code review of either side.

Two more places the revision boundary matters, and one where it must not

Checkpoints must not be keyed on the revision. A long PDF is transcribed in chunks, and the finished chunks are kept so a retry does not pay for them twice. But a retry is a new job, and a new job begins a new draft revision — so a checkpoint keyed on the revision would be written on every run and read on none. Nothing would error; the bill would just go up. The key is the article and the question.

The rollback export must cover every table. A table the exporter has never heard of behaves exactly like one it has nothing to say about — which is how a table added on 2026-08-31 was dropped from every export for a day, with each run reporting success and listing the files it did write. Nothing could have gone red: a file that is never written is a file the round-trip test never misses. The coverage record now takes its table list from the schema and follows foreign keys, not just article_id columns, so a child table keyed only on revision_id is in scope for the same reason its parent is.

The limit that remains: that guard sees a missing table, not a missing column. A new column on an already-exported table is still a hand-written line, and four of them have gone missing that way.

How the schema is meant to be shaped

Four house rules, all of them Greg's, all of them worth understanding before proposing a change that touches storage.

Get the database to do the work

Constraints, defaults, foreign keys and types are rules that hold for every writer — including the script somebody runs once at midnight and the migration that backfills a column three months from now. A rule enforced in application code holds only for the callers that went through that code. The pattern to copy is the CHECK listing the pipeline's 13 step names: it has caught three separate omissions, each time at the insert rather than in a job dying somewhere far away.

A nullable timestamp says more than a boolean

Where a flag is really "off, or on since some moment", store the moment. null is off, a date is on, and the same storage carries strictly more truth — for free, with no second column to disagree with the first. archived_at and public_at are both this shape. The one subtlety: re-asserting it must not move it, or the column quietly comes to mean "when did the client last send true".

Columns, not JSON — with an exception that has to argue for itself

A field you filter, sort, join or constrain on is a column. JSON is for a value the database has no business reading, and a revision has a few of those on purpose: the glossary, the ideas, the diagram, the outline tree. Each is an artefact that only means anything against the article it was written for. The rule is not "never JSON"; it is that a JSON column needs a sentence saying why it is not a column. Wanting an index on something inside the blob is the sign that sentence has stopped being true.

Migrating data inside a JSON column, and the operator that lies

Postgres's containment operator asks about a whole element, not a value inside one. A migration that renamed a pipeline step reached for the obvious WHERE steps @> '["toc"]' — and the array holds objects, so the answer is always no. The UPDATE succeeded, touched nothing, and the deploy carried on. Both predicates against the same table at the same instant: the wrong one matched 0 rows, the right one matched 3.

Two things to take from it. Run the SELECT half first and count the rows — a data migration that matched nothing looks exactly like one that worked. And check what else was derived from the blob: the job de-duplication key is a hash of those same step names, so rewriting the steps without recomputing it makes one request look like two.

Two words that must never be confused

article_revisions.status = 'published' means the pipeline finished this revision. articles.visibility = 'public' means a stranger may read this. They are two tables apart and the sharing column deliberately never uses the word "published", because two meanings of one word is how a mistake gets made at three in the morning.

Trade-offs, risks and open questions

Trade-offs somebody could reasonably decide differently

  • The copy is a real copy. A 289-block article means 289 rows duplicated for every draft, even one that only wants a glossary. A ~360-block article is roughly 1.3 MiB of copied payload. The alternative — a revision that stores only what changed, and inherits the rest by reference — is far more efficient and much harder to reason about, and would reintroduce the "nobody produced it" versus "the reader deleted it" ambiguity that copy-at-the-beginning removes. For an alpha with one reader, the boring answer wins. It is worth revisiting if storage or write latency ever shows up in a measurement rather than in an argument.
  • A retry re-runs completed steps. An earlier design carried forward from "this job's own previous draft" so attempt 2 would skip attempt 1's finished work. It is not built, because a retry is a new job with a new id, so that phrase resolves to nothing — and "the latest draft for this slug" would pick up another job's draft and resurrect precisely what copying-from-published exists to prevent. Paying for a few model calls on a retry is safer and far simpler. It is a real cost, and it is the honest place to look if retries get expensive.
  • Superseded revisions are kept and never demoted. The sweep clears only abandoned drafts and failed revisions; a revision that was published and then replaced stays exactly as it was, and nothing marks it as replaced — so several rows of one article can carry status = 'published' at once, and only the article's pointer says which is live. It is internally consistent, and it is a surprise waiting for whoever next writes a query that filters on the status alone. (They do go when the article does: deleting an article cascades through its revisions, its paragraph ids and the reader's comments.)
  • Deleting a user is currently blocked by their own library. Every owner key refuses the delete while any row is owned — inherited rather than chosen. Defensible for a reading library, but it means account deletion has to be an export-and-delete workflow somebody builds, not a button in a dashboard.

Risks — what a reader would actually experience

  • A note quietly stops pointing at anything. Rewrite a paragraph enough and it gets a new id, so the note is kept in the list but the highlight is gone from the page. Worst on PDFs, where re-reading the same file with the same model loses roughly a quarter of the ids (measured: 11 of 43). Nothing warns the reader. This is the single most likely way somebody feels let down by re-extraction.
  • An artefact that no longer describes the article. Carried glossaries, quotes, timelines and diagrams are shown with a stale label rather than hidden — a deliberate bargain, so the panel is not empty for two minutes and $0.20. It relies on the staleness fingerprint being right; where it is not, the reader is shown a confident interpretation of an article that has moved.
  • A backup that looks complete and is not. The rollback export is the only recovery there is, and it is assembled from a hand-written list. It has silently dropped a whole table for a day. It now takes its table list from the schema — but it still cannot see a column nobody remembered to export, and four have gone missing that way.
  • A fact about every paragraph vanishing in a re-extraction. The revision columns are held by the compiler; the 16 paragraph-level ones are a list of plain strings held only by a test. Miss one and a re-extracted article quietly puts its bibliography back into the main argument, or reads an author's callout box as ordinary prose.
  • A rule that only exists in a comment. Twice now something has been written down as though enforced when it is not: the requirement that a revision claiming a successful fetch must carry a reference to the source document, and the runtime column check described above. Both are the same failure — a reader of the code stops looking because the comment said the guard was there.
  • Shared-database noise looks like a bug. Development runs against one local Postgres shared by several agents and worktrees, so a refused publication on a dev box is often somebody else's script rather than the code under review.

Open questions

  • May a published revision be changed at all? Undecided, and it is already costing something visible: the glossary panel's "start over" is refused under Postgres because nobody has ruled on it. Every future "edit this artefact" feature waits behind the same question.
  • Should a reader ever see an old revision? Everything needed is stored — the rows are all there, keyed and dated. Nothing exposes them. "What changed when this was re-read?" is answerable and unanswered, and it is also the honest answer to a lost anchor.
  • What is the retention policy? There isn't one for published revisions. Nobody has been hurt by that yet; nobody has measured it either.
  • Renaming a slug. Every new article's URL ends in a permanent random id, put there specifically so a rename could redirect through it. The lookup is written; the rename is not.

FAQ

If a re-extraction deletes a paragraph, what happens to my comment on it?

The comment survives and stays in your list — it points at the paragraph's permanent id, and ids are never deleted. The reading view sorts such a comment to the end rather than dropping it, because it is still the reader's question. What it loses is the highlight in the prose: there is nothing left on the page to attach to. The same thing happens if the paragraph was rewritten rather than deleted, because a materially edited paragraph is given a new id.

Can I see the previous version of an article?

Not today. The rows are kept — nothing deletes a superseded revision — but nothing in the product serves them. It would be a feature, not a migration.

Is this basically git for articles?

It has lineage and it refuses conflicting writes, so the comparison is fair as far as it goes. What it does not have is merging, branching, diffs, or any way to look at history. A revision is a snapshot, and only the newest one is ever read.

If I ask for one more thing — say, a timeline — does it re-run the whole article?

No. The draft starts as a copy of what is published, so only the timeline step runs. Everything else is carried across untouched, and the five derived counts are recomputed at the end.

Does re-extracting an article lose my archive state, my own title, or my sharing setting?

No. All of that is on the articles row, not on the revision, deliberately and for exactly this reason.

What happens if a job dies halfway?

Nothing is published, so readers see the article they already had. The abandoned draft is swept later — only drafts and failed revisions, only ones no job still points at and no article is serving, only ones past the cutoff.

Why does a carried glossary not claim to be current?

Every artefact carries a fingerprint of what it was made from, compared against the paragraphs and outline at read time. So a carried glossary on a re-extracted article is shown, and labelled stale, until its step re-runs. Carrying it is not a claim that it is current; it is the choice to leave the reader something to look at rather than an empty panel.

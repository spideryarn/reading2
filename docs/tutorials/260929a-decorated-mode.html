Decorated mode

Show the reader every word the author wrote, in order and unchanged, and decorate it so the argument is easier to follow. Here is the research behind the idea, the rules it keeps, the playground that tries twenty-four decorations on a real essay, and the decisions still open.

10 minutes to skim · ~40 to read everything · written 2026-09-29, against dev at 6485d643 · the page itself: experiments/decorated/decorated.html

TL;DR

On 2026-08-28 Greg asked for a mode that shows the actual article, decorated: important sentences made more prominent and less important ones quieter, headings added where the author left none, small diagrams, glossary underlines, comments. The one hard rule is that no word of the author's is removed, reordered or rewritten.

Five independent research lists produced 168 ideas, and a sixth pass looked at the data we already had. The lists agreed on four ideas and split sharply on three questions: making text bigger or fainter, a label beside every paragraph, and letting the model pick the key sentences. The first and third pull against parts of Greg's brief. The playground puts 24 switches on one self-contained HTML page (22 from the original ideas, the rest added later). The page renders Anil Seth's essay The Mythology Of Conscious AI from the real pipeline data, with a script that proves no word was changed.

It is a playground, not a product. Nothing from it has been built into the app. The three questions it was built to settle have not been answered yet, and the worked example below shows what goes wrong if they are settled carelessly.

Four ways to picture it

1 · A critical edition, or a Talmud page

The text sits in the middle, untouched. Commentary goes around it: glosses in the margin, rubrics above sections, notes beside hard words. Scholars have read this way for centuries, and nobody reading such an edition mistakes the commentary for the text, because each has its own place on the page and its own typeface. Decorated mode is that layout, with the commentary written by a model.

2 · Road markings

A dashed line, a solid line and a zig-zag each mean one thing, and a driver can read them at speed because no marking is ever reused to mean something else. The playground treats typography the same way. Weight, faintness, underline style, the margin and colour are each a channel, and each channel has one owner. If the dotted underline means "glossary term", it never also means "assumption".

3 · An answer key, not a crib sheet

A summary handed over before you read lets you skip the reading. The same summary shown after you've read lets you check what you took in. The research kept coming back to this: identical decorations help or harm depending on whether they arrive before or after the reading. Two ideas are built on it: "Earn the ink" and "The section gate".

4 · A mixing desk

The playground has a panel of twenty-four switches, one per decoration, plus four presets. You are meant to turn things off and read what is left. Its README says "the ones you don't miss should not exist". It is a tool for making decisions, not a design.

The most valuable ideas

Of the 168 ideas, these nine are the ones I'd carry forward, whatever happens to the rest. The ranking is this tutorial's synthesis, not a list any one source made, though the top two follow the reading-science pass's own ranking. They're ranked by how much each does for the reader, how much agreement and evidence sit behind it, and whether it keeps the "not a way of not reading" rule. Orange cards are on the playground; grey ones aren't built yet. The built ones carry a ★ in the appendix table.

1Earn the ink (read-first)

Nothing we wrote about a passage can be read before the passage itself, so our summaries, labels and questions become a check on your reading rather than a replacement for it. (The playground does this per paragraph, for the gutter, the margin and the inline marks. Our headings, section summaries and the arc rail aren't held back yet.)

Two passes reached it independently, and the catalogue says it "de-risks almost everything else". The one idea that makes the others safe. Decision 5.

2The section gate

At the end of a section you say what you took from it, and only then does our summary open.

Recall followed by feedback is the best-evidenced cheap technique the reading-science pass found, and it was that pass's top pick. It turns a summary into an answer key.

3Whose voice is this

Mark where the essay is stating a view it's about to reject.

It prevents the error that reverses an argument's meaning, and the worked example shows our own skim path making that error by the first few paragraphs. First on the list of things to build next. It has to be conservative: a wrong "not the author's view" tint would create the very misreading it exists to prevent.

4The assumptions

The propositions the argument needs you to accept, marked under the exact words, with why the argument needs each one.

Four passes proposed it. It's interrogation made visible, and it's the most distinctively Spideryarn data we have.

5The arc rail

One sticky sentence saying where you are in the argument, not what this section says.

Four passes proposed it. It keeps track of the thing that fades fastest in an hour-long essay, and the data already existed unused.

6Our headings, visibly ours

Headings for the stretches the author left unheaded, set in a different voice from the author's.

The only idea all five passes proposed. This essay has four headings of its own in 8,300 words.

7Three territories and the channel budget

Author in the centre, us on the left, the reader on the right, with each visual property owned by one kind of mark.

It isn't a decoration. It's the rule that lets a dozen decorations share a page without becoming a ransom note, and the reason the page can be read at all. The picture.

8Relation words in the gutter

so, but, why, e.g.: one word per paragraph saying how it relates to the one before.

Three passes proposed it. It shows structure without adding content, the family the research calls safe, and costs one word of width.

9The referent lamp

Hover an ambiguous this or it, and what it refers to lights up where it stands.

"The best cheap idea not built." Backed by evidence, it needs one small pass, and it repairs the failure that eats the most attention per word.

Just outside the nine: highlight-then-compare (you mark what matters, then see what we marked). It's third on the catalogue's build-next list, and it's the only version of key-sentence marking the research considers safe. It sits outside the nine only because it needs interaction the app doesn't have yet. Worth seeing once, not ranked: the thesis as verse (the three sentences the essay exists to deliver, broken into lines at the author's own punctuation), which the catalogue calls "the most audacious thing available inside the rule". The one to be wary of: the skim path. It's the most requested and the most contested, for the reasons in Decision 3.

Pass 1The whole story in five steps

  1. The brief. Greg dictated the idea, and it was written up with one hard rule (no word of the author's removed, reordered or rewritten) and one soft rule (a decoration must not become a way of not reading).
  2. Six research passes. The brief went to six independent passes, none of which saw the others' answers. Each was given a different stance: typography as literature, editorial design, the cognitive science of reading, what browsers can now do, a cross-family model with no stance, and the session that knew our data.
  3. A catalogue. The 168 ideas were grouped and ranked. More useful than the list itself were the places where the passes agreed and the places where they disagreed.
  4. A playground. 24 switches, driven by the real pipeline data for one essay and one extra annotation file, plus a script that checks the article survived intact.
  5. Greg's review reshaped it: every mark now explains itself, logic symbols became words, the drop cap went, and emphasis moved from paragraphs down to sentences. The three big questions are still with Greg.

Pass 2The rules everything answers to

The hard rule: every word, in order, unchanged

We may not remove any of the article's words, and we may not change any of its words. Every sentence the author wrote is still on the page, in order, in full. What we may change is how it is displayed, and what we may add around, between, beside and on top of it.

— the brief, 2026-08-28

Allowed: size, weight, colour, faintness, spacing, underlines, marginal notes, inserted panels, inserted headings, inserted diagrams, hover behaviour. Not allowed: cutting a clause, paraphrasing, reordering. The playground enforces this with a script, verify.mjs, which compares every block on the page against the stored text character by character (Pass 4 covers how that script itself went quietly blind twice).

The soft rule, which decides more: not a way of not reading

This is the product's vision turned into a rule for the interface. Greg, 2026-08-24: "help them read efficiently, but deeply, help them internalize and interrogate." A decoration that lets the reader consume our summary instead of the prose breaks the rule. One that makes the prose easier to enter, follow and question keeps it. According to the catalogue, this rule knocked out more ideas than ugliness did.

The two rules can come apart

A decoration can keep every word and still delete it in practice. Text at 40% opacity on a dark page is text most people will not read. That is how the reading-science pass objected to "make less important stuff fainter": the hard rule is satisfied on paper and broken in effect. Decision 1 is about exactly this.

Three voices, three territories

The design pass supplied the organising idea: three parties put marks on the page, and each gets its own territory so a reader can always tell who is speaking.

The channel budget

Twelve decorations can share one page only if each owns a different channel, meaning one visual property. A channel has exactly one owner, and a block may carry at most two non-default channels at once. The playground's stylesheet is organised by channel rather than by feature for this reason.

The line that settles arguments

"Per screen, exactly one element may be at full ink + raised weight + extra space. That is the reader's one unit of attention, and every decoration is competing for it." — the design pass.

Two tests that came out of Greg's review

  • "What does a reader know after this that they did not know before it?" This is the test the drop cap failed. A section opening was already signalled three times, by a rule, a heading and a small-caps first line, so the large initial letter added nothing.
  • "The mark is the answer; the tooltip is the reason." If a mark can't answer its own question, it should be a word. This is why the logic symbols in the gutter (∴ ⊥ ∵) became so, but, why.

What the reading science brought

This was the pass that changed the most, because it's the one that says what doesn't work. Its opening was blunt: the aids people reach for first (pre-highlighting, summaries above the text, speed-reading modes, bolding the start of every word) are mostly the ones with weak or negative evidence. They share one failure: they raise a reader's confidence faster than their understanding. The aids with good evidence make the reader produce something, such as recalling, explaining or predicting.

Three findings run under almost every decision:

  • Going back is useful. About 10–15% of eye movements in normal reading go backwards, and they do repair work: working out what "it" refers to, re-checking a number. A decoration that discourages going back is working against the reader.
  • Filling the gaps can hurt. Readers with enough background learn more from a text whose gaps they bridge themselves. So our job is to show where a gap is, not to fill it.
  • Signalling structure is the safe family. Cues that expose structure without adding content reliably help, and they don't replace the prose.
The reading-science pass's list of decorations that would probably backfire
  • Pre-highlighted key sentences. "The obvious first feature, and the worst." In the standard review of study techniques, highlighting rates as low-value even when learners do it themselves. Done for the reader it is worse, because attention narrows onto the marked text. And our highlights will be model-quality, "which means confidently wrong somewhere in every article."
  • Making unimportant sentences smaller or fainter. Three objections: the reader can't check the model's judgement before reading the sentence; faint amounts to deleted; and mixing type sizes within a line makes the eye land in the wrong places. The counter-proposal is to vary spacing between blocks, by how dense the text is, which is a property of the text, rather than by importance, which is a claim about it.
  • Bionic reading (bolding the first half of every word): no peer-reviewed support, and it uses up the whole emphasis budget on something that says nothing about this text.
  • Speed-reading modes (one word at a time): they remove both the preview of the next word and the ability to go back, and the trade gets worse as the text gets harder.
  • A label beside every paragraph: it creates "a second, complete, faster-to-read article running down the margin".

Where the passes agreed

Four ideas were proposed independently by three or more of the five outside passes. That is the nearest thing to a signal the exercise produced, and all four are on the playground.

  1. Write the headings the author didn't (all five). This essay has four headings of its own in 8,300 words. Every pass added the same condition: ours must be visibly ours.
  2. One sticky sentence saying where you are in the argument, as opposed to what the section says. The pipeline already produced this data (arc.json) and nothing used it.
  3. Mark the turns: a word per paragraph for how it relates to the one before (so, but, why, e.g.). Readers who understand every sentence still lose track of what the sentences are doing together.
  4. Show the assumptions: ideas.json, the propositions the piece needs you to accept, most of them never argued for. At the time nothing used it, and it was called "the most distinctively Spideryarn thing we have". (It has since got its own mode in the app: Ideas.)
What the web-platform pass contributed, and where the playground ignored it

It proposed three rules for any plan:

  1. Paint the author's words, never wrap them. A newer browser feature (the CSS Custom Highlight API) can colour or underline any stretch of text without changing the page's structure, so overlapping marks stop being a problem. But it can only change colour, background, underline and text shadow. It can't change size, weight or spacing, and those are what Greg asked for. The playground wraps text in spans and pays the price. See paint or wrap.
  2. Nothing we add should end up in the clipboard. The playground originally got this wrong: copy three paragraphs and our headings and gutter notes came along. It's fixed, and verify.mjs checks it.
  3. Never hide the author's prose inside a collapsed section, because in most browsers find-in-page can't see collapsed text. The playground never did.

It also put the whole list against a support table showing which browsers ship which feature as of August 2026. Several of the nicer ideas are Chrome-only.

Pass 2The playground: what is on it and how it is made

It is deliberately outside the app. Nothing in it imports app code and the app imports nothing from it, so an idea can be tried without first arguing about how it fits the reading view's URL, its mode band or its layer limits. Most of the ideas will be thrown away, so there was no point paying that integration cost for them.

How the page is made

What annotations.json holds. For 108 of the 141 blocks it records a weight from 0 to 3 (how load-bearing the paragraph is), a role (claim, evidence, concession, rebuttal and so on, sixteen kinds in all), a connective (how this paragraph relates to the one before), the question the paragraph answers, a difficulty, and 212 marked spans: the key sentence, strong phrases, hedges, hinges, skippable asides and numbers that matter. The sentence-by-sentence skim grades are worked out from these at build time rather than asked for separately, so everything traces back to one set of judgements.

The 24 switches, in five groups

Each group is below. The full list, with what each switch does and where it came from, is in the appendix.

  • Structure: our headings, a one-line summary under each, the spine (the whole article as a strip of ticks down the left edge), and small caps on the opening line of each section.
  • The argument: the arc rail, turn markers, the relation word per paragraph, the rhetorical x-ray (hold x), and the assumptions.
  • The sentences: the skim path (hold s), salience relief, …and by size too contested, fade the skippable contested, hedges, numbers, and the thesis as verse.
  • The terms, and you: glossary underlines, definitions in the margin, a line per paragraph contested, the question each paragraph answers, your own marks, and the section gate.
  • The strange ones: Earn the ink (decorations appear only after you've spent long enough beside a paragraph to have read it) and argument gravity.

Four presets: all on, bare prose, the restrained set (ten switches), and everything Greg asked for (twenty-two). The panel opens with a legend, and every sample in it is drawn with the page's real styles, because a reader who doesn't know a mark exists will never hover over it.

Pass 3One paragraph, every layer

This is an early paragraph of Seth's essay, spya-epw4h3, the fifth of the body text. The one before it says Google engineer Blake Lemoine claimed the chatbot LaMDA was conscious, and that Google fired him. This is the author's text, complete and unedited:

But the question he raised has not gone away. Firing someone for breaching confidentiality is not the same as firing them for being wrong. As AI technologies continue to improve, questions about machine consciousness are increasingly being raised. David Chalmers, one of the foremost thinkers in this area, has suggested that conscious machines may be possible in the not-too-distant future. Geoffrey Hinton, a true AI pioneer and recent Nobel Prize winner, thinks they exist already. In late 2024, a group of prominent researchers wrote a widely publicized article about the need to take the welfare of AI systems seriously. For many leading experts in AI and neuroscience, the emergence of machine consciousness is a question of when, not if.

What we already knew about it, before decorating

  • Its place in the tree (tree.json). It is the last block of a span with no author heading, for which we wrote one: "Fantasies Of Artificial Minds". That span sits inside "Framing The Question", which is also ours.
  • Its label (labels.json): "Chalmers, Hinton and others treat machine consciousness as a when-not-if question."
  • The arc (arc.json) for the first fourteen blocks: "Machine consciousness is a mythology still untested against evidence, yet the stakes of believing it … are laid out before any argument against it begins."
  • The reader's own marks (comments.json): two answered questions, one on Chalmers and one on Hinton, and a bookmark.

What the judgement pass added

  • weight 2 of 3, role: rebuttal, connective: but
  • the question it answers: "Wasn't Lemoine just a crank Google fired?"
  • spans: "Firing someone … for being wrong." is strong; the Chalmers clause is a hedge; and the last sentence, "For many leading experts … when, not if", is the paragraph's key sentence.

A close copy of the playground's output, built from its real sentence grades: bright is "carries the argument" (grade 2), mid-grey "supports it" (1), dimmest "can be skipped" (0, which still stays readable). Most of this works as designed. The but tells you this paragraph pushes back on the last one. The question in the gutter tells you which objection it pushes back on. The hedge mark stops you reading "may be possible" as "is". Your own marks are orange and nothing else is.

Now hold s and read only the bright sentence

The skim path says the one sentence carrying this paragraph is: "For many leading experts in AI and neuroscience, the emergence of machine consciousness is a question of when, not if."

That is other people's position, and the essay spends most of its length challenging it, especially the idea that consciousness in today's silicon, digital AI is inevitable. (Seth doesn't rule artificial consciousness out altogether. He ends by arguing against overconfidence both ways, calling it neither inevitable nor impossible.) A reader skimming the bright path takes away a view close to the reverse of the article's, and it looks authoritative, because it's the one sentence we made brighter.

The judgement pass wasn't wrong: it is the sentence that carries this paragraph. What is missing is a second fact, that this is somebody else's view, stated in order to be refused. The reading-science pass warned about exactly this failure ("mistaking the steelman for the thesis"), and its fix, "Whose voice is this", is first on the list of things to build next. Its absence shows up within the first few paragraphs.

Two smaller things show up in the same paragraph:

  • The hedge mark is in slightly the wrong place. Build-time narrowing looks for the first hedge word, finds "suggested", and keeps the two words after it. That gives "suggested that conscious" and leaves the stronger hedge, "may be possible", outside the wavy line. See why hedges are narrowed.
  • The whole Hinton sentence is marked skippable, not just his credentials. Nobody judged it skippable directly: the rule treats any later, unmarked sentence in a weight-2 paragraph as elaboration. But the authority of the people Seth disagrees with is part of his point, because the essay argues against eminent people. Deciding what is skippable is itself a claim about the argument, and here a blanket rule made it.

With Earn the ink on, none of the gutter text would appear until you had been beside this paragraph long enough to read it. Then the question and the relation word arrive as a check on what you just read, not as a preview of it. That is the read-first case in one paragraph.

Pass 4The parts that were actually hard

None of these is needed to follow the decisions. They're here because each one would come back if the playground were rebuilt inside the app.

How long a mark is decides what kind of mark it is

The judgement pass was asked to mark hedges ("may be", "arguably") and numbers with "enough words around it to read as a fact". What came back were whole clauses: hedges up to 194 characters long, and "won the 2025 annual Berggruen Prize Essay Competition" counted as a number.

A wavy underline over four words marks a hedge. The same underline over a 194-character clause is a highlighted sentence, which is the thing the research argued hardest against, arriving through a layer designed to avoid it. "It built itself out of a vague word in a prompt."

Now the build narrows hedge and number marks to the marker word plus two words, caps them at 34 characters, and prints how many it moved (33 of 36). The lesson for any future decoration pass is to state the length in the schema. The first version of the narrowing also quietly dropped nine marks its word list didn't recognise, and "a mark we cannot place is worse than no mark, because it looks placed." The worked example shows that narrowing can still land slightly off.

The check that proves nothing changed went blind, twice

verify.mjs proves its own worth with a control: it deletes four words from a copy of the page and confirms the comparison goes red. Once tooltips added English prose inside HTML attributes, the control picked four words from a tooltip, deleted them, and the comparison stayed green. Deleting words from a tooltip changes nothing in the article. The control now takes its words from the prose itself.

The tooltip check had the opposite fault. It passed six marks that had no tooltip, because it was reading the legend's text out of the page's own inlined script. Both faults were found only because each check is run against a deliberately broken copy. Re-run on 2026-09-29, all four checks pass and all three controls go red when they should.

Overlapping marks, and why the text is chopped into pieces

One phrase can be a glossary term, sit inside a comment, and be part of the key sentence all at once. HTML elements have to nest, and two marks that merely overlap have no valid nesting. The playground's answer, which the app's own highlighter also uses (src/web/annotate.ts), is to cut the text at every boundary of every mark and label each piece with whichever marks cover it. Nothing nests, so nothing can fail to nest. The cost is the heavy markup in the worked example: one short paragraph becomes about twenty pieces. See paint or wrap for the alternative.

A glossary card that opened by accident and wouldn't close

Brushing past an underlined term while scrolling opened its card, and nothing closed it. The browser feature used for the card closes it on an outside click, but nothing told the reader to click. The fix was to separate two cases: a card opened by hovering closes when the pointer leaves; a card opened by clicking stays until it is dismissed. The fix itself had a bug. A hover timer is always already running by the time you click, and 420 ms later it un-pinned the card, so the pin looked set at the moment of the click and came undone half a second later. It was found by a browser agent watching the state rather than the screen.

Bugs it found in data the live app uses

The build reports every quote it can't place instead of skipping it, which is how it found these. Three of the ten assumption quotes, and one comment quote, don't match the article exactly, because the model retyped a curly apostrophe as a straight one or there's a non-breaking space. Two of the nineteen glossary terms match nothing because the article uses a plural ("Turing machines"). Anything in the app that anchors by exact quote would silently drop these. The catalogue says neither has been confirmed against the app yet. Re-run today, the build still reports six quote problems for this article.

"Earn the ink" measures time, not reading

It's a timer scaled to the length of the block. It can't tell reading from staring out of the window, and the catalogue says so: "It does not have to — the point is only ordering." The reading-science pass's version was stricter: it blurred every piece of text we generate until the reader had scrolled past the end of the span it describes. The playground holds back only each paragraph's own gutter, margin, role and inline marks. Our headings, their one-line summaries, the arc rail, the turn markers and the section gates can all still be read ahead of the prose they describe.

Decisions waiting on Greg

The playground exists so these can be judged with the prose in front of you rather than in the abstract. Each has a switch.

Decision 1 · Should less important text be smaller or fainter?

Greg asked for it twice ("make critical sentences bigger", "make less important stuff smaller or fainter"). The reading-science pass opposed both halves: fainter amounts to deleted, the reader can't check the judgement before reading, and mixed sizes within a line throw the eye off. The designer split it: never change body size, but a shallow ink ladder with a tested contrast floor is "the better half" of Greg's idea. The playground's default layer is that compromise, and the stronger Fade the skippable is tested separately.

Options:

  • A · Space and ink only (the playground's default, Salience relief). Important paragraphs get more air and fuller ink, and nothing drops below a readable floor. It costs little and is less dramatic than what Greg pictured.
  • B · Also size and fading (the switches …and by size too and Fade the skippable, both off by default). This is what Greg first described. It carries the risks above.
  • C · Vary spacing by density, not importance. This was the research's compromise: the property comes from the text itself rather than from a claim about it. It needs a density measure we don't have, and it isn't built.

What would decide it: read a few sections with A, then B. If B makes you skip text you later wish you'd read, that's the objection proving itself.

Decision 2 · Should every paragraph get a line in the margin?

We have a one-line label for all 141 blocks, and printing them is very tempting. Three of the four passes that addressed it said no. It becomes "a second, complete, faster-to-read article running down the margin", which is the definition of a way of not reading. The labels are more useful as search results, hover text and end-of-section feedback. On the playground the switch is contested and off by default, and even when on, labels are faint until you're beside them.

Decision 3 · Should the model pick the key sentences at all?

The reading science calls pre-highlighting "the obvious first feature, and the worst". Greg's second-pass note pulled the other way: "It doesn't do enough to emphasise the sections that are worth reading vs can be skipped (perhaps with spans rather than at block level)." The playground followed Greg and now grades every sentence 0 / 1 / 2. That skim path is part of the restrained preset, where it stays on the whole time rather than only while a key is held.

That means this disagreement has been partly settled by building it rather than by deciding it. The worked example is the case against it as it stands. The mitigations available:

  • Only while held (the s key): you ask it a question rather than reading inside it. It's already built as an alternative.
  • Only after reading (read-first).
  • Highlight, then compare: the reader marks what they think matters first, and ours appears afterwards as a comparison. The research ranked this as the only version that keeps the benefit without the harm. Not built.
  • Whose voice is this: a sentence stating someone else's view is never shown as carrying the argument without saying whose view it is. Not built.

Decision 4 · A mode, or a dial?

The panel of twenty-four switches exists because this is a playground. The design pass argued that the product should have one control with four positions, from bare to loud, rather than a panel of toggles. A dial is simpler for readers, and it forces us to decide which decorations belong at each level, which is the real design work. A panel keeps flexibility, but no reader will learn twenty conventions.

Decision 5 · Should the article decorate itself as you read it?

Two passes arrived at this independently (reading science's Read-first ink and Fable's Second reading), and the catalogue calls it "the strongest structural idea in the whole catalogue". It makes almost every other idea safe, because nothing we wrote about a passage can be read before the passage.

It also changes what the feature is: "from 'a decorated article' to 'an article that decorates itself as you read it'. That is either the best idea in the catalogue or too clever. It is the question the playground exists to answer." The costs are that it feels withholding on a re-read, that it needs to remember what you've read, and that the timer can't tell reading from staring.

Our headings: how visibly ours?

Every pass said generated headings must be distinguishable from the author's. The first build put a "not the author's" badge on each. Greg asked for that to move into a tooltip, so now the typeface alone tells you: ours are sans-serif small caps, the author's four are serif at full ink. It's quieter, but it only works for a reader who has noticed the difference. The web-platform pass's Confidence ink (a hand-drawn stroke that wobbles where we inferred rather than quoted) was another answer to the same problem, and it isn't built.

Words or symbols in the gutter

Symbols are one character wide and cost almost nothing to read, but only once you've learned them. Nobody had learned them, and some were wrong on their own terms: ⊥ means "contradiction", not "but". Words cost a little width and need no learning. Two symbols survived, + and =, "on the grounds that nobody has ever had to be taught either".

Unbidden definitions in the margin vs definitions on hover

A hover card only helps if you already suspect you need it. A gloss in the margin at the word's first use teaches you without being asked, but it costs margin space and attention on every first use. The playground does both: a dotted underline everywhere, as in the reading view, and a margin gloss on first use only.

Risks and fragilities

  • The model's judgements will be confidently wrong somewhere in every article. Every emphasis layer amplifies that. The worked example is one concrete case from block seven.
  • Can the reader tell our voice from the author's? The whole design depends on territory and typeface. If readers don't notice the difference, our headings and questions read as the author's.
  • Crowding. With everything on, the page is loud. That's intended, since it's a playground, but it means no one has yet tested what a sensible default set looks like on a second article.
  • One article, one shape. Everything was tuned on a single argumentative essay that names a bias, refutes a theory and draws a conclusion. A scientific paper, a news piece or a listicle would test the ideas differently. The skim grades' cut-off (33% / 36% / 31%) was chosen for this article.
  • The judgement layer doesn't exist as a pipeline step. annotations.json was made once, for this article. Trying any of this on a second article means writing that pass properly, with the lesson about stating lengths in the schema.
  • The playground can only be rebuilt on Greg's Mac. build.mjs reads the article from data/noema-mythology-of-conscious-ai/, which is left over from the filesystem store that was retired on 2026-09-05. It isn't in git (/data/ is ignored), so a fresh checkout or the remote box can't rebuild the page. The committed decorated.html still opens anywhere.
  • Accessibility is mostly unexamined. The web-platform pass's rule, that every importance cue needs a twin that doesn't rely on colour or size (for high-contrast modes and screen readers), isn't systematically applied.
  • What helps a novice can get in an expert's way. The reading-science pass warns that any always-on aid can become distracting redundancy for a reader who already knows the ground. Every persistent decoration therefore needs a remembered global off switch, and possibly a setting in the reader's profile. The playground's panel doesn't count, because it is a testing tool.
  • Browser support. Several of the nicest ideas (anchored margin notes, scroll-state, hand-drawn strokes) are Chrome-only or missing in Firefox as of August 2026.

Next steps

What the catalogue recommends building next on the playground, in its own order:

  1. Whose voice is this. Mark where the essay is stating a position it will reject. The worked example shows why this comes first. It needs a pass that marks attributed-position spans.
  2. The referent lamp. Hover an ambiguous this, it or such systems, and what it refers to lights up where it stands. It's cheap, backed by evidence, and needs one small pass. The catalogue calls it "the best cheap idea not built".
  3. Highlight-then-compare. The reader marks first, then sees ours.
  4. Skim conscience. When you scroll faster than anyone can read, the decorations withdraw instead of trying harder. It's the other half of Earn the ink.
  5. A structure sketch drawn as you go. A diagram of the argument that gains one node per section as you pass it, so it can never be read ahead of the prose.

Suggestions from writing this tutorial (mine, not yet anyone's plan):

  • Answer the five decisions above, or at least 1, 3 and 5, with the page open. Each has a switch, and each is a few minutes' reading.
  • Try a second article of a different shape before anything moves into the app. That means writing the judgement pass as a real pipeline step with lengths in its schema, and making the build read from the database (or a committed fixture) instead of the leftover data/ folder.
  • Confirm the two data bugs against the app (straight vs curly apostrophes in quotes, and plurals in glossary matching). They affect the live reading view whether or not decorated mode ever ships.
  • If it moves into the app, the natural first slice is probably the four ideas with the strongest agreement across passes (our headings, proposed by all five; the arc rail, relation words and assumptions, by three or four each) plus the glossary underlines the reading view already has. All of them signal structure, which is the family the research calls safe, and none needs a new model pass. Some neighbours already exist: Ideas mode uses ideas.json, Remember mode is the section gate for a whole article, and reading time draws where you've been on the spine.

FAQ

How do I actually use the page?

Open experiments/decorated/decorated.html straight from disk; no server is needed. The panel is at the bottom right. Start with bare prose, then the restrained set, then all on, then turn switches off one at a time. Hold s for the skim path and x for what each paragraph is doing. Hover over anything Spideryarn added and it will say what it is. That's enforced: verify.mjs fails if a mark can't explain itself.

Is any of this in the app?

No. Nothing from the playground is in src/. Some of the same data now has other homes (see next steps).

Isn't this just highlighting with extra steps?

That is the risk the whole research exercise was about. The ideas that survived mostly signal structure (where the turns are, what's assumed, where you are in the argument) rather than telling you which words to read. The one layer that does tell you which words to read, the skim path, is the most contested thing on the page.

Why this particular essay?

It's long (about 8,300 words), argumentative, and nearly unheaded (four headings of the author's own), and it spends long stretches stating views it rejects. That's a hard, realistic test for decorations meant to help follow an argument, and it had real comments and every artefact already made.

Why does it matter that the checks "prove themselves"?

Because a check that has never failed may simply not be checking. Two of this page's checks had quietly stopped working after markup changes, and they were found only because each one runs against a deliberately broken copy and must go red. This is the house rule in silent-success.md.

Where do I read the ideas themselves?

The catalogue, 260828c-decorated-mode-ideas.md, is the best single read. For the originals, it recommends five: Fable #12 (the thesis as verse), Fable #11 (the margin like a Talmud page), reading science #19 (the section gate), the reading science "would probably backfire" list, and the designer's "The system". All are in 260828b-decorated-mode/.

Glossary

Block
One paragraph, heading or figure of the article, with a permanent id such as spya-epw4h3.
Artefact
A file of model output about an article: the tree, labels, arc, glossary, ideas and so on. Each is keyed by block id.
Tree
The nested outline of the article. It is also the table of contents and the zoom structure. Every node has a title, which is ours when the author gave none.
Arc
A handful of sentences, each covering a range of blocks, saying where the argument has got to.
Channel
One visual property (ink, weight, size, space, underline style, colour, motion) owned by exactly one kind of decoration.
Apparatus
Everything we add to the page, as opposed to the author's text. It is never copied with a selection.
Hedge
A qualifier ("may", "arguably"): the author is not asserting this outright.
Hinge
The words where the argument turns.
Control
A deliberately broken copy that a check must reject, to prove the check can fail.
Steelman
The strongest version of the view an author is about to argue against.

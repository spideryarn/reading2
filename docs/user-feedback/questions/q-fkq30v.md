Done: the research, and your P.S. When a reader tells the guide, or writes in Why you're reading this, that they are refereeing, the guide now offers Referee as a button, even with experimental features off. It is never opened for them, and it comes with one sentence: the article's text already went to an AI provider when it was added, and Referee's Notices say what journals' rules are on that. Measured 14 of 14, and a magazine reviewer got no offer. On dev, not deployed.

The line between the two modes, which matches your lean: Sources (the new name for Peer review) asks "where does this piece sit?" and is for anyone. Referee asks "what do I have to judge, and have I looked at it?" and is only for someone who owes a verdict. It never gives one.

Four questions. None blocks what is built.

1. Both modes have a sub-mode called Claims, and they are different. Sources › Claims checks the claims the piece rests on against the web. Referee › Claims sets what the piece promises against the passages inside it meant to deliver it.
A. Leave both as Claims. Costs nothing; a referee with the switch on sees two Claims chips.
B. Rename Referee's to Promises. One word on one chip; it says what the list is.
C. Rename Sources' to Checks.
Recommended: B.

2. Referee › Candidates suggests who could review the piece. That is the journal editor's job, the persona you set aside.
A. Keep it. B. Move it out of Referee into its own experimental mode. C. Retire it.
Recommended: A for now. It costs nothing while it sits behind the switch.

3. Take Referee out of experimental features, so every reader sees it in the bar?
A. Yes. Referees find it without the guide; every reader's bar gets one more button.
B. No, keep it behind the switch; the guide offers it to referees (as built).
Recommended: B, until a referee has used it.

4. Which idea first? From the research, seven are not built. The top four are:
A. Starter criteria from the errors reviewers most often miss (Referee). Cheap, because Criteria exists.
B. "Does the cited work say that?" at the citing sentence (Sources).
C. What the piece does not cite: related work it leaves out (Sources).
D. Your own notes, sorted major and minor by you, taken out as a skeleton in your words (Referee).
Recommended: A.

Details

What you asked, on 9 Oct: peer review is a real opportunity with scientists and academics. Research what someone asked to write a review needs, then design how Spideryarn helps them notice things without writing the review for them. Work out how Peer review (now Sources) and Referee differ. And if a reader says they are a referee, offer Referee's tools.

What the research found. Reviewers given a paper with nine planted major errors found about 2.6 of them (BMJ studies, 607 reviewers). A review takes several hours, and novices struggle most with the background literature and judging novelty. Several major publishers forbid AI to make the assessment, and nearly every policy treats uploading a manuscript to an AI tool as a confidentiality breach. So the opportunity is helping a reviewer notice: second-look aids that point at passages, never a verdict.

The reviewer's job in six steps, against what we have. Saying yes or no: the guide, Structure, Skim. First pass: Skim, Structure, Ideas. Close read: Referee › Criteria and Referee › Claims. The literature around it: Sources (Bibliography, Reception, Claims). Writing comments: Comments and Referee › Mirror. The recommendation: nothing, on purpose. So steps 3 and 5 are Referee's, step 4 is Sources', and step 6 stays the reviewer's.

On question 1. B renames what you see on one chip and the words under it. Referee's Claims is stored as claims, so it is a small rename all the way down. C would rename half of the Sources work that is being finished now.

On question 2. Candidates is also the one Referee call that sees the authors' names, to keep them out of its own suggestions. Moving or retiring it would also remove that exception.

On question 3. With A, the guide's special case for Referee would go, and nothing else changes.

On question 4. The rest are: numbers that disagree with themselves, checked without a model; a figure beside the prose that describes it; and a log of what Spideryarn did, for venues that ask reviewers to declare AI use. A suits the evidence most directly. Offer starters only after the reviewer has written one criterion of their own, so we do not anchor them.

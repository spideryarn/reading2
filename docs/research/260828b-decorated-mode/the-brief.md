# Brief: "Decorated mode" — ideas wanted

## The product

Spideryarn is a reading app. Its whole point is to **augment** reading, not replace it:

> it augments human cognition, but it doesn't replace it … instead of trying to make things too
> easy, trying to replace the words with quick and easy summaries so much, but rather we help the
> user get what they need from it, help them read efficiently, but deeply, help them internalize
> and interrogate.
> — Greg (the product owner), 2026-08-24

You paste in a URL; a pipeline fetches the article, splits it into **blocks** (paragraphs, headings,
figures), gives every block a **stable id** (`spya-k3m9qt`), and then a set of model passes produce
artefacts *about* the article. The reading view is dark: near-black page, off-white text, a
Spideryarn orange as the single accent.

## The new idea: "Decorated mode"

Greg, 2026-08-28, dictated:

> The basic idea would be that we are still showing the actual article text, but we're sort of
> decorating it in a way that makes it easier to read, easier to skim, easier to follow the
> argument. So for example we might make critical sentences bigger, or phrases, or highlight them
> some other way. We might make less important stuff smaller or fainter. Perhaps we have callout
> boxes with quotes with particularly important things. Perhaps we include little diagrams showing
> the structure of things. Perhaps we include the headings, even if they're generated by us. We
> should certainly show the glossary underlines and comments — and basically play with how, given
> all of the power we have of LLM models understanding and the richness of HTML and what it can do
> for UIs, let's play with as many different ideas for how we can decorate or augment the article
> text.

## The hard constraint

**We may not remove any of the article's words, and we may not change any of its words.**
Every sentence the author wrote is still on the page, in order, in full. What we may change is
**how it is displayed**, and what we may **add around, between, beside and on top of it**.

So: size, weight, colour, opacity, spacing, background, underline, marginalia, inserted panels,
inserted generated headings, inserted diagrams, gutters, hover behaviour, scroll behaviour,
progressive disclosure of *our* additions — all fair game. Deleting a clause, paraphrasing a
sentence, or reordering paragraphs — not allowed.

A second, softer principle: a decoration must not become a way of **not** reading. If a device lets
the reader skip the prose and consume our summary instead, it is off-brief. If it makes the prose
itself easier to enter, hold, and interrogate, it is on-brief.

## The data we already have (all real, all committed, per article)

For each article, keyed by block id, we already generate:

- **blocks.json** — every block: `id`, `tag` (p/h2/h3/figure/blockquote), `kind`
  (text/heading/media/caption), `text`, `html`, `words`, `gistable` (false for boilerplate).
- **labels.json** — a one-line label for *every* block. E.g.
  `"spya-cvyfqe": "Blake Lemoine claimed LaMDA was conscious and Google fired him"`.
- **tree.json** — a deeply nested tree over the article (the ToC and the zoom tree are the same
  structure). Every node has `range: [firstBlockId, lastBlockId]`, `depth`, `title`, `gist`
  (a one-sentence summary of that span), and `sourceHeading` (the author's own heading, if the
  span had one — often null, so we have *generated* headings for sections that never had any).
- **summary.json** — summaries at three depths, each over a block range, each with `short` and
  `long` text, and each citing the block ids it came from, e.g. `[spya-e3cq2m]`.
- **arc.json** — a handful of "where we are in the argument" sentences, each over a block range.
  E.g. "Before computation itself can be interrogated, the psychological machinery that makes
  conscious AI feel plausible — anthropomorphism, exponential hype, techno-rapture — must be named
  as bias rather than evidence."
- **glossary.json** — terms/people/works this piece uses, each with `name`, `aliases`, `kind`
  (person/work/concept/…), `background` (a paragraph), `difficulty` (0–1), `centrality` (0–1), and
  the block ids where it occurs. Occurrences get underlined in the prose today.
- **ideas.json** — the *propositions the piece assumes*: `name`, `provenance`
  (assumed/argued/cited), `statement`, `whyYouNeedIt` (why the argument needs it), and
  `occurrences` (block ids + quoted spans).
- **comments.json** — the reader's own bookmarks and notes on quoted spans, with optional
  model answers.

We can also cheaply generate anything else a good idea needs — a new model pass is a prompt and a
JSON schema. So do **not** limit yourself to the artefacts above. If your idea needs "rhetorical
role of each sentence" or "the three numbers that matter in this piece" or "claim → evidence
edges", say so; that is a feature request we can fill.

## The article being used for the prototype

Anil Seth, "The Mythology Of Conscious AI" (NOEMA, ~8,300 words, 141 blocks, 19 glossary entries,
10 ideas, 25 summary entries, 15 comments). An argumentative long-form essay: it names a
psychological bias, refutes a theory, then draws an ethical conclusion. Assume that shape.

## What we want from you

**A long list of concrete, distinct ideas** for decorating the prose. Quantity *and* quality: aim
for 25–40. For each idea give:

1. **Name** — short and memorable.
2. **What the reader sees** — two or three sentences, concrete. Describe the pixels.
3. **Why it helps** — the reading problem it solves. Be specific about the cognitive or practical
   mechanism, not "it looks nice".
4. **Mechanism** — the actual HTML/CSS/JS. Name the properties. If it is a modern platform feature
   most people don't use (`::target-text`, `text-wrap: pretty`, anchor positioning, CSS
   `@scroll-timeline`/`animation-timeline: view()`, `content-visibility`, `hanging-punctuation`,
   `initial-letter`, `text-emphasis`, `mask-image`, `field-sizing`, popover, `<details name>`,
   custom highlights via the CSS Custom Highlight API, view transitions) say so — we like those.
5. **What data it needs** — from the list above, or a new model pass (say what the pass outputs).
6. **Risk** — how it could go wrong, be annoying, be ugly, or violate the "don't replace reading"
   principle.

Then finish with:

- **Your top 5**, ranked, with one line each on why.
- **Your one left-field idea** — the one you'd be a bit embarrassed to suggest but think might be
  great.

Bias towards ideas that are *surprising but defensible*. We already know about: pull quotes, a
progress bar, highlighted key sentences, hover glossary cards. Those are the floor, not the
ceiling. We want the ceiling.

Do not write code. Write the list. Markdown, headings per idea. Be dense; no preamble.

# Purpose, interest, curiosity, choice and personalisation

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: does knowing why someone is reading, what they are interested in and what they already know let a tool help them read better, and does letting them steer help or hurt? The short answer: the reader's *goal* demonstrably reshapes what is attended to and remembered, so asking for a purpose is well founded. Curiosity and interest help memory, but the best evidence is on trivia and school-age readers rather than experts reading papers. Choice reliably raises motivation, but control over *what to study and how* has near-zero average effect on learning, and the evidence that learners choose poorly is mostly argument, not data. Personalising content to interests has a few good trials, all in school maths, none on reading research papers.

## The short answer

- A reader's assigned perspective or goal changes which ideas they learn and recall, and a later change of perspective can retrieve ideas that were "unrecallable" under the first. The classic result is from undergraduates and short stories. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Readers build a "task model" from instructions and context, and that model steers how they read; the framework (RESOLV) is theory plus a review, not a single test. Relevance cues help readers find goal-relevant text, with the effect shown mostly on recall. (**Low-to-moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Interest and curiosity are associated with deeper learning from text and with better memory, including for incidental material encountered while curious. The curiosity studies are small lab experiments on trivia questions. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Interest is not the same as comprehension help: details that are merely interesting but irrelevant ("seductive details") tend to hurt retention and transfer. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Giving learners choice raises intrinsic motivation, effort and performance on average, but this is strongest for children and for choices unrelated to the instruction. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Letting learners control sequencing and pacing in educational technology has an overall effect near zero on learning (g = 0.05). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Personalising problems to interests helped algebra learners in one randomised study of 145 ninth-graders; there is no comparable evidence for adults reading papers. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Unrestricted generative AI help during practice can raise performance now and lower it once the help is removed; guardrailed tutors largely avoid that. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

## The evidence

### Goals and perspective change what is learned

Pichert and Anderson (1977): college undergraduates read stories from one of two assigned perspectives (the ERIC record's example is a home buyer or a florist; the widely cited version is a burglar vs a home buyer) or none. How important an idea was *to the assigned perspective* determined both how well it was learned and how well it was recalled a week later. A second experiment, summarised in the Anderson and Pichert technical report, found that shifting perspective at a second recall brought back more information that mattered to the new perspective but not the old. The authors' reading is that high-level schemata give a framework for understanding and guide retrieval of detail. (Abstract only, via ERIC records of the article and report; sample sizes and effect sizes not read.) Design is simple and has been replicated in many labs, but I did not verify a replication this session. **Moderate certainty · Analogy** (undergraduates, short narrative, not expository research prose).

Rouet, Britt and Durik (2017) set out RESOLV, a *theoretical model* in which readers build a **context model** (the physical and social situation) and a **task model** (goals and plans), and reading decisions follow how they interpret the task and implicit context cues (ERIC abstract summary; Crossref confirms authors and journal; the full text was blocked, so I cannot report the studies it reviews). It is a framework, not an effect size. **Low-to-moderate certainty · Indirect**: it is about readers working out what the task is, which is what Spideryarn's purpose box makes explicit.

McCrudden and Schraw (2007) review how instructions help readers find "germane" text. They sort relevance instructions into four kinds (targeted segments, elaborative interrogation, perspective, purpose) and propose a four-stage goal-focusing model (abstract only, via ERIC). I could not read the effect sizes; the abstract does not give them. **Moderate certainty · Indirect**.

### Interest and curiosity

Hidi and Renninger (2006) describe a four-phase model: triggered situational interest, maintained situational interest, emerging individual interest, well-developed individual interest (ERIC abstract). Theory-building, not an effect. Schiefele (1999) is a review that distinguishes personal from situational interest; its abstract reports strong links between interest and text learning, stronger for deep than surface learning, and largely independent of prior knowledge and cognitive ability (ERIC abstract only; no coefficients read). I did not open the Schiefele, Krapp and Winteler (1992) meta-analysis, so I give no pooled correlation. **Moderate certainty · Indirect**: mostly students, but the deep-vs-surface contrast is relevant to a "read deeply" product. Note that this is correlational evidence: people with more knowledge are often more interested, and the abstract's claim of independence from prior knowledge rests on the reviewed studies, not something I checked.

Kang et al. (2009), fMRI plus behavioural studies using trivia questions: self-reported curiosity correlated with caudate (reward-related) activity; more curious people spent more tokens or waiting time to see answers; higher curiosity correlated with better recall of surprising answers one to two weeks later (abstract summary via Europe PMC; participant numbers and statistics were not available to me). **Moderate certainty · Analogy**.

Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014): fMRI; people remembered information they were curious about better, and also *incidental* material encountered during high-curiosity states, on immediate and one-day-delayed tests; midbrain/nucleus accumbens activity rose with curiosity and its link with hippocampal activity tracked individual memory benefit (abstract summary via Europe PMC; N and statistics not available to me). **Moderate certainty · Analogy**: trivia and face photographs are not articles. The same study is the main source for the "curiosity spills over to nearby material" claim; I know of no replication on prose and did not search for one.

Seductive details: Rey (2012) meta-analysis of 39 experimental effects found small-to-medium harm to retention and medium harm to transfer from interesting-but-irrelevant additions (abstract summary via ERIC; no d values available). **Moderate certainty · Indirect**. This is the counterweight to "make it interesting": interest that is decoupled from the argument costs learning.

### Choice and learner control

Patall, Cooper and Robinson (2008), meta-analysis of 41 studies, children and adults: choice improved intrinsic motivation, effort, task performance and perceived competence. The motivation effect was larger when the choice was *instructionally irrelevant*, for children than for adults, with 2 to 4 successive choices, and when no reward followed (Europe PMC abstract summary; no numeric effect sizes available to me). **Moderate certainty · Analogy**.

Karich, Burns and Maki (2014), updated meta-analysis: of 85 articles, 18 met criteria; "the overall effect of including learner control within educational technology was almost zero (g = 0.05)"; moderate effects appeared in social studies/history and in comprehensive technology programmes; effects were larger for behavioural than academic outcomes, though both were small (ERIC abstract). **Moderate certainty · Indirect**: only 18 studies and heterogeneous technologies, but the finding is consistent with the argument below.

Kirschner and van Merriënboer (2013) argue that "the learner knows best" is an educational urban legend and that learners should not have maximum control over what they learn and how they progress (abstract on the Maastricht repository page; I could not read the full text, so I cannot say which studies they cite). It is an argument paper, not an experiment. **Contested · Indirect**: it is persuasive, but motivational benefits (Patall) pull the other way and much of its evidence is on novices.

### Personalisation

Walkington (2013): randomised experiment, 145 ninth-grade Algebra I students using Cognitive Tutor Algebra; half got standard story problems, half got problems matched to interests (sports, music, film). The personalised group solved problems faster and more accurately, with the largest gains in writing symbolic equations and among struggling students, and performed better on standard problems afterwards (ERIC abstract; no effect sizes read). **Low-to-moderate certainty · Analogy**: algebra problems, teenagers, one study.

Recent and unreviewed: a 2025 randomised study of 96 German fourth-graders found name-personalised narratives improved text-transfer scores but not recall (search-result summary of a University of Münster record; I did not open the paper, so this is **Not verified** below). A 2025 KAIST arXiv preprint reports interest-matched LLM-generated EFL passages improved comprehension and motivation retention; it is a preprint and is not load-bearing.

### Generative AI and over-helpful adaptation

Bastani et al. (2025, PNAS), a field experiment with "nearly a thousand" high-school maths students: GPT-4 access during practice improved practice performance, but when it was removed those who had used the unrestricted "GPT Base" scored about 17% lower than students who never had access; the "GPT Tutor" variant with learning-protecting prompts largely offset the harm (abstract via Europe PMC; the correction notice listed in Crossref (10.1073/pnas.2518204122) was not opened, so check it before quoting the 17%). **Moderate certainty · Analogy**. A 2025 Tsinghua arXiv preprint (Qin et al.) reports an AI instructional agent raised *perceived* learner control, which predicted post-test scores; preprint, not verified, not load-bearing.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- Most evidence is school-age or undergraduate, on short stories, trivia or maths, not experts reading papers. Experts have the prior knowledge that makes goals and interest work differently; whether an assigned perspective helps or constrains them is untested here.
- Perspective and goal effects are about *what is recalled*, and a goal can make a reader *worse* at everything else. The same pattern that helps a reader find the relevant part may produce narrow, goal-congruent memory. For a product whose aim is understanding the whole argument and "seeing problems", that is a cost, not a side issue.
- Curiosity findings are from fMRI studies with modest samples that I could not see; curiosity memory effects on prose and in real reading are not established here.
- "Interest improves comprehension" is partly causality in the wrong direction, and seductive-detail results show that interest-boosting additions can hurt.
- Learner-control evidence cuts both ways: choice improves motivation (Patall) while sequencing control does nothing on average (Karich). The claim that learners choose badly is argued (Kirschner) more than demonstrated in what I read.
- Personalisation to interests has one clean trial in this set, in maths. Personalisation of *difficulty or explanation level to prior knowledge* is not covered by anything I opened.
- Generative-AI personalisation can hurt learning once the help stops (Bastani), which is the strongest recent contrary signal.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### The reader profile and "why you're reading this"
How it works: an "About you" string and a per-article purpose go into model prompts; the previous version stored both but never fed them to a prompt, so the wiring is the point ([reader-profile.md](../../project/reader-profile.md)). The purpose is asked on the add page, in Metadata, in chat's greeting and in Skim ([skim.md](../../project/skim.md)).
**Supports**: the goal-shapes-memory literature (Pichert and Anderson; RESOLV; goal-focusing) is the strongest backing for asking, and for asking *early*.
**Suggests a change** (proposals): because a stated goal will narrow what is remembered, a mode could pose the purpose as the *route in*, then prompt for what the piece says that the purpose did not ask about (Pichert and Anderson's shifted-perspective retrieval suggests this recovers unattended content). Also consider letting the purpose be revised during reading, since RESOLV treats the task model as something readers construct and update.
**Untested**: whether a model that conditions its output on the purpose improves any reader outcome; no study here tests an AI doing so.

### Personalisation of explanations (glossary and chat)
How it works: the glossary uses the profile so *entropy* is not explained to a physicist ([reader-profile.md](../../project/reader-profile.md), [glossary.md](../../project/glossary.md)).
**Supports** (weakly, by analogy): Walkington's interest-matched problems helped, and the reader-profile idea of matching to background is in the same spirit.
**Suggests a change**: keep the personalisation to *background and level*, where the evidence is about prior knowledge, and be cautious about interest-flavoured examples that add interest-without-relevance (seductive details, Rey 2012).
**Untested**: any adult, article-length, AI-personalised explanation outcome.

### Experimental features switch (choice of which modes to see)
How it works: a single off-by-default setting shows only the polished features ([experimental-features.md](../../project/experimental-features.md)).
**Supports**: a curated default fits the case that learners choose poorly when given many options (Kirschner and van Merriënboer, argument-level).
**Suggests a change**: none firm; Patall's finding that irrelevant choices lift motivation does not argue for more modes.
**Untested**: whether readers who choose their own modes learn more or less than those on a default route.

### Dependence on AI assistance (vision.md's "augments, not replaces")
How it works: the stated aim is that the AI helps without doing the reader's thinking ([vision.md](../../project/vision.md)).
**Supports**: Bastani et al. is the closest direct evidence that an unguarded helper harms later performance and that a guardrailed one largely does not, which matches the brief.
**Untested**: that effect in reading tasks or with expert adults.

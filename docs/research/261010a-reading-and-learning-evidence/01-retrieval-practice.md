# Retrieval practice for prose

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

**The question:** if a reader has to pull an article's content out of their own head, rather than look
at it again, do they keep more of it, understand it better, and can they use it? And does that hold for
long, coherent prose and for adults who already know the field, or only for word lists and
undergraduates? **The short answer:** retrieval ("testing" in the literature) reliably beats re-reading
for what is remembered days or weeks later, and the best-controlled work uses real prose. But the
evidence for *understanding* and for complex material is thinner and more disputed than the headline
suggests, almost nothing has been done on expert adults reading research papers, and a hard test seems
to need the right answer shown afterwards to pay off.

## The short answer

- Recall after reading beats re-reading for retention a week or more later, in short prose passages and
  in roughly 2,500-word journal papers; the order flips if you test within minutes, when re-reading
  looks better. (**High certainty · Indirect**: undergraduates, short passages, memory for facts.)
- Across classrooms the average benefit is moderate (g about 0.5, 222 studies). (**High certainty ·
  Indirect**: students, not professionals.)
- Some of it transfers to new questions on the same material (d about 0.40 across 192 effect sizes),
  but it depends on what is practised: recalling a whole paragraph transferred; filling in single words
  mostly did not. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Without feedback, a hard test can do little. In the journal-paper study, short-answer testing beat the
  alternatives only when the correct answer was shown afterwards; a 2025 set of four experiments on
  texts found a testing effect only when feedback was given. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Question format matters less than whether the reader succeeds: produce-the-answer formats win when
  feedback is given or when success is reasonably high. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Whether the testing effect survives complex, tightly interlinked material is **contested**. A 2015
  special-issue review argued it shrinks or vanishes; the reply says that was overstated. Nobody has
  settled it with research papers. (**Contested · Indirect**)
- The famous result that retrieval beats concept-mapping is at least partly an artefact of the
  comparison (the retrieval group also got a second study period); a preregistered 2023 study found the
  advantage disappeared when mapping learners also memorised their maps. (**Contested · Indirect**)
- Existing knowledge of the topic did not reduce the benefit in four experiments, but those were
  undergraduates; the only expert-adult trial found was 40 medical residents, and I found no study of
  researchers reading papers. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)

## The evidence

### Does testing beat re-reading for prose?

**Roediger and Karpicke (2006)**, *Psychological Science*. Experiment 1: 120 Washington University
undergraduates studied two prose passages (256 and 275 words, from a TOEFL reading book, 30 idea units
each). One passage was studied again; the other was tested by writing down all they could remember, with
no feedback. Final recall was tested after 5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week. At 5 minutes re-studying won; at
2 days and 1 week the tested passage was recalled better (main effect of testing, F(1,117) = 36.39).
Experiment 2 (N = 180): students who studied once and were tested three times recalled 61% after a
week, against 40% for students who studied the passage four times, although the former had read it 3.4
times and the latter 14.2 times. The repeated-study group were also more confident they would remember
it. Full text read. Limitations: very short passages, undergraduates, memory for idea units, no transfer
test. (**High certainty · Indirect**)

**Rowland (2014)**, *Psychological Bulletin*, a meta-analysis of testing versus restudy. I read the
abstract only: it reports that initial recall tests produced larger benefits than recognition tests and
that the pattern fits "effortful processing" accounts better than semantic-elaboration accounts. A
review by Fraundorf et al. (2023) gives its figures as g = 0.50 overall, 0.41 for delays under a day and
0.69 for delays over a day; I did not verify those against Rowland himself.

**Yang et al. (2021)**, *Psychological Bulletin*: a meta-analysis of classroom quizzing, 222 studies,
48,478 students, overall g = 0.499, varying with the control condition, feedback, repetitions and design
(abstract only). **Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan (2017)**, *Review of Educational Research*:
practice tests beat restudy and every other comparison condition (abstract only; the figure g = 0.70 is
second-hand from Fraundorf et al. 2023). **Agarwal, Nunes and Blunt (2021)**, *Educational Psychology
Review*, a systematic review of 50 classroom experiments: I confirmed the record on Crossref but could
not read the paper; the report that 57% of 49 effect sizes were medium or large is a secondary summary.
The abstracts I read do not say how publication bias was handled.

**The nearest match to our readers.** Kang, McDermott and Roediger (2007), Experiment 1: 48
undergraduates read four papers from *Current Directions in Psychological Science* (about 2,500 words
each, figures removed), then took a multiple-choice test, a short-answer test, read a list of the key
statements, or did a filler task; the final test came three days later. People who had not read the
papers scored 0.30 (MC) and 0.02 (short answer), so the tests measured real learning. Full text read.
This is the closest the literature gets to "read a short academic paper and be tested", and the
population is still undergraduates.

### Free recall, cued recall or multiple choice?

- *Free recall* (write what you remember, no cue but the title) is the format of the Roediger and
  Karpicke experiments above, and the closest to Spideryarn's Recall mode.
- **Kang et al. (2007)**. Experiment 1, no feedback: an intervening multiple-choice test gave better
  final scores than a short-answer test, probably because short-answer success was so low on these
  papers, and it did not beat simply reading the statements. Experiment 2 (another 48 students), with
  the correct answer shown after each question: short-answer testing gave the best final scores and beat
  reading the same statements. Initial success in Experiment 2 was low (my text extract of the paper is
  partly garbled; it reads .19 for short answer). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- **Smith and Karpicke (2014)**, *Memory*: four experiments, 372 participants, educational texts, tests
  one week later, verbatim and inference questions. All formats beat study-only; short-answer and
  "hybrid" (recall, then choose from options) beat multiple choice only in the experiment where
  short-answer success was higher (abstract only). The lesson is about success rate more than format.
- Rowland's abstract points the same way: recall beat recognition (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**).

### Feedback: whether, what, and when

- **Whether.** Butler and Roediger (2008, *Memory & Cognition*): after reading passages and taking a
  multiple-choice test, immediate or delayed feedback gave more correct answers and fewer carried-over
  wrong choices ("intrusions") on a later cued-recall test than no feedback (abstract only; N not
  available to me). Macaluso and Fraundorf (2025, *Memory*; four experiments, college students reading
  texts, tested a week later): **an overall testing effect appeared only when feedback was given**
  (abstract only). That is about learning from text directly, and it cuts against assuming free recall
  alone is enough.
- **What.** Fraundorf et al. (2023, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*; a review, full
  text read): saying only "correct" or "incorrect" confers little or no benefit; giving the right answer
  helps; extra explanation has not reliably added more.
- **When.** Butler, Karpicke and Roediger (2007, *JEP: Applied*): after a multiple-choice test on
  280-300-word passages, delayed feedback beat immediate feedback on a later cued-recall test; the type
  of feedback made no difference (abstract only; N not given). Fraundorf et al. summarise the wider
  picture: delayed feedback is often at least as good in labs, immediate feedback tends to win in
  classrooms. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**) What this means for a back-and-forth conversation
  with an AI is untested.

### Delay to the final test

At a few minutes after reading, re-reading looks better; after two days and a week, testing is better
(Roediger and Karpicke 2006). Larsen, Butler and Roediger (2009, *Medical Education*) found quizzed
topics at 39% against 26% for reviewed topics at about **six months**: 40 paediatric and emergency-medicine
residents, each quizzed on one topic and reviewed on the other, with feedback; d = 0.91 (abstract only).
That is the longest delay and the most expert sample I found, but it is a small trial on taught clinical
content, not reading. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

### Transfer: does it help beyond the exact questions practised?

**Pan and Rickard (2018)**, *Psychological Bulletin*: 192 transfer effect sizes from 122 experiments,
N = 10,382; overall d = 0.40. Transfer was strongest to different test formats, to application and
inference questions, and to medical diagnosis; weakest for rearranged stimulus-response items and
worked-example problems; it depended on how similar the retrieval response was to the transfer task, on
elaborated retrieval, and on how well people did on the practice test (abstract only).
(**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

**Butler (2010)**, *JEP: LMC*: four experiments with prose passages; repeated testing beat repeated
rereading on a one-week test of new inference questions, including questions from a different subject
area (abstract only). **Hinze and Wiley (2011)**, *Memory*, give the caution: after fill-in-the-blank
tests the benefit was confined to the repeated items at two and seven days, and only open-ended recall of
a whole paragraph improved performance on new questions (abstract only; N not available to me). The scope
of processing during retrieval seems to decide how far the benefit spreads.
(**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

### Does it work for complex, coherent prose?

This is the main contested question. **van Gog and Sweller (2015)**, an introduction to a special issue of
*Educational Psychology Review* (full text read, open access), argue that the testing effect "decreases
or even disappears" as the number of interacting elements a learner must hold at once rises. Their
Table 1 lists studies with the authors' own rating of "element interactivity": word pairs and fact lists
are low and show the effect; short text passages are rated medium-to-high (with question marks) and mostly
show it, including Roediger and Karpicke (2006) and Agarwal et al. (2008); but the table also lists Kang et
al. (2007) Experiment 1, with journal papers rated high, as a null for both short-answer and
multiple-choice, and worked examples on electrical circuits as a null. They cite Gates (1917) as finding
that the advantage of recitation over reading was smaller for connected prose than for word lists.
**Caution:** the ratings are the authors' judgement, not a measurement; the paper is a framing review, not
a meta-analysis; and several of its nulls also lacked feedback or other conditions that matter elsewhere.
Kang et al.'s Experiment 1 null for short answer turned positive in Experiment 2 once feedback was added,
which suggests feedback, not complexity alone, may have been the missing ingredient.

The reply, Karpicke and Aue (2015), as I know it only from secondary descriptions (see Not verified),
argues that element interactivity was never measured, that the nulls used isolated words or massed
practice, and that the original review omitted studies showing an effect with complex material. Neither
side tested research papers read by experts. (**Contested · Indirect**)

### Does it beat other active strategies (concept mapping)?

**Karpicke and Blunt (2011)**, *Science* (abstract-level via Europe PMC; the paper is paywalled): students
who practised retrieval on a science text did better than students who built concept maps one week later,
including on inference questions. A later critique describes it as about 20 per condition, one
sea-otter text, a verbatim effect of d = 1.50. **Replications**, as reported by Mayrhofer et al. (2023):
Lechuga et al. (2015) found d = 0.96 on verbatim and 0.62 on inference questions, notably smaller;
O'Day and Karpicke (2021) reported a near-identical result; Blunt and Karpicke (2014) found no advantage
when both groups had extra memorisation.

**Mayrhofer, Kuhbandner and Frischholz (2023)**, *Frontiers in Psychology* (full text read): a
preregistered replication, N = 230 German university students, final test one week later (16 items, 14
verbatim, 2 inference). The retrieval group beat concept mapping on verbatim questions (0.73 vs 0.59) but
**not** a concept-mapping group that also memorised its map (0.70, p = .854). The authors say the original
comparison confounded retrieval with an extra five-minute study phase and different instructions. On the
two inference questions, retrieval was descriptively *lower* (0.71 vs 0.78), which they call tentative.
One study, but preregistered and larger than the original, so the claim that retrieval beats elaborative
study is not safe. (**Contested · Indirect**)

### Does it work for experts?

- **Macaluso and Fraundorf (2025)**: background material improved retention overall but **did not change
  the size of the testing benefit**; the authors conclude the benefit does not depend on prior domain
  knowledge (abstract only, college students). The caution repeated: an overall testing effect appeared
  only with feedback.
- Larsen et al. (2009) used medical residents (above). Fraundorf et al. (2023) say relatively limited
  work has tested retrieval practice in physicians and that more rigorous work is needed.
- Buchin and Mulligan (2022, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, "Retrieval-based learning and prior
  knowledge") looks directly relevant, but I could read only the Crossref record, so I make no claim
  from it.
- I found no study of retrieval practice on researchers or professionals reading papers in their own
  field. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)

### Calibration, effort and time

Retrieval makes people feel they know less: in Roediger and Karpicke (2006) the restudy groups predicted
better recall, and Mayrhofer et al. (2023) saw the same in judgements of learning. So retrieval does not
inflate confidence, but readers will often experience it as the worse strategy. Nothing I opened reports
learning per minute for prose in adults; the studies mostly equate time.

### Generative-AI questions

The one study I found that tests LLM-written retrieval questions (An et al., 2025) is an **arXiv
preprint** with about 60 students in two data-science courses, comparing a week with generated
multiple-choice practice against a week without; it reports 89% against 73% accuracy and does not
describe randomisation (abstract only). It is not load-bearing. I found no peer-reviewed controlled trial
of AI-generated questions on learning from an article with a delayed test.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Population.** Almost all undergraduates or school pupils; prose mostly 250-2,500 words chosen by the
  experimenters; the outcome is usually memory for facts or "idea units". One expert-adult trial found,
  and it was clinical teaching, not reading.
- **Complexity** is a live dispute (above), and both sides argue over the same studies.
- **The concept-mapping win** is partly a confound. That matters because it was the main evidence that
  retrieval beats "deeper" elaborative study.
- **No feedback, little effect** in two sets of results (Kang et al. 2007 Experiment 1; Macaluso and
  Fraundorf 2025). A tester that never shows the right answer risks wasting the reader's effort.
- **Retrieval lowers confidence** and is disliked, which matters in a product where the reader can simply
  stop.
- **Publication bias** is not addressed in any abstract I read.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Recall mode (free recall, hints, never fail twice)

**How it works today.** The reader types or says what they took from the piece; the model corrects at
most once, links the passage, and nudges them to remember more; after two rungs of hinting it tells (see
[learn-mode.md](../../project/learn-mode.md) and [learning-vision.md](../../project/learning-vision.md)).
The earlier research ([261002c](../261002c-recall-and-tutorial-pedagogy-for-remember-mode.md)) already
supplied the hint ladder and "give the answer after a miss".

**Supports.** Free recall of the whole piece is the format of the strongest experiments (Roediger and
Karpicke 2006), and an attempt followed by the answer is what the feedback results favour (Kang et al.
2007; Macaluso and Fraundorf 2025). "Never make them fail twice" fits the finding that success rate
matters more than format (Smith and Karpicke 2014).

**Suggests a change (proposals).** (1) The benefit is smallest right after reading and grows over days,
so a recall prompt a day or more later may beat one straight after reading. (2) Open-ended recall
transferred better than fill-in-the-blank (Hinze and Wiley 2011), which argues against making Recall
more cloze-like. (3) Retrieval feels worse than re-reading; the mode could say that struggling is
expected. **Untested:** whether feedback from a conversation is as good as scripted feedback.

### Quiz mode (short answers, a path, a marker)

**How it works today.** Up to twenty small short-answer questions in a model-set order, each marked
against the article, a reference answer behind a collapsed control, and no grade
([quiz.md](../../project/quiz.md)).

**Supports.** Short answers with feedback were the best format on journal papers (Kang et al. 2007), and a
path of easy-first small steps raises the success rate that Smith and Karpicke (2014) found to matter.
Marking against the article and showing what it says is the feedback kind that works; "no grade" avoids
the verification-only trap (Fraundorf et al. 2023).

**Suggests a change (proposals).** Initial success on journal papers was low in Kang et al., so early
questions should be answerable. Pan and Rickard say transfer depends on how like the practice the target
task is, so a mix of verbatim and inference-style questions is better than facts alone. **Untested:** LLM
questions as retrieval practice (preprint only), and whether opening the reference answer before trying
costs the benefit (I did not check whether the panel allows that).

### Spacing across days (not built)

[learning-vision.md](../../project/learning-vision.md) calls coming back after a delay "the strongest
effect in the literature". This file is about retrieval, not spacing as such, but its delay results
(testing beating re-reading from two days on; six-month retention in residents) point the same way and
**support** building it.

### Expert readers

The profile and reason-for-reading adaptation is not contradicted: topic knowledge did not shrink the
retrieval benefit in the one study that tested it (Macaluso and Fraundorf 2025). Whether retrieval helps
as much for an expert reading a paper in their own field is **untested**.

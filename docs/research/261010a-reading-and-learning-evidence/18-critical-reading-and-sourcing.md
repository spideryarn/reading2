# Critical reading: checking sources, reading across documents, and correcting what you believed

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: does a reader judge a piece better if they attend to who wrote it, where and when;
if they check it against other documents rather than staying inside it; if the text confronts a
mistaken belief head-on; or if they ask "why would this be true?" as they go? The short answer is
that **source-checking is the best-supported of these and it can be taught**, but almost all the
trials are on school pupils and undergraduates judging web pages, not experts reading papers in
their own field. **Refutation text** (a passage that states a common misconception, says it is
wrong, then gives the correct account) reliably helps in the short run, with an open question
about how long it lasts. "Why would this be true?" prompts are mixed. And there is a warning for a
tool like ours: the one large experiment on AI fact-checks found they did not help people tell true
from false.

## The short answer

- Readers who judge which sources are reliable learn more from a set of web sources, and a short
  instruction unit on judging reliability improved both judgements and later learning in
  undergraduates (Wiley et al. 2009, two experiments). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Lateral reading (leaving a page to find out about its source, as professional fact-checkers do)
  separated fact-checkers from historians and students in one small think-aloud study, and a handful
  of teaching studies show large gains in its use. The best of them are uncontrolled or only
  summarised in what I could open. Durability is untested. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Lateral reading is a method for judging an unfamiliar *publisher*. It does not tell you whether a
  paper's argument is sound, and nobody has tested it on expert readers of research papers.
  (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Refutation text improves post-test knowledge relative to other learning conditions (g = 0.41, 44
  comparisons, N = 3,869; abstract only). One experiment with teachers found the benefit
  short-lived. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Trained peer reviewers still miss most planted errors: 607 BMJ reviewers found about 2.6 of 9
  major errors, and short training moved this a little (Schroter et al. 2008; Cochrane review 2023,
  low-certainty evidence). Noticing flaws in a paper is hard for experts and does not improve much
  with brief instruction. (**Moderate certainty · Direct**)
- Asking readers to "explain why" has a reasonable record on short facts and expository text, but
  the one study I found on multiple-source synthesis was null (86 online participants).
  (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- An LLM's fact-check, shown to 2,159 US adults, did not improve discernment of true from false
  headlines, and made some false ones more believable where it was unsure. Human fact-checks did
  help (DeVerna et al. 2024). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Whether any of this lifts the *depth* of a reader's own critique of a paper they are reading is not
  something I could find tested. (**Low certainty · Direct**)

## The evidence

### Does attending to the source improve what you learn?

**Wiley et al. 2009** (*American Educational Research Journal*, abstract only). Two experiments with
undergraduates doing web-based science inquiry. In Experiment 1 students explained what caused the
Mt St Helens eruption from search results; regression showed that how well they discriminated
reliable from unreliable sources predicted what they learned. In Experiment 2 a short unit (SEEK)
taught source evaluation on a different topic (the Atkins diet); that group made more differentiated
reliability judgments and then learned more on the volcano task than a comparison group. This is the
closest thing here to a causal chain from "attend to sources" to "learn more", in adults.
Limitations: web pages and science topics a layperson reads, a brief post-test, and no effect sizes
in the abstract. A think-aloud follow-up (a *Reading Research Quarterly* paper, 2012, abstract only; 10 better and 11 poorer
learners) found better learners did more sense-making and self-explanation on reliable sites than on
unreliable ones, which is descriptive, not causal.

**Pérez et al. 2018** (*Learning and Instruction*, abstract only). A classroom unit teaching ninth
graders three source dimensions (author competence, author motivation, pre-publication validation)
made treated pupils rate less reliable links lower and cite more reliable sources (a scientific
journal rather than a personal blog) in a transfer task with contradictory texts, compared with
controls. Outcomes varied by the type of question. Fifteen-year-olds, not experts.

**Brante & Strømsø 2018** (*Educational Psychology Review*, abstract only). A review of 18 sourcing
interventions in educational settings. Using multiple partly conflicting documents was common and was
"positively related to outcome measures". The authors report that none of the studies measured *how*
students look for source features, and that interventions rarely address motivation, since checking
credibility takes effort. No pooled effect size. Younger students were taught credibility, older ones
also taught how a source shapes interpretation.

**Expert readers.** Wineburg 1991 (*Journal of Educational Psychology*, abstract and opening only)
had working historians and high-school seniors think aloud about documents on the Battle of Lexington;
the groups reasoned differently about evidence, and the author attributes this partly to beliefs that
frame historical inquiry. Shanahan, Shanahan & Misischia 2011 (*Journal of Literacy Research*,
abstract only) had six disciplinary experts (two each in history, chemistry, mathematics) think
aloud: differences appeared in "sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, close reading and
rereading, critical response to text" and in how structure and graphics were used. N is six, so this
is hypothesis-generating, but it is the nearest I found to *how experts actually read*, and it says
that what counts as critical reading differs by field. I found no think-aloud study of working
scientists evaluating a paper in their own subfield that I could open (see Not verified).

### Lateral reading versus vertical reading

**Wineburg & McGrew 2019** (*Teachers College Record*, abstract only). 45 participants: 10 PhD
historians, 10 professional fact-checkers, 25 Stanford undergraduates thought aloud over live
websites. Historians and students "often fell victim to easily manipulated features" (logos,
domains) and read *vertically*, staying on the site. Fact-checkers read *laterally*, leaving after a
quick scan and opening new tabs to judge the site, and "arrived at more warranted conclusions in a
fraction of the time". It is a small, expert/novice comparison on non-science topics. It describes
what the fact-checkers do; it does not show that copying them makes anyone better. The historians
(highly skilled readers of sources) failing at this is the point: reading skill within a page does not
substitute.

**Teaching it.**

- Breakstone et al. 2021 (*HKS Misinformation Review*, read in full via the publisher page). 87
  undergraduates in an online nutrition course did four one-hour modules. No control group. Students
  using lateral reading on a climate-denial site went from 3/87 (3.4%) to 67/87 (77.0%); total score
  (13-point scale) from 3.95 to 7.08. The authors say they do not know the durability of the change,
  and participants were paid in gift cards. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**: large, but
  single-group.)
- Brodsky et al. 2021 (*AERA Open*, abstract only). 221 college students in a civics course improved
  pre to post in using lateral reading on COVID-19 news; confidence rose much more than skill (it was
  only weakly associated with lateral reading).
- Wineburg et al. 2022 (*Journal of Educational Psychology*; metadata confirmed via Crossref; I could
  not open the abstract or text, so figures come from the research group's own summary page, which is
  not the article). Six one-hour lessons in district government classes; the group's page describes a
  cluster-randomised, repeated-measures analysis with treated classrooms significantly better at
  judging credibility. I could not confirm sample sizes or effect sizes. Treat as **claimed, unverified**.
- Kohnen et al. 2020 (*Journal of Media Literacy Education*, abstract only). Eighth graders taught
  fact-checker strategies more often left a page to investigate and preferred more credible sources
  when two were presented, but "few students improved" on judging a single deceptive site. Authors
  conclude skills need background knowledge of how the web is structured. Axelsson et al. 2021
  (*Future Internet*, abstract only; 209 upper-secondary students) report better lateral reading and
  credibility judgements after online tutorials.
- Panizza et al. 2022 (*Scientific Reports*, abstract only). Two pre-registered online experiments
  (N = 5,387): a pop-up advising lateral reading and click restraint raised use of fact-checking
  strategies and helped accuracy when the source was unknown; monetary incentives helped accuracy
  more overall. So the effort cost matters.
- Guess et al. 2020 (*PNAS*; a 2023 correction concerns a line of text in the report, not the
  headline result as far as I read). Generic "tips to spot false news" improved discernment by 26.5%
  in a US national sample and 17.5% in a highly educated Indian online sample; the effect persisted
  weeks later in the US but not in India, and was absent in a rural Indian sample. This is
  a lighter intervention than lateral reading, but it shows the effects are not uniform.

Population is school pupils or undergraduates; materials are websites, advocacy pages and news
headlines. None involved a specialist reading a paper in their own field. A known caveat from the
authors themselves is that none of the studies could test whether habits last or apply outside the
course.

### Refutation texts

A refutation text names a plausible misconception, flags it as wrong, and then explains the correct
view. **The strongest summary is Schroeder & Kucera 2022**
(*Educational Psychology Review*, abstract only): a random-effects meta-analysis of between-subjects
experiments, 44 independent comparisons, N = 3,869, **g = 0.41** against other learning conditions
(p < .001), described as "consistent and robust across a wide variety of contexts". I could not
open the moderator, publication-bias or delay results; the abstract does not give them.
Tippett 2010 (*International Journal of Science and Mathematics Education*, abstract only) reviewed
two decades of work and concluded that reading refutation rather than traditional expository text
is "more likely to produce conceptual change", finding no developmental trend; her reference list
includes conference papers and unpublished work. Guzzetti et al. 1993 (*Reading Research Quarterly*)
is the early meta-analysis I was asked to check; I could confirm its existence and title only, not
its numbers.

**Does it last, and for whom?** The only adult test I could open is a *Journal of Experimental
Psychology: Applied* paper (2020, abstract only; authors not recorded here): refutation texts corrected strongly held false ideas among in-service teachers, but
"these effects may be short-lived" and did not change intentions to use the practices. Most of the
literature is on school pupils and undergraduates, short passages and common-sense science
misconceptions; whether a refutation helps an expert whose "misconception" is a technical belief
in their field is untested here. A Science paper (Costello et al. 2024; 2,190 conspiracy believers;
GPT-4 dialogues; belief down ~20%, still present at two months, abstract only) is a form of
tailored refutation by AI. Note that the journal published an Editorial Expression of Concern on
2026-06-11 and the authors have deposited correction materials. I confirmed both exist and could not
open them, so I do not rely on the headline number.

### Teaching argument evaluation more generally

Abrami et al. 2015 (*Review of Educational Research*, abstract only): 341 effect sizes from
quasi- and true-experiments with standardised critical-thinking measures, weighted mean **g+ = 0.30**,
heterogeneous. Effective strategies included dialogue, authentic problems and mentoring. Both general
and content-embedded instruction worked. This supports the idea that argument evaluation can be
taught, at modest size, and mostly with discussion rather than solitary reading.

### Elaborative interrogation ("why would this be true?")

Ozgungor & Guthrie 2004 (*Journal of Educational Psychology*, abstract only): elaborative
interrogation during expository text reading improved recall, inference and coherence, with larger
inference benefits for lower-interest students and larger coherence benefits for lower-knowledge
students. By contrast, an 86-participant online experiment (*Open Information Science* 2019, abstract
only) found "why" prompts did not significantly improve integration or transformation of ideas across
five climate texts. A ten-technique meta-analysis (Donoghue & Hattie 2021, *Frontiers in Education*,
abstract only; 242 studies, mean effect 0.56) cautions that most studies use surface or factual
outcomes and that effects were bigger for lower-ability students. Dunlosky et al. 2013 lists it as
one of ten techniques; I did not open the monograph's rating (see Not verified).

### Reviewers' error detection

Schroter et al. 2008 (*Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine*, abstract read in full): 607 BMJ
reviewers randomised to face-to-face training, a self-taught package or control, each sent three test
papers with nine major and five minor inserted errors. Baseline average **2.58 of 9 major errors**;
later papers 2.71 and 3.0; interventions had "small effects"; biased randomisation was found most
often. The conclusion: editors should not assume reviewers detect most major errors, "particularly
those concerned with the context of study". The 2023 Cochrane review of reviewer training (10 RCTs,
1,213 units; abstract read in full) found low-certainty evidence that training slightly improves error
detection (MD 0.55, 95% CI 0.20 to 0.90; one RCT, 418 reviewers), and moderate-certainty evidence that
emailed checklist reminders had little or no effect on completeness of reporting (MD 0.02). This is
the only **Direct** evidence here: the population is people doing the job Spideryarn's Referee mode
is for.

### Generative AI

- DeVerna et al. 2024 (*PNAS*, abstract plus full text via Europe PMC). Pre-registered randomised
  experiment, N = 2,159 US participants, 40 real political headlines. The LLM labelled 90% of false
  headlines correctly yet its fact-checks did not significantly improve discernment or sharing of
  accurate news; human fact-checks did. They *decreased* belief in true headlines mislabelled false
  and *increased* belief in false headlines the model was unsure of. People who chose to view the AI
  check were more likely to believe false headlines. Headline-level judgements, not reading a paper.
- Lee et al. 2025 (CHI, a conference paper; survey of knowledge workers, self-reported): reports
  reduced cognitive effort and confidence-related shifts with generative AI. Not load-bearing.
- Danry et al. 2023 (CHI, conference, abstract only; N = 204): AI that framed its information as
  *questions* improved discernment of logical flaws compared with no feedback and with causal AI
  explanations. A short task on socially divisive statements, so Analogy at best.
- Si et al. 2024 (NAACL, conference, title only): LLM explanations help verification except when
  convincingly wrong. I did not open its results.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Almost nobody here is an expert reading a paper.** The people tested are pupils, undergraduates,
  teachers and general-public online samples reading short web items. Fact-checkers and historians
  are experts at sources, not at the substance of a scientific literature. The leap to "a
  researcher reading a paper in their field" is untested.
- **Lateral reading answers "who is this publisher?"** not "is the inference sound?" A journal-hosted
  paper passes a lateral check and may still be flawed. The reviewer evidence (2.6 of 9 errors) is
  about the second problem, where sourcing doesn't help.
- **Durability is mostly unknown** (Breakstone 2021 says so; the teachers' refutation study found fading).
- **Uncontrolled and self-reported designs** dominate the lateral-reading teaching studies; the best
  controlled one I could not open.
- **Confidence outruns skill** (Brodsky 2021).
- **The Costello result carries an Expression of Concern** that I could not read.
- **Heterogeneity.** Abrami's critical-thinking meta-analysis is heterogeneous; the refutation
  meta-analysis's publication-bias checks were not visible to me.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Sources: Bibliography, Reception, Claims

How it works: one band, three panels. Bibliography lists "every work the piece cites, with a link out";
Reception is "what the rest of the web says about the piece"; Claims lists the claims the piece rests
on, which the reader can check against the web ([sources.md](../../project/sources.md),
[bibliography.md](../../project/bibliography.md), [reception.md](../../project/reception.md)).

- **Supports.** The mode does outside-the-document checking, which is the lateral-reading move, and
  the evidence that doing so beats staying inside the page is real (Wineburg & McGrew 2019; the
  teaching studies). Wiley 2009 suggests that discriminating reliable from unreliable sources predicts
  learning. Reception, "if no one has written about this piece, let's just say so", avoids inventing
  authority.
- **Suggests a change (proposals).** (1) The reader still has to *do* the leaving. DeVerna's result
  warns against presenting a model's verdict; showing the source rows and letting the reader decide is
  the safer shape. Keep the rows primary and the model's labels secondary. (2) Show the source's
  author position, motive and pre-publication validation, the three dimensions Pérez 2018 taught, on
  each Reception row, so the reader gets the sourcing prompt without a lesson. (3) Where the model is
  unsure, say so; DeVerna found unsure labels raised belief in false items.
- **Untested.** Whether expert readers behave like Wineburg's students here, or whether the panel
  changes what they conclude about a paper.

### Referee mode

How it works: five sub-modes for someone asked to peer review (Criteria, Claims, Mirror, Candidates,
Hidden text); the design principle is "no verdict" and not writing the review for the reviewer
([referee-mode.md](../../project/referee-mode.md)).

- **Supports.** The error-detection data (2.6 of 9) say reviewers need help noticing, and the
  Cochrane finding that brief training barely helps is an argument for a tool in the loop rather than
  for training alone. The "no verdict" rule matches DeVerna: an AI label did not improve discernment.
- **Suggests a change.** Referee's Mirror and Candidates could be framed as *questions* rather than
  findings (Danry 2023, conference, N = 204), as a proposal to test, since question framing beat causal
  explanations on a short discernment task.
- **Untested.** Whether a tool that surfaces candidates raises the 2.6 detected-error figure. The
  Schroter design (planted errors, three test papers) is a ready-made test.

### Ideas and FAQ

How they work: Ideas lists "the ideas an article leans on without stating, and the ideas it puts
forward" ([ideas.md](../../project/ideas.md)). FAQ lists the questions a careful first-time reader
would ask, each with the passages where the piece itself responds ([faq.md](../../project/faq.md)),
described there as "interrogate, worked out in advance".

- **Supports (weakly).** FAQ is a pre-made form of "why/how" questioning; Ozgungor & Guthrie 2004
  found elaborative interrogation improved inference and coherence, especially for lower-knowledge
  readers.
- **Suggests a change.** Elaborative interrogation helps when *the reader generates* the answer.
  Reading someone else's answer may not do the same, and the null synthesis study suggests prompts
  alone do not give integration across documents. A proposal: let the reader attempt a one-line answer to a
  FAQ question before revealing the passages.
- **Untested.** Whether reading model-written questions has any learning effect at all for experts.

### Refutation text

Spideryarn does not have a refutation feature. The evidence (g = 0.41 on average; short-lived in the
teachers' study) suggests a place for one: where the reader's own note or Learn-mode answer states a
common misconception, a "here is the usual wrong idea, here is why the piece says otherwise" card.
Product idea, not recommended on this evidence alone, because the population is non-expert and
retention is doubtful.

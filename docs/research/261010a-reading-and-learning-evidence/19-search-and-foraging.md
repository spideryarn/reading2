# Searching a document, information foraging, and searching as learning

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: when a reader can *search* a document for a passage, by its words or by what it means, do they read differently, and do they learn more or less? The short answer: the evidence says very little about search *inside one document* by experts, so most of what follows is by analogy from three neighbouring literatures: information-foraging theory (how people allocate attention), task-oriented reading (what happens when you read in order to answer something) and "searching as learning" (learning during web search). Together they say that a goal makes you learn the goal-relevant parts better and the rest worse, that the cost of a lookup falls on retention rather than on the immediate answer, and that tools which hand over a finished answer teach less than tools which make you assemble one. A tool that returns *places to read* sits on the safer side of every one of these, but nobody has tested it directly.

## The short answer

- A reading goal narrows learning. Telling readers in advance what is relevant improves learning of the relevant parts at no extra reading time, and the non-relevant parts are learned less well (two experiments, undergraduates, short texts). So a reader who arrives by a search query will probably remember the matching passage better and the rest of the piece worse. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Answering questions with the text open produces better answers *now* but the advantage can vanish after a day: in one study of 70 secondary students, text availability helped intratext question scores, and the advantage was gone in free recall 24 hours later. Lookup performance is a poor guide to what was learned. (**Low certainty · Analogy**, abstract only)
- Which task you set changes how deeply the text is processed: an integration question (essay) produced more cross-text integration and better deep learning than short intratext questions, with no difference in plain recall. A search box encourages the second kind of task; a "how do these connect?" prompt encourages the first. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**, abstract only)
- Skimming under time pressure is a "satisficing" strategy: it helped memory for the important ideas, not for details or inferences, and its edge depended on how easy navigation was. Selective reading is not intrinsically shallow, but it buys the main points and gives up the rest. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**, abstract only)
- Receiving a synthesised answer in place of sources produces shallower learning and less effortful work, across several experiments, but the measures are largely self-report and advice-writing on everyday topics. This is evidence about *replacing* reading with an answer, not about searching inside a piece you then read. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- A search tool is only as good as the reader's judgement of when to look: in task-oriented reading, deciding whether to search was tied to judgements of learning, and skilled comprehenders differ from less skilled ones in monitoring and controlling search. (**Low certainty · Analogy**, abstract only)
- Searching-as-learning is a young field: its own reviews say experimentally obtained ground truth linking search behaviour to knowledge gain is "very limited", and eye-tracking results conflict about whether learners who gain more read more or less. (**Contested · Analogy**)

## The evidence

### Information foraging: why a search box changes where you look

Information-foraging theory (Pirolli & Card 1999, *Psychological Review*) borrows optimal foraging from biology and models people as trading the value of information against the cost of getting it. Its three parts are *patches* (clusters of information, such as a section), *scent* (cues such as a heading or a snippet that let you judge a patch's value before entering it) and *diet* (which items to pursue at all). I confirmed the paper's metadata only; I could not open the text, and my account of its three components comes from a search-result summary of the abstract, so it is a description of a theory, not an evidenced finding (see Not verified). The theory is a model that explains well-known behaviour; it is not itself an experiment showing that any intervention improves learning.

What it gives the design question is a vocabulary. A results list with a place bar, a printed score and a snippet is a scent display. The reader reading a snippet is judging a patch.

### Skimming is foraging, and it has a measured cost

Duggan & Payne (2009, *J Exp Psychol: Applied*) is the closest experimental study of selective reading of expository text. Three experiments gave readers only enough time to read half of each document (abstract only). Compared with reading half the text, skimming improved memory for important ideas but not for less important details or inferences. Skimming showed no advantage over simply reading the first or second half of every paragraph. In the two website-like experiments, the skimming advantage depended on how easy it was to navigate between pages. Skim readers spent more time on text earlier in a paragraph and earlier in the document, which the authors read as "satisficing": stop when it is good enough. Population: undergraduates (the abstract does not give N). Text: expository passages, not research papers. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

For Spideryarn: a reader who jumps from hit to hit is skimming by a different route, and the same costs (details and inferences left behind) are the plausible price.

### A goal narrows learning

McCrudden, Schraw & Kambe (2005, *J Educ Psychol*) tested relevance instructions: telling readers beforehand which parts matter. In Experiment 1 (scientific text, read sentence by sentence on a computer) the instructions improved learning of relevant segments without increasing reading time, while non-relevant segments were learned less well and took longer to read. Experiment 2 found the same pattern with an informational narrative. The authors call this a "no increased effort" pattern and attribute it to goal-focusing (abstract only; N not given in what I read). McCrudden & Schraw (2007, *Educ Psychol Rev*) reviewed this literature and proposed a four-stage goal-focusing model (abstract only). Population: undergraduates (as I read it); texts short and constructed. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

This is the central point for search. The reader who types "arguments against substrate independence" has set a relevance instruction for themselves. The study says they will learn the matching passages efficiently and the rest less well. That is a feature if the goal is to find something and a cost if the goal is to understand the piece.

### Question-answering is not comprehension

Task-oriented reading is the research tradition that studies reading when you know you must do something with the text, usually answer questions.

- Cerdán & Vidal-Abarca (2008, *J Educ Psychol*): undergraduates answered either one integrative essay question across several scientific documents, or shorter intratext questions. The integration question increased integration and *decreased* processing of isolated units, improving deep learning; there was no difference in memory recall. Half the sample thought aloud. (abstract only; N not given). (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Cerdán, Mañez & Serrano-Mendizábal (2021, *Reading Research Quarterly*): 70 secondary-school students read three expository texts on a history topic, then answered intra- and intertext questions with or without the texts available. The text-available condition scored higher on intratext questions, but that superiority disappeared in free recall after a 24-hour delay without the materials. (abstract only). (**Low certainty · Analogy**; adolescents, one topic)
- Vidal-Abarca, Mañá & Gil (2010, *J Educ Psychol*): in the first experiment, 7th and 8th graders read two texts and answered 16 questions with all actions logged; in the second, eighth graders judged learning before each question and then decided whether to search. Deciding to search tracked judgements of learning. Task-oriented reading "places specific demands on readers related to metacognitive monitoring" (abstract only). (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Cataldo & Oakhill (2000, *J Educ Psychol*): good comprehenders located information in a text more efficiently than poor comprehenders, but their advantage shrank when the text was scrambled, suggesting that search is guided by the reader's mental representation of the content and its order (abstract only). This is a useful warning: finding something quickly is partly a *product* of having understood the piece, not an alternative to it. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)

### Searching as learning: a field with reviews, thinner experiments

- Marchionini (2006, *Communications of the ACM*) argues for "exploratory search" that goes beyond predictable fact retrieval; I could open only the one-sentence abstract, so I cite it for the framing and nothing more.
- Vakkari (2016, *J Information Science*) is a literature survey that conceptualises learning as changes in knowledge structures and categorises empirical studies of search and learning (abstract only). Rieh, Collins-Thompson, Hansen & Lee (2016, same journal) is a critical review that proposes "comprehensive search": iterative, reflective, integrative sessions supporting critical and creative learning, not passive reception (abstract only).
- von Hoyer et al. (2022, *Frontiers in Psychology*) builds a model of the learner, interface, retrieval backend and web knowledge. I read the first half of the full text. It reports that direct answers on a results page may lead to "too short and shallow learning episodes" because learners stop before visiting any source, that short search episodes can produce overestimated knowledge, that learners typically fail to use strategies such as note-taking, and that experimentally obtained ground truth linking behaviour to knowledge gain is "very limited". (**Moderate certainty as a review of an immature field · Analogy**)
- Eye-tracking studies disagree. Bhattacharya & Gwizdka (2018, ETRA, conference paper, 30 participants, web search) found participants with larger knowledge gain had greater total reading-sequence length, duration and fixations (arXiv abstract); the other search result describes the same group's earlier claim that higher-gain participants read *less*, which I could not trace. Gritz, Hoppe & Ewerth (2024, ECIR; arXiv version not peer reviewed) found higher-gain learners spent more time reading and processed more words. Both are small, conference-level, web-search studies, and are shown only to illustrate that the field has not settled what reading behaviour predicts learning. (**Contested · Analogy**)

### Does handing over the answer matter? Generative AI evidence (2024–2025)

- Melumad & Yun (2025, *PNAS Nexus*): seven online and laboratory experiments, reported n = 10,462 (the introduction says 10,426, an inconsistency in the paper). Experiment 1 (Prolific, 1,104 analysed) compared ChatGPT with Google for planning a vegetable garden: Google users spent more time searching (743 s vs 585 s) and reported learning more (3.86 vs 3.43 on a rating scale); Experiment 2 held the facts constant using simulated results and found the same direction (3.96 vs 3.71); Experiment 3 (lab, 250 students) compared Google results with Google's AI Overview. Advice written after LLM use was shorter and more homogeneous, and raters (Experiment 4) found it less helpful. Limits: the paper reports F tests rather than effect sizes, outcomes are largely self-report and text features rather than knowledge tests, the task is everyday advice, and the LLM versions differed across experiments. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Stadler, Bannert & Sailer (2024, *Computers in Human Behavior*): 91 university students were randomised to ChatGPT 3.5 or Google to research a socio-scientific issue. The LLM group reported lower cognitive load but produced lower-quality reasoning and argumentation (abstract only). (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; one topic, one session)
- Kreijkes et al. (2025, *Computers & Education*): a pre-registered randomised experiment with 405 students aged 14–15 in England, who studied two passages with an LLM, with note-taking, or both, and were tested on comprehension and retention three days later. Note-taking alone, or with the LLM, beat LLM-only on both, even though most students preferred the LLM and rated it more helpful (abstract only). This also separates *perceived ease* from *delayed retention*. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; teenagers, short passages)

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **No study I found tests find-in-page or semantic passage search in one document against reading alone.** A targeted search found nothing; this is a gap, not a negative result. Everything above is analogy.
- **Populations and materials.** Mostly secondary students and undergraduates reading short constructed passages, or lay adults doing web tasks. Expert adults reading papers in their own field are essentially absent. Prior knowledge plausibly changes the picture: von Hoyer et al. report that learners with more prior knowledge formulate better queries and navigate more efficiently, so experts may use search more productively and lose less.
- **Goal-focusing can be good.** The relevance-instruction results show relevant material learned better with no more time. For a reader whose real goal is "does this paper contain X?", narrowing is the aim and not a defect. The finding becomes a cost only where the reader's goal is understanding the whole.
- **Lookup success is not learning.** The text-availability study shows the gap, but it is an abstract-only read of one adolescent sample.
- **AI-versus-search results compare the wrong thing for Spideryarn.** They contrast a synthesised answer with link lists. Spideryarn's search returns places in the author's text.
- **Self-report and proxy outcomes.** The largest study (Melumad & Yun) leans on self-reported learning and text features.
- **Foraging theory is a model, not a test.** It organises findings; it does not tell us that a better scent display improves comprehension.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Search mode (words, quick, meaning)

How it works today: one box, three matchers. *Words* is find-on-page; *quick* asks a small decision model whether each paragraph "mention[s] or discuss[es]" the query, in about a second; *meaning* asks a larger model for passages with a confidence and one line of reasoning. Every result is "an index into the piece": press it and the page goes there, and the reasoning line is "never the passage's content" ([search.md § Why this is on the augment side](../../project/search.md#why-this-is-on-the-augment-side-of-the-line)). Search can start from a bottom-bar box, `/`, or the command bar, and results show a place bar so each hit is located in the whole piece ([search.md § Where in the article](../../project/search.md#where-in-the-article-on-every-result)).

**Supports.** The design stays on the safer side of the answer-versus-sources evidence: it returns places to read, not a synthesised answer, which the Melumad & Yun and Stadler results suggest matters. The printed confidence and the card that says it is "not a measurement" answer the overconfidence worry that von Hoyer et al. raise about short search episodes. The place bar and spine marks supply scent and show the *shape* of a result, which the foraging vocabulary says readers use to decide whether to enter a patch.

**Suggests a change (proposals only).**
1. Goal-focusing predicts that a reader who only follows hits will remember those paragraphs better and the rest less well. One cheap mitigation to consider is showing, after a search, what share of the piece the reader has visited or read through the hits, using the existing reading-time machinery ([reading-time.md](../../project/reading-time.md)), so the narrowing is visible rather than silent. This is untested.
2. The finding that an integration task beats intratext questions suggests a search result list that also offers "how do these passages relate?" (an integrative prompt) as a way to push from lookup toward connection, perhaps through chat ([chat-from-a-mode.md](../../project/chat-from-a-mode.md)). Untested in this form.

**Untested.** Whether passage-level semantic search changes how carefully people read the rest of a piece, whether a place bar or confidence score reduces the skimming costs seen in Duggan & Payne, and whether experts are harmed at all. These could be studied with Spideryarn's own logs and a small A/B (search available versus not) with a delayed free-recall measure; the measurement risks are the ones in the Kreijkes study (preference diverges from retention).

### Chat tools that search the article

How it works today: chat can run both matchers as tools (`search_article_meaning`, `search_article_words`) and cite passages with chips that jump to the text ([search.md](../../project/search.md), [chat-tools.md](../../project/chat-tools.md)).

**Supports.** Citation chips keep the reader one press from the source, the opposite of the "stop before visiting any source" failure von Hoyer et al. describe.

**Suggests a change.** The LLM-versus-web-search results warn against any chat answer that *replaces* the passage. If chat answers are long and the reader seldom presses a chip, the effect studied by Melumad & Yun could apply. Measuring the chip press-through rate would be a cheap check on whether that happens.

**Untested.** Whether an answer plus citations behaves like a link list (better) or like an LLM summary (worse).

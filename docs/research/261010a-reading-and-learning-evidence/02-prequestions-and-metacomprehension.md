# Questions before reading, questions you write yourself, and knowing what you have understood

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

Three linked questions. Does being asked questions *before* you read help you learn from the text,
and does it help only the content the questions touched? Does getting readers to write their own
questions help? And how well can readers tell, afterwards, what they have and have not understood
(the field calls this *metacomprehension*; the match between your confidence and your actual
performance is *calibration*)? Short answer: prequestions reliably help for the content they ask
about and, in the best current meta-analysis, do essentially nothing for everything else. Teaching
people to ask questions helps comprehension modestly, mostly in school-age work. Readers are
poor at judging their own comprehension (about r = .24 across 94 studies). The best-supported fix is
to make them produce something from memory after a delay (a summary or keywords), and, for
calibration, to show them a standard to compare their answer against.

## The short answer

- Answering questions before reading improves later memory for the *questioned* content: g = 0.66 in a 2025 multilevel meta-analysis. (**High certainty · Indirect**: mostly undergraduates, short passages and videos.)
- It does **not** produce a general benefit for the rest of the text: pooled g = 0.01 for non-questioned content. Individual studies disagree (video helped everything in one, not in another), and a 2023 review calls the untested-content evidence "highly inconsistent". (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Adding feedback (the correct answer) to prequestions gave bigger benefits than prequestions alone in that meta-analysis. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- ChatGPT-written prequestions worked about as well as human-written ones in four experiments (one journal paper, abstract only). (**Low certainty · Indirect**)
- Teaching students to generate questions about what they read improved comprehension: median effect 0.36 on standardised tests, 0.86 on researcher-made tests (1996 review). Evidence is old, school-age-heavy and says little about experts reading papers. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Readers judge their own comprehension poorly: pooled r = 0.24 (95% CI 0.21 to 0.26) between predicted and actual performance, 145 subgroups from 94 studies. (**High certainty · Indirect**: the texts are mostly short expository passages.)
- Writing a summary or keywords *after a delay* (not straight after reading) raises that accuracy; the 28-effect meta-analysis of such interventions found g = 0.46. Self-explanation alone did not help. (**Moderate to High certainty · Indirect**)
- Showing readers a standard to compare their recalled answer to improved their calibration, including on later texts, especially for weaker performers. (**Low to Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

## The evidence

### 1. Prequestions: what they help

*Terms.* A **prequestion** is a question asked before you study the material; a **pretest** is the same idea where you must attempt an answer, usually wrongly, first. Researchers separate **questioned** content (what the questions asked about) from **non-questioned** content (the rest of the passage).

**The founding study.** Richland, Kornell and Kao (2009): five experiments in which undergraduates read an essay about vision. One group answered questions about embedded concepts before reading; another was given longer to read, with the same concepts emphasised in italics or bold so that mere attention could not explain the gap. The question group scored better on the later test in all five experiments, *even when only items they had got wrong on the pretest were analysed* (abstract only; effect sizes not read). Experiment 5 showed the questions without asking for an answer, as a further control for attention. (**High certainty · Indirect**)

**The 2023 review.** Pan and Carpenter (2023, *Educational Psychology Review*) reviewed more than 60 peer-reviewed articles (I read the first third of the full text). For directly questioned content the benefits are consistent across text, video, trivia and word pairs. For untested material presented for study, the evidence is, in their words, "highly inconsistent", with some studies showing no benefit and some showing one. Evidence on inferences and transfer is thin ("in its infancy"). Benefits show up immediately and out to about 48 hours; one classroom study found the advantage no longer significant after a week, another found one at a week. Delayed feedback eliminated the benefit for word pairs. Several classroom studies had no control group, and in one MOOC study pretesting increased dropout. (**High certainty for questioned content · Indirect**)

**The 2025 meta-analysis.** King-Shepard and colleagues (2025, *Educational Psychology Review*; I read the abstract and the page text, not every table): prequestions raised learning of the specific information asked about (g = .66) but found "no evidence of a general learning benefit" for other information (g = .01). Footnote 24 gives 6,704 participants in experimental groups and 5,299 in controls. The specific effect held across variations in learning event, sample and assessment, and conditions with feedback did better than prequestions alone. I could not see the number of studies, confidence intervals or a publication-bias analysis on the page, so treat the precision as unchecked. (**Moderate to High certainty · Indirect**)

**Does it spread to the rest of the text? The split between video studies.** Carpenter and Toftness (2017, abstract only): with a short educational video, prequestions helped both questioned and non-questioned content, and the authors noted that earlier reading studies had found no benefit, or even impairment, for non-questioned content, possibly because readers focus on the questioned parts. Toftness and colleagues (2018, abstract only) tried authentic lecture videos over 20 minutes: the prequestion group beat controls overall and on prequestioned information, but non-questioned performance was no different (Experiment 1) or marginally better (Experiment 2). Benefit did not depend on interest in the material. So the "general benefit" appears only in the shortest, most controlled video, and shrinks as materials become realistic. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

**AI-written prequestions.** Pan and colleagues (2025, *JARMAC*, abstract only): across four experiments, ChatGPT-generated prequestions improved learning of an educational text relative to simply reading, matched human-written questions, and beat studying an AI-generated outline. The benefit appeared on identical and on non-identical test questions, which is encouraging for transfer but not the same as "all content" (abstract only; materials and sample not checked). (**Low certainty · Indirect**: one lab, one paper, undergraduate-style samples, short educational text.)

### 2. Reader-generated questions

Rosenshine, Meister and Chapman (1996, *Review of Educational Research*; abstract only) reviewed intervention studies that taught students to generate questions as a comprehension strategy. Tests used new material. The overall median effect was 0.36 on standardised tests and 0.86 on experimenter-developed tests; the skill-training and reciprocal-teaching approaches did about as well as each other. The abstract does not give ages or text type; the review is of strategy instruction, which is a different thing from asking a reader to generate questions once, unaided. King (1992, *Educational Psychologist*; abstract only) found that a guided questioning strategy (specific question prompts requiring critical thinking, with learner autonomy) improved learning in high school and college lectures compared with other study strategies. Both tell us that *taught, prompted* question generation can help; neither says whether an expert reading a paper gains from writing their own questions, nor whether it beats simply reading the questions a model writes. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

### 3. How well do readers know what they understood?

**Poor, and for a known reason.** Prinz, Golke and Wittwer (2020a, *Educational Research Review*; abstract only): 145 independent subgroups from 94 studies; relative metacomprehension accuracy (can you rank which texts you understood best?) is "only moderate", r = 0.24 [0.21, 0.26], moderated by learner, text, judgement-timing and test-format factors. Glenberg, Sanocki, Epstein and Morris (1987, *JEP: General*; abstract only) found poor calibration with inference, verbatim and idea-recognition tests, and evidence from three experiments that readers judge their familiarity with the *topic* instead of what they learned from *this* text. That is the "illusion of knowing" idea originating in Glenberg, Wilkinson and Epstein (1982), which I could confirm exists in Crossref but could not open. Dunlosky and Lipko (2007, *Current Directions*; abstract only) say that accuracy "has been consistently shown to be quite poor" and then argue that the usual measurement method may itself constrain it. (**High certainty · Indirect**)

**What improves accuracy: a delayed generation task.** Thiede, Anderson and Therriault (2003, *JEP*; abstract only): readers who wrote five keywords per text *after a delay* judged their comprehension more accurately than immediate-keyword and no-keyword groups; better monitoring led to better regulation of study and then to higher test performance. Thiede and colleagues (2005, *JEP: LMC*; abstract only) showed the delay between reading and the keyword task is the critical lag, and that not all delayed tasks help; delayed generation tasks seem to supply diagnostic cues about comprehension. Anderson and Thiede (2008, *Acta Psychologica*; 87 college students; abstract via Europe PMC) found accuracy improved only for delayed summaries, which led readers to base their judgement on gist, not detail. Prinz, Golke and Wittwer (2020b, *Educational Psychology Review*; abstract only): across 28 effect sizes (2,236 participants), "situation-model-approach" interventions (delayed summaries, delayed keywords, delayed diagrams, self-explaining, concept mapping, rereading, setting a test-expectancy) raised relative accuracy by g = 0.46. All but self-explaining were individually significant; setting a comprehension-test expectancy "tended" to be the most effective; comprehension itself improved slightly. Work by Wiley and colleagues moved this into a real course (Wiley et al., 2016, *JEP: Applied*; abstract only) found that test-expectancy plus self-explanation improved relative and absolute accuracy and study strategies in an undergraduate research-methods course. (**Moderate to High certainty · Indirect**)

**Feedback and standards.** Glenberg et al. (1987) found calibration improved when readers took a pretest that gave self-generated feedback. Nederhand, Tabbers and Rikers (2019, *Applied Cognitive Psychology*; 122 first-year psychology students, seven texts with key terms; abstract only): half saw the correct definitions ("standards") after recalling them; they were better calibrated on the same texts and on *new* texts where standards were no longer available, and low performers gained most. A 2018 companion (127 students, *J Cognitive Psychology*; abstract only) found that standards broken into idea units helped more than full definitions over several texts. These are definition-recall tasks on student texts. (**Low to Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

**Experts.** Griffin, Jee and Wiley (2009, *Memory & Cognition*; abstract via Europe PMC): with participants of varying baseball knowledge reading baseball and non-baseball texts, greater knowledge improved *absolute* accuracy (how big is the gap between predicted and actual score) within the domain, but not *relative* accuracy (ranking texts); readers were generally underconfident, less so with more knowledge. This challenges the assumption that experts judge themselves worse than novices, but it also says domain expertise does not fix the ranking problem. (**Low certainty · Direct for the "expert" question, Analogy for materials**: one domain, short texts.)

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Almost nothing here tests expert adults reading real papers.** Prequestion work uses undergraduates, 5 to 30 minute videos, and short essays. Metacomprehension work uses short expository passages, and its dependent measure is usually a multiple-choice or short-answer test taken minutes later.
- **"Does it help the rest of the text" is the central dispute.** The meta-analytic answer (g = .01) is a flat zero, not a small positive. The only positive reading result I have is video, and it shrank with realistic lectures. A product that primes the reader with 5 questions may lift those 5 answers and nothing else.
- **A prequestion benefit is a benefit on the *test of those questions*.** That is the right outcome if what you want to retain is the key points; it is the wrong outcome if you want broad, unprompted understanding or the ability to notice what the author did *not* ask about. No study I found measured whether prequestions change what experts notice in a paper.
- **Delay and feedback matter in ways that are easy to design out.** Delayed feedback killed the word-pair effect; the metacomprehension fix only works when the generation task is *delayed*. A quiz that appears the moment you stop reading may be using the wrong timing.
- **Judgement tasks are a laboratory artefact in part.** Dunlosky and Lipko (2007) argue the standard ranking method constrains accuracy, so r = .24 should not be read as a fact about all comprehension monitoring.
- **Effect sizes I could not read.** For several papers I read only the abstract; Thiede 2003's accuracy figures, Richland 2009's effect sizes and Pan 2025's samples are not reported here.
- **No expert-reader AI feedback study found.** Whether an LLM telling you what you got wrong improves calibration, and whether it breeds over-reliance, has no peer-reviewed test I could find. A 2026 arXiv synthesis on AI use and the competence–confidence gradient (Koch, arXiv:2603.29681) turned up in search; it is a preprint I did not open, so it is kept out of the evidence.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Quiz ([quiz.md](../../project/quiz.md))

*How it works today.* The article asks short-answer questions after the reader has read, in batches of about a dozen ordered easy-to-hard and central-first; the reader answers and the model marks the answer against the *article*, using its own reference answer only as a reference ([§ A reference answer is not an answer key](../../project/quiz.md#a-reference-answer-is-not-an-answer-key)). Answers are kept, and quiz marks are drawn in the prose.

**Supports.** Comparing your answer to a standard is what Nederhand et al. found improves calibration, including on later texts; marking against passages the reader can open is a good form of standard. Easy-first ordering does not conflict with anything here.

**Suggests a change (proposals).** (a) Ask for a confidence rating before the mark is revealed and show a small running "you said you knew it; you didn't" record; no confidence rating appears in the doc, and the calibration literature says readers do not otherwise get this signal. (b) The research favours questions asked *after a delay*; a Quiz offered the moment you finish reading may overstate what you know. A prompt to return later (or a delayed re-offer) is worth testing, not assuming.

**Untested.** Whether LLM marking against a model-written reference gives the same calibration gains as the human-authored standards in the experiments.

### FAQ ([faq.md](../../project/faq.md))

*How it works today.* Up to twelve questions a careful first-time reader would ask, each answered only with the piece's own passages, with a prioritised order by centrality and difficulty.

**Suggests a change (proposal).** FAQ shows answers; prequestions work because you *attempt* an answer first and then discover it. A "guess before you reveal" state on a few top questions would be the prequestion format, with the passage as feedback. But the meta-analysis (g = .01 for non-questioned content) means a pre-reading question set should be understood as steering attention to *those* questions, not as general priming; the three broad "pressure" questions are better suited to this than the low-level ones. **Untested** for expert paper readers.

### Learn ([learn-mode.md](../../project/learn-mode.md))

*How it works today.* The reader types or speaks what they remember of the article, and the model corrects briefly, links the passage and nudges them to remember more.

**Supports.** This is structurally the delayed-summary intervention that raised metacomprehension accuracy (Thiede et al., Anderson and Thiede, Prinz et al. 2020b): produce the gist from memory, then compare. The benefit is tied to delay and to gist, not detail, so Learn is better after a gap than straight after reading, and a prompt framing "what was the argument?" over "what details do you recall?" matches the evidence.

**Untested.** Whether the correction itself or the act of recalling is doing the work for expert readers, and whether the model's corrections are good "standards".

### Skim ([skim.md](../../project/skim.md))

*How it works today.* Three passes over the paper at increasing depth, a route through its quotes.

**Untested.** Nothing in this literature speaks directly to multi-pass skim routes. The closest hint is that a preview works only for what it points at (Pan and Carpenter 2023; King-Shepard 2025): the stops chosen on pass one are the content that will be retained best.

# Does easy look-up stop you learning the word?

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

Greg's worry (2026-10-09): *"maybe the glossary, because it's constantly making it easy for you to look up the answer, means that you're not actually learning it."* The literature on **glosses** (a short definition or translation put beside a word in a text) is large, but almost all of it is about second-language (L2) learners reading simple stories. Short answer: a gloss reliably helps you learn the words it covers, compared with reading with no help, so easy look-up does not by itself stop learning. But nobody has shown it is the *best* way to learn the word. The mechanisms that do have good evidence (producing an answer before seeing it; being tested on it later) suggest that "try first, check second" should beat "just be told". For the expert adult reading a paper in their own field, who is Spideryarn's reader, there is almost no direct evidence at all.

## The short answer

- Glossed reading produces more word learning than unglossed reading, on immediate and delayed tests (45.3% vs 26.6% of target words immediately; 33.4% vs 19.8% later). So a gloss is not a crutch that cancels learning relative to *no help*. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**: L2 learners, mostly short texts.)
- Gloss formats that make you do a small act of choosing (multiple-choice glosses) beat plain translations; in-text glosses and end-of-text glossaries did worst. Effort at the moment of look-up seems to matter more than how easy the look-up is. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Whether glosses help *comprehension* of the text is mixed and weaker than the vocabulary evidence; I could not confirm the headline meta-analytic figure. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- There is no good experiment comparing "gloss always visible" with "gloss on click" with "guess first, then reveal" in adult readers of expository texts. This is the central gap. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Producing an answer before seeing it, even wrongly, improves later memory for the right answer when feedback follows (generation effect, errors-then-feedback, testing effect). These are strong results in general and the main reason to think "guess, then check" would beat "just show". Whether they transfer to term meanings met while reading a paper is untested. (**High certainty · Analogy**)
- Handing a learner the answer while they practise can raise performance during practice and lower it afterwards, unless the help is designed to make them think (one large field experiment on school mathematics with a chatbot). A warning about the shape of the risk, not a result about glossaries. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Repetition matters: glosses plus repeated encounters helped advanced learners more than repetition alone. (**Low certainty · Indirect**)

## The evidence

### Does a gloss help at all?

**Yanagisawa, Webb & Uchihara (2020)**, a meta-regression of 42 studies (N = 3,802 learners, 359 effect sizes), found glossed reading beat unglossed reading on both immediate and delayed vocabulary tests: 45.3% and 33.4% of target words learned with glosses, against 26.6% and 19.8% without. The page I read gave percentages, not Hedges' g or confidence intervals, so I give no standardised effect size. Learning fell from immediate to delayed tests in both conditions. Population: L2 learners at all proficiency levels; materials: mostly short passages; comparator: the same text unglossed. Limits: study designs differ widely, and Boers' (2022) review of the field says conclusions about how well glossing works, and how best to do it, are provisional because of the variety of designs and poor reporting. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

**Hulstijn, Hollander & Greidanus (1996)**: Dutch advanced students of French read a short story with marginal L1 glosses, a bilingual dictionary, or no help; 16 target words appeared once or three times. The abstract says repetition helped incidental learning more with glosses or a dictionary than with no help, and suggests that with dictionaries readers skip words or guess wrongly, weakening the repetition effect. No statistics in the abstract; I did not read the full text (abstract only). (**Low certainty · Indirect**: advanced learners, but fiction, and an L2.) It points to *seeing the answer plus repetition*, not to avoiding the answer.

### Does the format of the gloss matter? Single versus multiple choice

In the meta-regression, **multiple-choice glosses** (several candidate meanings, the reader picks) were the most effective type; in-text glosses and glossaries the least. L1 glosses beat L2 glosses, with no interaction with proficiency. Text, picture and audio glosses did not differ meaningfully. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

**Rott (2005)** is a small think-aloud study: 10 native English speakers learning German, one folk tale, target words repeated three more times after the gloss. It suggests multiple-choice glosses led to "more robust and complete" form-meaning connections because readers repeatedly searched for and evaluated candidate meanings; weaker connections came from linear reading and no motivation to pin down a meaning. Main-idea comprehension was equal across conditions; multiple-choice readers tended to recall more supporting ideas. With N = 10 and qualitative analysis this is **hypothesis-generating only** (**Low certainty · Analogy**; full text read). Rott's introduction reports, of Hulstijn (1992), that multiple-choice glosses beat L2 synonym glosses but L1 translations beat multiple-choice, and that a significant number of participants chose a wrong meaning. So a choice format can plant a wrong meaning. I did not read Hulstijn (1992) itself.

### Always-visible versus click-to-reveal

I found **no clean experiment** isolating this in adult readers. The meta-regression compares gloss *types* (in-text, marginal, glossary, multiple-choice), not on-demand versus automatic. Indirectly, formats that make the reader do something (choose, compare) did better than passive placements, but effort, position and who chooses to look up cannot be separated. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)

### Inferring first, then checking: the involvement-load idea

The **Involvement Load Hypothesis** says retention depends on how much *need*, *search* and *evaluation* a task puts on a word. Its empirical test is **Hulstijn & Laufer (2001)**, *Language Learning* (the lead "Laufer & Hulstijn 2001" is the separate theory paper in *Applied Linguistics*; both titles confirmed via Crossref, only the empirical abstract opened). Two parallel experiments with EFL learners in two countries: ten unfamiliar words learned by (a) reading comprehension, (b) reading plus fill-in-the-blank, (c) composition using the words. Retention was highest for composition, lower for reading plus fill-in, lowest for reading alone (abstract only; no statistics in it). (**Low certainty · Analogy**) A later meta-analysis (Yanagisawa & Webb 2021, 10.1111/lang.12444) tests how well the hypothesis predicts outcomes across studies; I confirmed the title but not its findings.

The specific claim that *inferring* a meaning beats being *given* it is Hulstijn (1992), which I could not read. I treat "infer-first helps retention" as **plausible, not demonstrated here**.

### The general memory results that bear on "try first"

Not about reading, but the strongest evidence on what happens when you attempt before you are told.

- **Generation effect.** Bertsch et al. (2007), meta-analysis of 86 studies (445 effect sizes): material you produce yourself is remembered better than material you only read; average effect 0.40, about 0.4 of a standard deviation. Mostly undergraduates and word lists. (**High certainty · Analogy**; abstract only.)
- **Testing effect.** Rowland (2014), meta-analysis: tests beat restudying for later retention, and recall tests beat recognition tests, which the author reads as support for effortful processing. Abstract only; no figures in it. (**High certainty · Analogy**)
- **Vocabulary-specific testing.** Karpicke & Roediger (2008, *Science*): Swahili-English word pairs; once an item had been recalled, more *restudying* did nothing for delayed recall, but repeated *testing* had a large effect; students' predictions of their own performance did not correlate with actual performance (abstract only). Adults, a laboratory list. (**High certainty · Analogy**)
- **Failing before being told.** Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), six experiments: trying and failing to answer, then seeing the answer, beat reading question and answer together (made-up trivia and weak word associations; abstract only). (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

### Terminology in disciplinary reading

I found no experiment on hyperlinked definitions in adult science or research-paper reading with delayed outcomes. Nagy & Townsend (2012) argue that academic vocabulary is best learned in context with many chances to use the words, but I saw only secondary descriptions, so it is a framing here, not evidence.

### Generative AI

**Bastani et al. (2025, PNAS)**: nearly 1,000 high school maths students; an unrestricted GPT-4 interface raised practice grades by 48% but left students 17% *worse* than controls once access was removed; a version with hints and guardrails raised practice grades by 127% and largely avoided the later harm (abstract only). Different domain, age and task; it shows the *shape* of the worry (support in practice, cost later), not that glossaries cause it. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**) Preprints and coined slogans (including Kosmyna et al. 2025) are kept out of the evidence.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Wrong comparator.** Nearly all gloss studies compare a gloss with *no help*, where the reader often cannot work the word out. That shows look-up beats ignorance, not that it beats a few seconds of thinking first.
- **Wrong reader.** L2 learners meeting words they have never seen are not experts meeting a term of art. The expertise-reversal literature (Kalyuga et al. 2003; I confirmed only the title) says scaffolds that help novices can stop helping experts; that could cut either way for a glossary.
- **Short delays.** Most tests come minutes to days after reading; Greg's worry is about weeks later.
- **Selection.** In click-to-reveal designs, readers who look up more differ from those who do not.
- **No single story.** Comprehension effects are mixed, and the field review itself calls conclusions provisional.
- **A choice format can mislead**, as above.
- **Unconfirmed:** Abraham (2008) numbers and Yun (2011) (see Not verified).

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Glossary mode and the underlined terms

How it works today: [glossary.md](../../project/glossary.md) says terms the piece uses non-obviously are underlined in the prose in every mode ("The underline is always there"), and pointing at one shows a hover card (`ProseHoverCard.tsx`) without opening the band. The explanation "still arrives only when the reader points at something"; the model does not push it into the prose. So it is **reader-initiated, on demand, always available**, not an always-visible gloss.

- **Supports.** On-demand look-up beside the text is the shape of the glossed condition that beat unglossed reading. Not pushing explanations at the reader leaves room to infer first. Definitions drawn from the piece itself fit context-based learning (as a framing).
- **Suggests a change (a proposal, not a finding).** Nothing here shows easy look-up hurts, but the memory results suggest a cheap experiment: an optional "what do you think this means?" step before the card reveals the entry, or a hide-definition setting. Multiple-choice glosses are the nearest direct support, with the warning that wrong options can mislead.
- **Untested.** Whether a hover card is easier to forget than a marginal gloss; whether expert readers need the cue; whether repeated appearances across a long piece (Hulstijn et al. 1996) do the fixing.

### Maths (TeX drawn as maths)

How it works today: [maths.md](../../project/maths.md) renders delimited TeX in the prose as maths. It is display, not explanation, so the gloss literature does not apply. **Untested.**

## Not verified

- **Yun (2011)**: a search suggests a University of Kansas PhD dissertation, not a CALL article as the lead had it; not opened; not used.
- **Hulstijn (1992)** chapter: record opened (open access at dare.uva.nl), text not read. Its findings appear only as reported by Rott (2005).
- **Yanagisawa & Webb (2021)**: title and DOI via Semantic Scholar; no abstract or findings.
- **Chun & Plass (1996)**, **Mondria & Wit-de Boer (1989)** (a Dutch study that search snippets say found no positive correlation between guessability and retention), **Nagy & Townsend (2012)**, **Fang (2006)**: seen only in search results or not found; not load-bearing.

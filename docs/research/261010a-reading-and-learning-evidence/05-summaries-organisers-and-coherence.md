# Summaries, advance organisers and text coherence

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: does reading a summary before the full text help you understand it, does a summary
work as a substitute for reading, and does it matter how "easy" the text or summary has been made?
The short answer is that the best-known idea, the *advance organiser* (a short framing passage read
first), probably helps on average, but mostly in studies of students on short school or lab
passages, and its benefit depends on what the reader already knows. The warning that matters most
for Spideryarn is that help which suits a novice (explicit, fully connected, gap-free text) can lose
its value, or even backfire, for someone who knows the field, which describes most of Spideryarn's
readers. No study found here tests the exact thing Spideryarn does: an AI summary beside a long
article that an informed adult is also reading.

## The short answer

- Advance organisers had a positive average effect on learning and retention in the one broad
  meta-analysis located (135 studies, 1980), but that is old and I saw only its abstract. The
  earlier literature was read as inconclusive or negative, and Mayer's reanalysis says organisers
  work only "if used in appropriate situations and measured properly". (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Instructional help that suits novices tends to fade or reverse for experts. A 2025 meta-analysis
  (60 experiments) found high-assistance instruction helped low-knowledge learners (d = +0.51) and
  low-assistance instruction helped high-knowledge learners (d = -0.43), though mostly in
  instructional-design tasks, not in reading research papers. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Whether a more coherent, explicit text helps or hurts a knowledgeable reader is **contested**.
  Some studies found knowledgeable readers learned more, on inference-type measures, from a *less*
  coherent text. A later study of 143 college students found the opposite for skilled readers, and
  one of the original findings failed to replicate within its own paper. (**Contested · Indirect**)
- Repairing a text's causal links helped both stronger and weaker readers, but only when the
  original text was difficult. (**Low certainty · Indirect**)
- In two second-language studies (483 and 180 participants), elaborating a text (adding explanation)
  did at least as well as simplifying it. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Randomised studies of generative AI used in place of searching, reading or practising find less
  effort and often thinner reasoning or retention (91 students; 405 pupils aged 14 to 15; seven
  experiments with mostly self-reported "learning"). None tests an AI summary read alongside the full
  text. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Whether a summary can replace the full text for remembering main points is **not settled here**.
  The classic study (Reder and Anderson 1980) could not be read; see "Not verified".
  (**Low certainty · Analogy**)

## The evidence

### 1. Advance organisers: framing before the text

An advance organiser, in David Ausubel's 1960 sense, is a short passage of general, relevant
concepts given *before* unfamiliar material, meant to give the new detail something to attach to.
Ausubel's abstract states the hypothesis that "the learning and retention of unfamiliar but verbal
material can be facilitated by the advance introduction of relevant subsuming concepts" (Ausubel
1960, abstract only; I did not see his results).

- **Meta-analysis.** Luiten, Ames and Ackerson (1980) examined 135 published and unpublished studies
  and concluded that advance organisers "were shown to have a facilitative effect on both learning
  and retention", looking at grade level, subject, presentation mode and ability as possible
  moderators (abstract only; no effect sizes seen). **Moderate certainty · Indirect.** I stop short
  of "High" because it is 46 years old, includes unpublished work, rests on school and college
  samples with short passages, and I could not check the moderator results.
- **The earlier verdict was the opposite.** Mayer (1979) opens by quoting a previous review: "Advance
  organizers, as presently constructed, do not facilitate learning". He argues that review was weak
  on theory, on measuring the right outcomes and on experimental control. His nine tests, he
  reports, favour an "assimilation encoding" account and show that organisers "can influence the
  outcome of learning if used in appropriate situations and measured properly" (abstract only). The
  honest summary is "helps under conditions", not "helps".
- **Previews help weaker readers of exposition too.** McCormick (1989): 76 fifth-grade readers, more
  and less skilled, scored significantly higher on questions about social-studies text when a
  written preview came first (abstract only). Children, one study. **Low certainty · Analogy.**
- **Headings are a cheap cousin.** An experiment with 51 university students ("Caption it!", 2023)
  found headings helped comprehension and made students' judgments of their own comprehension more
  accurate; the effects on memory were not significant (abstract only). One small study. **Low
  certainty · Indirect.** It is the one result here that speaks to *calibration* (knowing what you
  know) rather than recall.

### 2. Help that fits novices and not experts

The *expertise reversal effect* is the finding that a support which helps beginners can stop helping
(or hurt) people who already know the area, partly because it makes them process what they could have
supplied themselves.

- Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters and Brod (2025) meta-analysed 60 experimental studies (176 effect
  sizes, N = 5,924, PRISMA-reported). Low-prior-knowledge learners benefited more from high
  assistance (d = 0.505) and high-prior-knowledge learners from low assistance (d = -0.428). The
  pattern varied with type of assessment, educational level and domain, was less clear for younger
  students and for fields such as the humanities and language learning, and was asymmetrical:
  adding help for novices helps more than withholding it from experts costs (abstract only).
  **Moderate certainty · Indirect.** The studies are mostly about instructional design (worked
  examples, diagrams, guidance), not summaries of articles, and I could not see which moderators are
  specific to reading.

### 3. Text coherence and prior knowledge: the "reverse cohesion" effect

*Coherence* is how well a text spells out how its ideas connect; *cohesion* is the surface signals
(repeated terms, connectives). The proposal is that gaps force a knowledgeable reader to infer the
links, and that this effort builds a deeper mental model of the situation described (a "situation
model"), as opposed to memory for the wording (the "text base").

- McNamara, Kintsch, Songer and Kintsch (1996, *Cognition and Instruction*). Experiment 1: junior
  high pupils, a biology text in three versions; advantages for globally coherent and for more
  explanatory text. Experiment 2 crossed local and global coherence with background knowledge in four
  versions: low-knowledge readers benefited from a coherent text, "whereas high-knowledge readers
  benefit from a minimally coherent text", and only on the measures that tap the situation model
  (inference and problem-solving questions, sorting), not on free recall (abstract only).
- McNamara and Kintsch (1996, *Discourse Processes*): two experiments with history texts.
  Experiment 1 found a high-coherence advantage on recall and multiple choice, while high-knowledge
  readers did better on a keyword-sorting task after the low-coherence text. In Experiment 2 the
  sorting result "failed to replicate", though high-knowledge readers did better on open-ended
  questions after the low-coherence text, with little effect of a one-week delay (abstract only).
  The same paper contains both support and a failed replication.
- O'Reilly and McNamara (2007, *Discourse Processes*): 143 college students read a high- or
  low-cohesion text. The low-cohesion advantage appeared only for *less-skilled* readers with high
  knowledge; skilled readers with high knowledge did better with the high-cohesion text. The
  cohesion-by-knowledge interaction appeared only on text-based questions, and for low-knowledge
  readers high cohesion helped bridging-inference questions, not text-based ones (abstract, via the
  ERIC record). The authors conclude the relationship is more complex than the reverse cohesion
  effect suggests.
- Linderholm and colleagues (2000): history texts whose causal structure was repaired (events in
  time order, goals made explicit, coherence breaks explained) helped both more- and less-skilled
  readers, but only for the difficult text, not the easy one (abstract only).

Read together: the reverse cohesion effect shows up in some designs but is fragile, depends on
reading skill as well as topic knowledge, appears on deeper measures and not on recall, and has not
been shown for expert adults reading research papers. **Contested · Indirect.**

### 4. Simplified versus elaborated text

Both studies are on second-language readers, so they are **Analogy** for Spideryarn's readers, but
they test the exact choice between "cut the difficulty" and "add the explanation".

- Yano, Long and Ross (1994): 483 Japanese college students read 13 passages in a native baseline,
  simplified or elaborated form. Comprehension (30 multiple-choice items) was highest after the
  simplified version, "but not significantly different" from the elaborated one; the type of
  modification interacted with question type (abstract only).
- Oh (2001): 180 Korean high-school students at two proficiency levels, six passages, 18 items. The
  author concludes that elaboration is "at least equally successful as—if not more successful
  than—simplification", while retaining more native-like language (abstract only; this is the
  author's reading of ANOVA results I could not see).
- **Low certainty · Analogy.** What transfers is a design principle, not an effect size:
  elaboration keeps the original vocabulary in view; simplification removes it.

### 5. Generative AI instead of searching, reading or practising

None of these puts a summary next to the full article, so they speak to the risk of *substitution*,
not to Spideryarn's design.

- Melumad and Yun (2025, *PNAS Nexus*): seven experiments, including 1,104 and 1,979 online
  participants and 250 lab participants, comparing LLM synthesis with web search on everyday topics
  (growing vegetables, a healthier lifestyle). LLM groups spent less time, reported learning less
  that was new, and wrote shorter advice with fewer facts; independent raters judged that advice
  less helpful and informative (p < .001 in the main comparisons). One experiment's learning
  difference was only marginal (p = .086). The "shallower knowledge" claim rests on self-report and
  text-feature proxies, not a knowledge test, and the authors say standardised tests might favour
  LLMs and specialised domains may differ (full text, results; I did not read the discussion).
  **Low-to-moderate certainty · Analogy.**
- Stadler, Bannert and Sailer (2024): 91 university students randomly assigned to ChatGPT-3.5 or
  Google to research one socio-scientific question. LLM users reported lower cognitive load and wrote
  lower-quality reasoning and argumentation; diversity of recommendations did not differ (abstract
  only). One topic, one short session. **Low certainty · Analogy.**
- Kreijkes and colleagues (2025): pre-registered, 405 pupils aged 14 to 15, two texts, tested three
  days later. Note-taking, alone or with an LLM, gave better comprehension and retention than the LLM
  alone, although pupils preferred the LLM (read via a summarised record of the abstract; I could
  not open the paper). **Low-to-moderate certainty · Analogy** (adolescents, school texts).
- Bastani and colleagues (2025, *PNAS*): nearly 1,000 high-school maths pupils; unguarded GPT-4
  access improved practice performance but, once removed, left pupils 17% worse than those who never
  had it (abstract only). Not reading, but a clear case where improvement in the moment hid a loss in
  learning. **Moderate certainty · Analogy.**
- A **preprint** (Sim, Eiger and Kohno, arXiv 2609.28820; not load-bearing) reports that AI
  summaries of a *video* often omitted the central event and that a misleading summary distorted
  later memory. It concerns video, not text; the sample size was not on the page I read.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **No direct test.** I found no experiment where adult experts read an AI summary before,
  alongside or after a long article and are tested on delayed understanding.
- **Old and small.** Most organiser and coherence work is from 1960 to 2007, with school or
  undergraduate samples, short constructed passages and mostly immediate tests. The 2025
  meta-analysis is largely about instruction, not reading.
- **The reverse cohesion effect is shaky.** A failed replication sits inside McNamara and Kintsch
  (1996), and O'Reilly and McNamara (2007) found it did not hold for skilled readers.
- **Measure matters.** Where an expert benefit from gaps is found, it is on inference and
  open-ended questions, not recall. A study that only tested recall would call the gap-filled text
  worse.
- **Self-report is not depth.** Melumad and Yun's "shallower knowledge" is inferred mostly from
  reports and proxies; the page also shows inconsistencies (total N given as 10,426 and 10,462; one
  condition's model named as both GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o).
- **Contrary to our caution.** Previews can help weaker readers, and a summary replacing reading may
  not be worse on every outcome (see "Not verified").

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Summary: Brief and Fuller

How it works today ([summaries.md](../../project/summaries.md)): a mode beside the prose with three
views, Brief, Fuller and Thread. Each paragraph links to the passages it rests on, and it is written
for someone who has not read the piece (see the
[2026-10-05 research](../261005c-what-makes-a-longer-summary-followable-by-someone-who-has-not-read-the-piece.md)).
The doc's own "What is still open" says: "No evidence it helps."

- **Supports** the idea in principle. An orienting passage ahead of the detail is the
  advance-organiser logic, and Luiten et al. and Mayer give conditional support. Linking each
  paragraph to its passages keeps the reader pointed at the text, which is in the spirit of every
  finding that substitution risks shallow knowledge. (Moderate · Indirect)
- **Suggests a change (proposal).** Expertise reversal and the coherence findings imply a gap-free
  summary may help most the reader who knows least about this piece, and less (or oddly) the one who
  knows the field. The reader's profile rule is to "assume the background they claim", a
  self-description, whereas these effects depend on actual knowledge of the topic. Worth
  considering: keep the summary a choice rather than a default (it already is a press), and trial,
  for expert readers, a variant that asks for their own one-line gist before showing the Brief,
  since the studies locate the gain in effortful inference. A hypothesis, not a finding.
- **Untested:** a summary *after* reading as a check against one's own gist; delayed retention or
  calibration on long articles. No study located.

### Ideas ("what you need to bring")

How it works today ([ideas.md](../../project/ideas.md)): it lists "the ideas an article leans on
without stating" and the ones it puts forward, judged against the reader's profile.

- **Supports.** It is closer to Ausubel's actual organiser (supplying missing background concepts)
  than a summary is, and the expertise-reversal results favour tailoring it to what the reader
  already knows, which the profile stamp does. (Moderate · Indirect)
- **Untested:** whether such a list improves understanding of the piece itself.

### Summary Thread

How it works today ([tweets.md](../../project/tweets.md)): the article as a numbered thread, each
post linked to its source passages, written on arrival.

- **Untested.** I found no study of thread-formatted summaries; it inherits the organiser evidence
  only loosely. Keeping the links to source passages fits the substitution worries above.

### Link hover summaries

How it works today ([links.md](../../project/links.md)): hover cards on the article's own links,
with a streamed summary relating the destination to the piece.

- **Untested.** Nothing found on short hover summaries and later understanding. They preview a
  different text from the one being read, so the organiser evidence is a weak analogy at best.

### The fidelity guard

How it works today ([summaries.md](../../project/summaries.md), "The fidelity guard"): each
paragraph is checked against the passages it cites.

- **Supports**, weakly: the only evidence located on summary errors is a video preprint showing a
  misleading summary can distort memory, so guarding accuracy is a sound bet, not tested on text.

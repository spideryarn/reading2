# Tutoring, scaffolding and the right amount of difficulty

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: what makes one-to-one tutoring work, how much does it really add, and when does help (scaffolding), difficulty, or an "easier" version of the material stop helping a particular reader? The short answer: tutoring helps a lot, but about 0.8 standard deviations rather than Bloom's 2, and most of the benefit seems to come from small steps with feedback rather than from a human being. Support that fades, struggle before instruction, and "just-right" difficulty each have real evidence, but nearly all of it is from children or students doing maths and science problems, not from adults reading papers. The best-supported principle for Spideryarn's readers is the least glamorous one: help that suits a novice gets in an expert's way, so the level of help should follow the reader. Whether pitching *text* at an easier or harder level suits the reader is far less well tested than the tutoring literature suggests.

## The short answer

- Human one-to-one tutoring beats no tutoring by roughly 0.8 SD, not Bloom's 2 SD; step-by-step computer tutors come close to human tutors (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**).
- Across dozens of randomised school tutoring experiments the pooled effect is about 0.29 SD, larger with teachers or paraprofessionals as tutors, frequent sessions and school-hours delivery (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**).
- Computer-based scaffolding raises outcomes (g = 0.46 over 144 STEM studies), but the same meta-analysis found the effect did not depend on whether the scaffolding was changed over time, so "fade it" has thinner support than its fame suggests (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**).
- The zone of proximal development is a framing, not a tested quantity. What is testable is "contingent, fading, handed-over help", and the review of that found few effectiveness studies (**Low certainty · Analogy**).
- Guidance that helps novices becomes redundant or harmful for more knowledgeable learners (expertise reversal): many experiments plus a 2025 meta-analysis I could not open; mostly technical domains and a handful of reading and hypertext studies (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**).
- Difficulty helps long-term learning only if the learner can overcome it; difficulty they cannot overcome is simply undesirable (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**).
- Attempting a problem before being taught (productive failure) beats teaching first, g = 0.36, but the evidence is school maths and science, older students gain more, and younger learners and general skills favoured teaching first (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; whether the effect is causal is **Contested**).
- The "85% rule" (aim for about 15% errors) is derived for simple machine-learning classifiers and tested on models; it is not evidence about adults reading (**Low certainty · Analogy**).
- Whether to pitch a text easier or harder to suit the reader: I found no peer-reviewed test with expert adults reading papers. The classic claim that high-knowledge readers learn more from less explicit text could not be opened, and one small adult study did not test it (**Low certainty · Indirect**).

## The evidence

### How big is the tutoring effect?

Bloom (1984) is famous for "the 2 sigma problem": mastery-taught students tutored one-to-one did two standard deviations better than classroom students. I verified only the paper's title and journal (Crossref), not its numbers.

VanLehn (2011), a review of studies from 1975 to 2010, is the standard correction. I could not open the paper; the figures below come from VanLehn's own slide deck summarising it (labelled as such: a secondary source by the same author). Against no tutoring: human tutors d = 0.79 (10 effects, 80% of them reliable), step-based computer tutors 0.76 (28 effects), sub-step-based 0.40 (26), answer-based 0.31 (165). Head to head, human versus step-based computer tutoring was 0.21 (10 effects, 30% reliable), and human versus sub-step-based was -0.12 (5 effects), i.e. no human advantage. So the interaction has to be fine-grained enough to give feedback on each step, and beyond that a human adds little. Population: students; tasks: mostly well-defined STEM problems (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**).

Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan (2023) pooled dozens of randomised PreK-12 tutoring experiments: 0.288 SD (SE 0.029). Larger when tutors were teachers or paraprofessionals, for earlier grades, at least three days a week, and in school hours (abstract only) (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; children, school subjects).

Takeaway: the realistic target is "a good small-step tutor", not Bloom's number.

### Scaffolding and fading

The word comes from Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), a study of tutoring young children. I could confirm only the bibliographic record, not the text (see Not verified).

van de Pol, Volman and Beishuizen (2010) reviewed a decade of teacher-student scaffolding research. They identify three defining features: contingency (help fitted to the learner's current state), fading, and transfer of responsibility. They found only a small number of effectiveness studies, which suggested scaffolding works, and name measurement as the main obstacle (abstract only) (**Low certainty · Analogy**: mainly classroom teaching).

Belland et al. (2017) meta-analysed 144 experimental studies (333 outcomes) of computer-based scaffolding in STEM, primary school to adult: g = 0.46 on cognitive outcomes, positive across age groups, strongest for adults and at the principles level of assessment. The effect did not depend on context-specificity or on whether, or how, the scaffolding was changed over time (abstract only) (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; problem-centred STEM, not reading). The last point is the awkward one: fading is central to scaffolding theory, yet this large synthesis did not detect that it mattered. I did not see its publication-bias analysis.

### The zone of proximal development

The ZPD (the gap between what a learner does alone and what they can do with help) is an idea about where to pitch help. No study I opened tests it as a quantity. The testable content is contingency, and the van de Pol review is the nearest summary: encouraging but thin. Treat it as a framing (**Low certainty · Analogy**).

### Help that helps novices hurts experts

Kalyuga (2007), a narrative review (not a meta-analysis), collects experiments in which techniques effective for novices, such as worked examples, integrated explanations and structured hypertext, lose their advantage or reverse for learners with more prior knowledge, and links the pattern to older aptitude-treatment-interaction research. Reading-related examples in it, as reported there: university students on productivity-management text, where structured hypertext beat linear text for the less knowledgeable while the knowledgeable did (non-significantly) better with linear text (Calisir and Gurel, 2003); and low-knowledge readers learning more from a hierarchical map while high-knowledge readers showed no significant difference (Potelle and Rouet, 2003). The review is candid that most work is in well-defined technical domains, that the range of methods tested is narrow, and that poorly defined domains remain to be studied. It also describes fast diagnosis of expertise: a rapid "verify these statements about a sentence" test correlated with conventional reading-comprehension measures at a fraction of the testing time (3.7 times shorter, from Kalyuga 2006) and was used to choose the level of guidance (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**).

A 2025 meta-analysis exists (Tetzlaff et al.). I could verify title and authors but not read its results, so nothing here rests on it.

### Difficulty and its limits

Soderstrom and Bjork (2015) review the learning-versus-performance distinction: conditions that produce more errors during practice often produce more durable learning, and learners misjudge this because ease feels like learning. They state the limit explicitly: when a difficulty cannot be overcome by the learner, for example when nothing can be retrieved, it becomes undesirable, and identifying when difficulties help remains an open challenge (full text read) (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; mostly word lists, motor skills, short materials). Spideryarn's hint-ladder rule is this limit put into practice.

### Struggle before instruction

Sinha and Kapur (2021) meta-analysed 53 studies (166 comparisons) of problem solving first and instruction second versus the reverse: Hedges' g = 0.36 (95% CI 0.20 to 0.51), and 0.37 to 0.58 when designs followed productive-failure principles closely. Larger for older students, longer interventions and quasi-experimental designs; younger learners (grades 2 to 5) and domain-general skills favoured instruction first. The authors' publication-bias-adjusted estimate is g = 0.87, which I would read with suspicion: a correction that raises an effect is unusual (abstract only) (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; causality **Contested**). Population: students; tasks: maths and science problems. Reading research has no equivalent.

### Is there an optimal error rate?

Wilson et al. (2019) derive that for gradient-descent learners on binary classification, learning is fastest when training error is about 15.87% (accuracy about 85%), and test this on artificial and biologically plausible neural networks. The abstract frames it as relevant to training humans, animals and machines, but the evidence I read is model-based (abstract only) (**Low certainty · Analogy**). It is a suggestive number, not a target for a reader answering questions about a paper.

### Does pitching text easier or harder help?

Almost nothing I could open tests this for expert adults reading papers.

- Schurer, Opitz and Schubert (2020): 90 German university students (30 law students) read a copyright-law hypertext in a high- or low-cohesion version. The low-cohesion text produced more mind wandering and lower comprehension; law students comprehended more overall; no knowledge-by-cohesion interaction was reported, so the study cannot confirm that experts do better with harder text. One text, small groups, and the paper has inconsistencies between abstract and results (**Low certainty · Indirect**).
- The classic "reverse coherence" finding (McNamara et al. 1996: low-knowledge readers gain from a coherent text, high-knowledge readers from a less coherent one) I saw only through search summaries, so it sits under Not verified.

### Generative AI tutors (2025)

Bastani et al. (2025, *PNAS*), nearly 1,000 high-school maths students: access to a ChatGPT-like tutor raised practice performance (grades up 48%), but once access was removed those students did 17% worse than students who never had it. A version with learning-protecting prompts raised practice performance by 127% and largely avoided the harm (abstract only) (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**). Kestin et al. (2025, *Scientific Reports*), an RCT in a Harvard physics course, reports students learned more in less time with a carefully designed AI tutor than in an in-class active-learning session (abstract only; sample size and effect sizes not read) (**Low certainty · Analogy**; one course, one institution, novelty effects possible).

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- Nearly every effect above comes from children or undergraduates on maths, physics or short passages; transfer to an expert reading a paper in their own field is an assumption.
- Fading, the core of scaffolding theory, was not detected as a moderator in the largest computer-scaffolding meta-analysis.
- Productive failure's benefit is larger in quasi-experiments, which are more prone to bias, and the bias-adjusted estimate moved up rather than down.
- Human tutoring's edge over a good step-by-step computer tutor was small (0.21). That is encouraging for an AI tutor, and also a warning: a chatty tutor that does not respond to each step sits in the weaker answer-based category.
- The AI-tutor trials are early, and I read only their abstracts.
- Expertise reversal could mean our default is too generous to experts; see below.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Tutorial (Learn mode)

How it works: each turn is a brief reaction, one small cited piece of the article, and one task, climbing as the reader succeeds and stepping down otherwise; an expert with a narrow reason for reading is taken straight to the argument ([learn-mode.md](../../project/learn-mode.md), § Tutorial; reasoning in [learning-vision.md](../../project/learning-vision.md)).

**Supports**: small steps with feedback is where the tutoring evidence puts the benefit (VanLehn); the turn shape, contingent task difficulty and stepping down are what scaffolding means in van de Pol; Bastani's result supports the design choice never to just hand over the answer.
**Suggests a change** (proposal): Kalyuga's rapid test (verify a few statements) is a tested way to choose the level of guidance, whereas the mode currently infers level from the profile and the reader's first reply. A one-question diagnostic at the start could be tried.
**Untested**: whether any of this helps expert adults reading papers, and whether the 100-word turn target beats longer turns.

### Recall hint ladder

How it works: a cue, a sharper cue, then tell; never more than two rungs ([learn-mode.md](../../project/learn-mode.md), § Recall; earlier research in [261002c](../261002c-recall-and-tutorial-pedagogy-for-remember-mode.md)).

**Supports**: Soderstrom and Bjork's limit, that a difficulty the learner cannot overcome stops being desirable, is the rationale for "tell after two rungs".
**Suggests a change**: none. The 85% rule is too far from this task to justify a numeric target for hit rate.
**Untested**: the right number of rungs for adult readers.

### Explore

How it works: one move a turn prompts the reader's own thinking about the piece ([learn-mode.md](../../project/learn-mode.md), § Explore).

**Supports**: productive failure suggests that having the reader attempt a prediction or position before the model says more could help older learners, who gained most in the meta-analysis.
**Untested**: all of it for reading; the productive-failure evidence is problem solving.

### Brief and Fuller summaries

How it works: two levels of plain-words summary; the middle Simple level was removed on 2026-10-04 ([summaries.md](../../project/summaries.md), § Two levels).

**Supports**: two reader-chosen levels fit expertise reversal, because the reader picks the amount of help.
**Suggests a change**: nothing I found says a system-chosen easier or harder text version improves expert comprehension, so leave the choice with the reader rather than inferring it.
**Untested**: whether reading a summary first changes later reading of the full piece; that is a question for another file in this library.

# Headings, outlines, structure and signalling

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: do headings, overviews, tables of contents and other "signals" help people remember and understand what they read, who do they help, and does teaching readers to look for a text's structure help too? The short answer: signals reliably help readers remember *the structure and the signalled content*, mostly at the cost of unsignalled detail; the best evidence is from undergraduates and a few adult-reader studies, not from experts reading papers in their own field. Teaching structure awareness helps, but the strong meta-analytic evidence is from school pupils. Nothing I could open tests machine-generated headings or outlines against author headings.

## The short answer

- Headings, overviews and summaries make readers remember a text's *topics and their organisation* better, and make recall better organised around those topics (Lorch, Lorch & Inman 1993, college students; abstract only). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Signals mainly help the content they point at. Unsignalled content is not helped and, when only some topics are signalled, is recalled *worse* than in a text with no signals (Lorch & Lorch 1996, Exp. 3, abstract only). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Signals can trade detail for breadth: signalled readers recalled more topics but less about each (Lorch et al. 1993, Exp. 2). (**Low–Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Signals work by changing *how* people read (encoding), not just by helping retrieval (Lorch & Lorch 1995; Meyer & Poon 2001; both abstract only). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Among competent adult readers, only some use headings to organise their reading; those "topic structure processors" had larger working memory and wrote the most accurate summaries (Hyönä, Lorch & Kaakinen 2002; correlational, abstract only). (**Low certainty · Indirect**)
- Signals help most when prior knowledge is low; for high-knowledge readers the benefit shrinks or reverses. The best evidence is from text-and-picture signalling, so this is an analogy for headings in prose (Richter et al. 2016; Alpizar et al. 2020; a text-graph experiment, Applied Cognitive Psychology 2020). (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Teaching text-structure strategies improves immediate comprehension, but effects are weaker against strong comparisons, far transfer is small, and in upper-primary pupils the gain vanished at delayed tests (Hebert et al. 2016; Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. 2020). Adult evidence is thin: one 9-hour training study with younger and older adults (Meyer & Poon 2001). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- No peer-reviewed study I could open compares model-generated headings or outlines with author headings. (**Untested**)

## The evidence

### Do signals improve memory?

Lorch (1989, *Educational Psychology Review*) is the standard review of "text-signalling devices" (titles, headings, previews, overviews, summaries, typographic cues). I opened only the bibliographic record, not the text, so I rely on later primary studies for claims (the review's conclusions are in Not verified).

Primary studies, all by the Kentucky group, all with college students reading expository texts, all opened at abstract level only:

| Study | Design | Outcome | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lorch, Lorch & Inman 1993, *J Educ Psych* 85 | 2 experiments; signals (headings, overviews, summaries) vs none; N not available in abstract | Topic recall; content recall | Signals gave better memory for topics and organisation; recall better organised by topic; more topics recalled, less per topic |
| Lorch & Lorch 1995, *J Educ Psych* 87 | Signals (headings, overviews or summaries) vs none; recall with or without topic cues | Free and cued recall | Cues helped recall much more in signalled conditions: consistent with signals changing processing strategy |
| Lorch & Lorch 1996, *J Educ Psych* 88 | 3 experiments | Free recall | Simple topic structure: no effect. Complex structure: changed distribution and organisation of recall. Half-signalled text: unsignalled half recalled worse than in an unsignalled text |
| Lorch, Lemarié & Chen 2013, *Psicología Educativa* 19 | Headings vs preview sentences vs none; college students | Subtopic memory; fact memory; outlines | Headings beat previews for subtopic memory; same for facts; both beat none for outlines |

No effect sizes or delays were available from the abstracts, and the delays were presumably immediate or minutes. Two points matter: signalling had *no* effect when the text was structurally simple (1996, Exp. 1), and the benefit is for structure memory more than for detail.

Meyer, Brandt & Bluth (1980, *Reading Research Quarterly*; abstract only) studied ninth-graders on well-organised problem/solution and comparison texts. Good comprehenders followed the text's top-level structure whether or not it was signalled; comprehension underachievers did so only when signals were present. The authors call the structure strategy "a particularly effective retrieval mnemonic". This is the origin of the idea that structure-aware readers do better and that signals can substitute for missing structure awareness. Adolescents, not adults.

### Who benefits? Skill, knowledge, and reading style

- Hyönä, Lorch & Kaakinen (2002, *J Educ Psych* 94; abstract only): eye fixations of adults reading multi-topic expository texts clustered into four strategies (fast linear, slow linear, non-selective reviewers, topic-structure processors). Only the last group paid close attention to headings; they had the largest working-memory capacity and wrote the most accurate summaries. Correlational and small-sample, so it cannot say whether attending to headings *causes* better summaries. It does show that even competent adults differ in whether they use structure cues. Related: Hyönä & Lorch 2004 (eye fixations and topic headings; bibliographic record only).
- Prior knowledge. The multimedia literature finds signalling helps mainly low-prior-knowledge learners. Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016, *Educ Res Rev* 17; abstract only): 27 studies, 45 comparisons, N = 2,464; signalled text-picture material gave a small-to-medium benefit on transfer and comprehension, r = 0.17, 95% CI [.11, .22], moderated by prior knowledge and largest for novices. Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020, *ETR&D* 68; abstract only): 29 studies, 44 effects, N = 2,726; d = .38; larger in higher-quality studies and those controlling for prior knowledge. A text-graph experiment (N = 101) found an expertise-reversal pattern: signals helped low-knowledge students but not high-knowledge ones (Applied Cognitive Psychology 2020; abstract only). These signals are mostly colour or arrows linking text and picture. They are an analogy for headings in prose, not a test of them.
- Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018, *Educ Res Rev* 23) is a further signalling meta-analysis; I confirmed the citation but could not read its results.

### Do headings help comprehension and judging what you know?

Caption it! (*Applied Cognitive Psychology* 2023; 51 university students; abstract only): headings improved comprehension and the accuracy of readers' judgments of their comprehension, but not memory or the accuracy of memory judgments, and changed cognitive load. One small experiment, direction of the load effect not stated in the abstract. (**Low certainty · Indirect**) It is useful because it is one of the few studies that tests *calibration* (knowing what you know) alongside recall.

### Does teaching structure awareness help?

- Hebert, Bohaty, Nelson & Brown (2016, *J Educ Psych* 108; abstract and summary via OpenAlex): 45 studies, Grades 2–12. Text-structure instruction improved expository comprehension; effects were smaller against stronger comparison groups; teaching more structures and adding writing were associated with bigger effects. It was effective at maintenance, near transfer (untaught structures) and far transfer, but far-transfer effects were small and inconsistent. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**) The population is school pupils, many with or at risk of reading difficulties.
- Bogaerds-Hazenberg et al. (2020, *Reading Research Quarterly*; abstract only): 44 studies, Grades 4–6. Immediate effects g = 0.25 (questions), 0.57 (summarisation), 0.37 (recall), 0.38 (structure knowledge), but *no advantage at delayed post-tests*. Individual practice moderated delayed effects. This tempers Hebert et al. on durability. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Meyer & Poon (2001, *J Educ Psych* 93; abstract only): younger and older adults, 9 hours of structure-strategy training vs interest-strategy training vs none. Only structure training increased total recall and recall of the most important information, improved the consistency of strategy use across five passages, and helped learners use signals better; training plus signalling were additive. Of everything here this is the closest to our readers (adults), but it is a single study, a long training, and not experts reading papers.
- Armbruster, Anderson & Ostertag (1987, *RRQ*; citation checked only) is another structure-instruction study; I did not read it.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- The adult evidence is a small set of mostly one laboratory's studies with college students and short, specially built texts. Few report effect sizes in what I could open; delays are short.
- Signals can *hurt* unsignalled material (Lorch & Lorch 1996), and trade depth for breadth (Lorch et al. 1993). A tool that signals only some parts of a text is therefore not harmless.
- A simple structure gains nothing from signals (1996, Exp. 1). A well-organised research paper with author headings may already be fully signalled.
- Expertise reversal: for high-prior-knowledge readers, extra signals may be redundant or intrusive (multimedia studies, so an analogy).
- Structure instruction effects fade at delay in at least one large meta-analysis (Bogaerds-Hazenberg 2020), and far transfer is small and inconsistent (Hebert 2016).
- Published signalling meta-analyses report heterogeneity moderated by study quality (Alpizar 2020). I did not see publication-bias checks.
- Nothing opened covers generative-AI headings. A wrong heading mis-frames what follows; no study tests this.

## What it means for Spideryarn

**Structure mode and the tree** ([structure.md](../../project/structure.md), [granularity-zoom.md](../../project/granularity-zoom.md)). Today it draws the article's nested tree, with labels and gists, in the band, in "fisheye" (open around where you are) or "expanded" form. *Supports*: the case for visible topic structure is the signalling literature's best-supported result, better memory for topics and organisation. The tree is available beside the prose rather than inserted into it, which respects the finding that signals work through how people read. *Suggests a change*: the half-signalled result suggests that if the tree's coverage is patchy (some sections richly labelled, others not), unlabelled sections may be recalled worse; proposal: show coverage evenly or say where labels are missing. *Untested*: whether reading the tree *before* the text (an overview) beats consulting it during.

**Generated headings** ([structure-step.md](../../project/structure-step.md); the earlier proposal in [ideas-reading-science.md § 4](../../research/260828b-decorated-mode/ideas-reading-science.md)). The step keeps author headings verbatim unless uninformative and builds a tree over flat articles. *Supports*: signalling a flat text's topics is what Lorch's results would predict helps, particularly for complex topic structure. *Suggests a change*: the 1996 finding that signals only help when the structure is complex suggests generating headings only for long or complex flat articles. *Untested*: any comparison of model-made against author headings, and what an inaccurate model heading costs.

**Overviews and structure training** (no mode owns this). The structure-strategy evidence, especially Meyer & Poon 2001, suggests that teaching readers to ask "what is the top-level structure here (problem/solution, comparison, cause)?" improves recall of the main ideas. A proposal, not a finding: surface the author's own signal words and the piece's rhetorical shape. *Untested* for expert readers of papers.

**Calibration.** The headings experiment (Caption it! 2023) is a pointer that structure cues may improve comprehension *judgement*. This is a hypothesis for any mode that claims to help readers know what they know.

## Not verified

- Lorch (1989) *Educ Psychol Rev* 1(3): 209–234, doi 10.1007/BF01320135. I confirmed the title, author and page range via Crossref but could not open the text. The claim that signals help the content they cue more than unsignalled content came from a search summary and is not used in the evidence; the primary studies above support it.
- Schneider et al. (2018), doi 10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001: citation confirmed via Crossref, results not read.
- Sanchez, Lorch & Lorch (2001) *Contemp Educ Psychol* (doi 10.1006/ceps.2000.1056), Lorch & Lorch (1996) *Contemp Educ Psychol* "Effects of headings on text recall and summarization" (doi 10.1006/ceps.1996.0022), Hyönä & Lorch (2004): citations confirmed, abstracts unavailable.
- Question versus statement headings, and generated versus author headings: searches did not find studies I could open.
- Hebert et al. 2016's reported large effect (ES 0.96) for students with or at risk of disabilities came from a search-result summary; not verified.

# Speaking versus typing

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

Greg's belief, as the product encodes it: *"voice is lower effort, it's faster. And people probably
say more and probably provide more context. So that's why we try and encourage that rather than
typing."* The question is whether the research backs each clause: faster, easier, more content, more
useful content, and better for learning. Short answer: **faster and more content, yes, well
supported; "lower effort" and "better learning" are weaker.** Speech is about three times faster than
phone typing, and spoken answers to open questions contain more words and more topics. But the one
direct test of voice against text for an AI tutor found no learning difference. The oral-versus-
written explaining literature is small, mixed and mostly on students, and its best-supported claim
is narrower than "speaking is better": oral explaining is more elaborate and helps with difficult
material, while writing organises better. The evidence base is almost all short messages, survey
answers and student explanations, not experts talking about research papers.

## The short answer

- Speech recognition entry was about 2.9 times faster than a phone touchscreen keyboard (153 vs 52
  words per minute in English) with fewer corrected errors, but slightly more errors left in the
  final text. Lab study, short messages, 2016-era recogniser. (**High certainty on speed · Analogy**)
- Voice answers to open survey questions on sensitive topics had more words and more topics than
  typed answers, with no difference in sentiment. Randomised, N = 1,001 smartphone panellists.
  (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Voice answers are not always better liked or produced: when voice was pushed, response rates fell,
  and a survey of panellists found only about half would be willing to use it. (**Moderate
  certainty · Indirect**)
- Oral explaining tends to produce more elaborative, personally-addressed explanations; written
  explaining tends to produce better organised ones. Which helps learning depends on the outcome
  and the text. (**Low certainty · Indirect**)
- For difficult texts, oral explaining beat written explaining on comprehension (N = 115); for easy
  texts there was no difference. (**Low certainty · Indirect**, one study, abstract only)
- Explaining to a fictitious listener on video helped over restudying; writing to them did not. The
  proposed mechanism, social presence, is a hypothesis, and a 2021 review says several non-
  interactive teaching effects failed to replicate. (**Contested · Indirect**)
- Thinking aloud itself does not change performance on average (r near zero, 94 studies), though it
  takes longer; being asked to explain does change it. (**High certainty · Analogy**)
- A preprint on a voice AI tutor (N = 86 MBA students) found voice nearly doubled interaction and
  cost 2.8 times as much, with no learning gain over text. Not peer reviewed. (**Low certainty ·
  Indirect**, preprint)

## The evidence

### Is speech faster, and is it less effortful?

**Speed.** Ruan, Wobbrock, Liou, Ng and Landay (2018) had participants enter short messages on an
iPhone 6 Plus, using a deep-learning recogniser (Baidu Deep Speech 2) or the built-in keyboard, in
English and Mandarin. Speech was 2.93 times faster in English (153 vs 52 words per minute) and 2.87
times faster in Mandarin. Corrected errors during entry were fewer by speech (5.30% vs 11.22%) but
errors left in the final text were slightly higher (1.30% vs 0.79%). Read from the abstract (via
the arXiv version, [1608.07323](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.07323)); N and timing details not
recorded here. Limits: short messages rather than reflective prose, a lab not a train, a recogniser
that has since changed. Typing on a laptop keyboard is much faster than phone typing, so the
three-times figure should not be carried over to a desktop reader. **Analogy** for Spideryarn, whose
users are mostly on larger screens.

**"Lower effort."** I found no peer-reviewed study of effort for dictating reflections on reading.
Two nearby results. Rzepka, Berger and Hess (2022; lab, N = 116, information search) found that
talking to a voice assistant gave lower *perceived* cognitive effort and higher enjoyment than a
text chatbot, depending on how goal-directed the task was (abstract only). That is perceived effort
on a lookup task. A 2023 study of English-to-Chinese translation trainees (found by search, not
opened, so it appears under Not verified) reportedly found dictation reduced typing effort without
saving time or lowering cognitive effort overall. So "lower effort" is supported for the motor act of
entering text and for felt ease, not demonstrated for the thinking.

### Do people say more, and is it better content?

Höhne, Gavras and Claassen (2024) randomised 1,001 smartphone survey respondents to answer open
questions on sensitive topics by text or voice. Voice answers had more words and more topics; sentiment
and extremity did not differ (abstract only). Revilla and Couper (2026; N = 1,001, Spanish opt-in panel,
questions about nursing homes) found that designs pushing respondents to dictate or record gave more
themes and more characters than a typing-only control, but response rates were lower, "especially"
when recording was requested (abstract only). Revilla, Couper and Ochoa (2018) found about half of
the panellists (N = 1,476) already used voice input at least sometimes and 54% said they probably or
definitely would use it for open questions, with willingness lower among those who found the survey
hard (abstract and summary of the paper's figures).

This supports "people say more and cover more". It does not show the extra is useful, accurate or
well organised; more words and topics could include filler, repetition and digression. A search
result describing a further study (not opened) suggests voice answers are more intuitive and typed
answers more deliberate; I treat that as unverified. Populations are survey panellists answering
questions about public matters, not readers reflecting on a text.

A parallel in memory reports: Sauerland and Sporer (2011; N = 192, staged crime video) found spoken
descriptions contained more details than written ones with no accuracy difference, and the authors
speculate both working-memory and social causes (abstract only). Analogy: eyewitness recall of a
video, not reading.

### Oral versus written explaining, and learning

This is the closest literature to the Learn mode design (say what you took from it).

- **Lachner, Ly and Nückles (2018)**: 48 students studied a hypertext on combustion engines, then
  explained it orally or in writing to a fictitious other. Written explanations were better organised
  and that organisation was linked to higher conceptual knowledge; oral explanations were more
  elaborative and were linked to better transfer (abstract only). Small N; different benefits for
  different outcomes. **Low certainty · Indirect**.
- **Jacob, Lachner and Scheiter (2020)**: N = 115 students read a hard and an easy text and then
  explained orally, in writing, or retrieved (control). With the easy text, no difference. With the
  hard text, oral explaining led to better comprehension than written, and retrieval was lowest.
  The modality effect was mediated by more personal references and more comprehensive
  explanations, and the authors attribute this to social presence while speaking (abstract only). The
  retrieval control did worst, which fits the explaining literature but is one study. **Low
  certainty · Indirect**.
- **Hoogerheide, Deijkers, Loyens, Heijltjes and van Gog (2016)**: two experiments. Studying with
  an explain goal versus a test goal made no difference, and writing an explanation to fictitious
  students did no better than restudying. In the direct comparison only explaining on video beat
  restudying. The authors tie this to feelings of social presence (abstract as shown on the Utrecht
  research portal; the full text returned 403). **Contested · Indirect**, because the parent
  effect has replicated unevenly.
- **The meta-analysis inside Lachner et al. (2021, *Learning by writing explanations*)**, as reported in that paper's abstract, pooled self-explaining studies: g = 0.29 for
  conceptual knowledge and 0.22 for transfer, moderated by modality, with oral larger than written. In
  the same paper two experiments (N = 147 lab; N = 51 field) found writing to a fictitious student gave
  no benefit over controls; only self-explaining beat control on transfer in Experiment 2. **Moderate
  certainty on "oral > written" · Indirect** (abstract only; meta-analysis details not checked).
- **Lachner, Hoogerheide, van Gog and Renkl (2021)** reviewed learning by teaching without a real
  audience and note that several recent studies failed to replicate the effect, then discuss
  boundary conditions (abstract only). This is the review to read before leaning on "explain it to
  someone".
- **Fiorella and Mayer (2013)**: I confirmed the journal title and DOI only. A UCSB thesis record
  found by search reports that preparing to teach helped on an immediate test (d = .55-.59) while
  actually teaching helped on a one-week delayed test (d = .79-.90). That is a thesis record, not the
  article, so it is **not load-bearing**.

None of these is about reading research papers. Materials are short expository texts on mechanics,
biology and similar, read by undergraduates. In nearly all of them the explaining is *to a fictitious
other*; in Spideryarn's Learn mode the listener is a real, responding model, which none of these
studies tested.

### Thinking aloud

Fox, Ericsson and Best (2011), a meta-analysis of 94 studies and about 3,500 participants, found that
concurrent think-aloud had an effect on performance near zero, whereas procedures asking people to
describe or explain their thinking were reactive and raised performance. Both lengthened task time
(abstract only, via the OpenAlex record; the figures r = -.03 were read in a search summary and are not
confirmed here). The relevance: saying out loud what you are doing does not damage the thinking, and
being asked to explain can help. **High certainty · Analogy** (mostly problem-solving, not reading).

### Spoken retrieval versus typed retrieval

Putnam and Roediger (2013; three experiments, paired associates) found few differences between typed
and spoken responses on a first test, a similar testing effect either way, and that silent retrieval
helped about as much as overt (abstract). Response mode, in other words, does not appear to be where
retrieval practice gets its power. This supports offering both modes, but it is word-pair material.
**Moderate certainty · Analogy**.

### Voice and an AI

Yang, Van Alstyne and Dellarocas (arXiv preprint, 2026, preregistered) randomised 86 MBA students to
a structured AI tutor; within the tutored arm they alternated weekly between voice and text. Tutoring
helped (6.63 points more than a consumer-AI holdout), but voice did not improve learning over text,
nearly doubled conversational interaction, cost 2.8 times as much and was preferred over time
(abstract of the arXiv record, labelled preprint). Taken at face value: voice changes how people
interact, not how much they learn. **Low certainty · Indirect** (one preprint, adults but a course
not a paper).

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **The learning claim is the weakest link.** The strongest result for speaking (Jacob 2020) is one
  experiment, with an effect only on a difficult text; the 2016 and 2021 papers find that writing to
  a fictitious other often does nothing, which is a finding about writing as much as about speaking.
  Written explaining organises content better (Lachner 2018), and a reader who wants an argument
  tidied might benefit from typing.
- **Speed is about phone typing.** Ruan 2018 used a touchscreen keyboard. On a laptop, with the
  skills of a working researcher, the advantage is smaller (not measured here).
- **More words is not more insight.** The survey results count words and topics. The only study
  that scored explanation quality, Jacob 2020, tied the oral advantage to personal references and
  comprehensiveness, which are not the same as correctness.
- **Takeup.** In surveys, pushing voice reduced response and half the panel would not want it.
  Many settings (open offices, trains, shared houses) make speaking impossible, and some readers
  will feel self-conscious. I found no peer-reviewed study on self-consciousness in dictation for
  adults; that is an untested concern, not evidence of one.
- **Dictation transcription errors** are a real cost: Ruan found slightly more uncorrected errors
  in the final text under speech.
- **Social presence is a theory.** It is invoked to explain the video-versus-writing gap but is
  not itself measured in the abstracts I read; the replication problems flagged by Lachner 2021 make
  it hard to rely on.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Dictation (talking into a text box)

How it works: a button records, transcribes twice (live words are decoration, a second pass uses a
vocabulary) and strips fillers; the button's card says why talking is worth it
([dictation.md](../../project/dictation.md)). **Supports:** the speed and the word-and-topic quantity
claims are the best-supported parts of Greg's belief. Removing "ums" is consistent with the one
known cost, that spoken text is messier. **Suggests a change (proposal):** the card could say
"faster and fuller" rather than anything about learning, and offer the keyboard as an equal. The
typing-is-slower claim should not be made to laptop users without a caveat. **Untested:** whether
extra spoken content helps the model give better corrections.

### Live conversation

How it works: press Live and talk to a companion that has the piece in front of it, switching in and
out of typing in the same thread ([live-conversation.md](../../project/live-conversation.md)).
**Supports:** voice raised engagement in the one voice-versus-text AI tutor preprint, and perceived
ease in a lab search task. **Suggests a change:** the preprint found no learning gain and 2.8 times
the cost, so the case for Live is preference and fluency rather than learning outcomes; worth
saying that plainly in the product, and watching cost. **Untested:** any voice-versus-text
comparison for a reader and a document.

### Learn mode

How it works: the reader says, or types, what they took from the article, and the model corrects
briefly and nudges them to remember more ([learn-mode.md](../../project/learn-mode.md)).
**Supports:** explaining to a listener helps over restudying in several studies, and oral explaining
looked stronger than written on difficult text (Jacob 2020, Lachner 2018 for elaboration and
transfer). Spoken retrieval works as well as typed (Putnam and Roediger). **Suggests a change
(proposal):** because writing organised content better in one study, a final "type a two-line
summary" step after talking could capture both benefits; it is a speculative design, untested.
**Untested:** whether a responding model counts as the social presence that fictitious listeners
only imitate, and whether the findings carry over to experts reading papers.

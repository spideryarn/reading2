# Typography and on-page decoration

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: does it help a reader if the page itself is marked up for them (highlighted sentences,
bold fragments, hard-to-read fonts, a particular typeface or line length), and does it help to give
different voices (author, model, reader) different faces? The short answer is that nearly all of the
famous "make the page do the work" tricks are weak or null, and the one that is not null, highlighting
chosen by someone else, is a double-edged tool: good marks help a little, bad marks mislead readers
and leave them feeling they understood. Nobody has tested the thing Spideryarn actually does with
fonts (marking who is speaking), so that rests on design reasoning, not evidence.

## The short answer

- **Highlighting that someone else chose helps memory and comprehension a little when it is good**, in a pooled analysis of 36 articles; it is not a free lunch, and a 2023 digital-reading experiment found no average benefit at all (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**).
- **Highlighting that is wrong hurts, and it hurts calibration too**: undergraduates reading passages with inappropriate pre-highlighting scored lower, and their confidence ratings ran backwards against their actual scores (**Low-to-Moderate certainty · Indirect**, one study read in abstract).
- **Bionic Reading (bolding the start of each word) does not speed reading or improve comprehension**: four independent eye-tracking and reading-time studies, 2024-2026, all null, and one finds a small cost (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**; abstracts only).
- **Hard-to-read fonts as a "desirable difficulty" did not hold up.** A 17-lab pooling, a meta-analysis and several preregistered Sans Forgetica studies found no reliable learning benefit; what disfluency reliably does is lower confidence and add study time (**High certainty that there is no dependable benefit · Indirect**).
- **Reading on screens costs a little comprehension for expository text, and readers on screens are slightly worse calibrated**: g = -0.25 (Clinton 2019) and -0.21 (Delgado 2018); nothing for narrative, nothing for reading time (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**).
- **Typeface, size and line length matter much less than people assume within a normal range.** Print size has a wide plateau of equally fast reading; fonts change speed for individuals but not comprehension; line length evidence is thin and inconsistent (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**).
- **There is no direct evidence on marking author/model/reader voices with different typefaces.** The design is reasonable and is not contradicted by anything found, but it is untested (**No evidence · Analogy**).

## The evidence

### Text that someone else has already highlighted

**What the pooled evidence says.** Ponce, Mayer and Méndez (2022) combined 36 published articles (1938-2019, 85 effect sizes) comparing students reading academic text with and without highlighting. Highlighting done by the learner improved memory (average effect 0.36) but not comprehension (0.20). Highlighting supplied by an instructor, the closest analogue to what a model would do, improved both, at 0.44 each, for college students (0.41) and school students (0.48). I could read the abstract and the page's metadata only: no confidence intervals, heterogeneity or publication-bias checks were visible, and the statistic is not named on the page, so treat the numbers as indicative. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**: students, mostly short passages, usually tested on memory of the marked content.)

**A later, single digital experiment found nothing.** Masón and colleagues (2023, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning*) randomly assigned 170 university students to highlight themselves, read experimenter-provided highlighting, or read plain text on screen. They measured literal and inferential comprehension, transfer and calibration at immediate and delayed tests. Linear mixed models showed no main effect of condition on any outcome. Highlighting helped only those who scored higher on cognitive reflection (resisting a snap answer): the authors call it "amplification", where people who already read actively get more out of the aid. (abstract only; **Moderate certainty · Indirect**.)

**A preregistered study that pre-highlighted some sentences.** In Experiment 2 of Geller, Davis and Peterson (2020), a passage about groundwater had select sentences in yellow highlighting, in Sans Forgetica, or unmodified. Pre-highlighted information was recalled better than unmodified information. (abstract only; N not visible to me. The abstract does not say what happened to the unhighlighted sentences, which is the question that matters most for attention narrowing.)

**Does highlighting make you forget the unmarked parts?** Our earlier note ([ideas-reading-science.md](../260828b-decorated-mode/ideas-reading-science.md)) said attention narrows onto the marked text and incidental learning of everything else drops. The one study I could read on this found the opposite: Yue, Storm, Kornell and Bjork (2015) had people read a passage twice, with or without highlighting, with massed or spaced readings, and tested a week later. Highlighting helped overall, especially with massed reading, and did **not** impair memory for non-highlighted information. This was highlighting by the reader, not by someone else, so it does not settle the pre-highlighted case. Its more useful finding for us concerns calibration: students who doubted or were unsure about highlighting benefited more than those who favoured it. (abstract only; sample size not visible.) The attention-narrowing claim is classic but I did not verify a source for it, so it is "Contested" rather than established here.

**Bad highlights.** Gier, Kreiner and Natz-Gonzalez (2009) randomly assigned 180 undergraduates to read passages with no highlighting, appropriate highlighting or inappropriate highlighting (a 3 x 2 design with low and high text difficulty). Inappropriate highlighting lowered comprehension and metacognitive accuracy: for those readers, ratings of how well they understood and how well they had answered were negatively correlated with their actual scores. Higher confidence went with lower performance. The abstract says this repeated earlier work (Silvers and Kreiner 1997, whose bibliographic record I confirmed but whose findings I did not read). (abstract only; **Low-to-Moderate certainty · Indirect**: one paper, a short-passage laboratory design, undergraduates.)

**Overall verdict on highlighting.** Dunlosky and colleagues' (2013) review of ten study techniques rated highlighting "low utility", chiefly because benefits were limited or inconsistent (abstract). That rating covers highlighting in general, not model-quality highlighting of a paper you already know something about.

### Signalling and typographic cues

Typographic or colour cues that mark key ideas are called "signalling". Beege and colleagues (2021, *Cognitive Processing*) gave 138 university students a text on tsunami formation with colour signalling and/or a disfluent font. Signalling improved transfer, which the authors attribute to lower cognitive load; font fluency had no effect on learning (abstract only; one experiment, one topic). A larger meta-analysis of signalling exists (Schneider, Beege, Nebel and Rey 2018) but I could confirm only its bibliographic record, so I leave its numbers out (see Not verified). (**Low certainty · Analogy**: multimedia-learning materials, not research papers.)

### "Bionic Reading" and fixation guidance

Bionic Reading bolds the first part of each word on the claim that it guides the eye and speeds reading.

- Snell (2024, *Acta Psychologica*) had participants read 100 paragraphs in Bionic or normal font and found no significant difference in reading time.
- Beelders (2025, *Journal of Eye Movement Research*): Bionic font did not significantly change fixation durations, number of fixations or reading speed, and did not help with rare or unfamiliar words.
- Spear and colleagues (2025, *Attention, Perception & Psychophysics*): bolding the first half of words did not help relative to bolding the middle or last half, and cost reading relative to unbolded text; knowing only the first few letters was not enough to recognise whole words.
- Zhang, Zhao and Wang (2026, *Acta Psychologica*), second-language academic reading, five conditions: standard Bionic (bold start) and bold end gave no reliable gain in reading efficiency or comprehension; two gradient variants reduced efficiency without improving accuracy.

All four read in abstract form only, so numbers (N, effect sizes) are not available to me. They agree with each other, with different labs and methods. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**: adults, mostly short paragraphs; the 2026 study is L2 readers. None used a research paper.)

### Hard-to-read fonts as a "desirable difficulty"

The original claim: Diemand-Yauman, Oppenheimer and Vaughan (2011, *Cognition*) reported that information in hard-to-read fonts was better remembered, in a laboratory study and in high-school classrooms (abstract only; no sample sizes in the record I read).

It has not held up.

- Meyer and 9 co-authors (2015, *JEP: General*) tested whether disfluent fonts make people think more carefully on counterintuitive maths problems. Pooled with 16 replication attempts, they found no effect on solution rates, under any moderator they tried; disfluent fonts only slightly increased response time. (abstract only; this is about reasoning, not memory.)
- Xie, Zhou and Liu (2018, *Educational Psychology Review*) meta-analysed text-based learning: 25 articles, 3,135 participants. No overall effect on recall (d = -0.01) or transfer (d = 0.03). Disfluency lowered judgements of learning (d = -0.43) and lengthened learning time (d = 0.52). (abstract on the publisher page.)
- Weissgerber, Brunmair and Rummer (2021) re-examined that meta-analysis, found errors and inconsistencies in coding and effect sizes, judged its transfer results "rather robust" and doubted its recall and moderator results. So the null is the best reading, but with a mistake-prone analysis behind it.
- Geller, Davis and Peterson (2020, *Memory*): three preregistered experiments; Sans Forgetica gave no benefit compared with unmodified text, generation, or pre-highlighting. Huff, Maxwell and Mitchell (2022): four experiments, nearly 300 participants, no effect on correct or false memory. Geller and Peterson (2021): three preregistered experiments (N = 231, 232, 232), a moderate benefit only when no test was expected.
- Weissgerber, Terhorst and Rummer (2024, *Metacognition and Learning*): a preregistered direct replication (N = 96) of an earlier result that disfluency helps high working-memory-capacity learners found null effects for disfluency, capacity and their interaction on every outcome.
- Beege and colleagues (2021): disfluent fonts improved metacognitive accuracy and effort but increased cognitive load and frustration, with no learning gain.

So: **no dependable learning benefit; reliable effects on feeling less sure and taking longer.** (**High certainty about the null · Indirect**: students, short materials, no expert readers.) The reduced confidence is interesting but is not a reason to degrade type; the cost in time and frustration is real.

### Screen against paper

- Clinton (2019, *Journal of Research in Reading*): 29 reports (33 studies; performance n = 2,799; reading time n = 1,233; calibration n = 698), randomised experiments published 2008-2018. Screens had a negative effect on performance (g = -0.25), limited to expository text (g = -0.32) with no difference for narrative (g = -0.04). No reliable difference in reading time (g = 0.08). Judgements of performance were better calibrated on paper (g = 0.20). (abstract.)
- Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman and Salmerón (2018, *Educational Research Review*): 54 studies from 2000-2017 (38 between- and 16 within-participant designs); paper advantage g = -0.21 in both designs. The advantage was larger under time pressure than self-paced reading, held for informational texts, and grew over the publication years. (abstract; the figure of about 171,000 participants in the record is as listed by two databases and I did not check it against the paper.)
- Clinton-Lisell and Litzinger (2026, *Education and Information Technologies*), a network meta-analysis of 56 studies with 79 effect sizes: paper led when scrolling was required (g about 0.35-0.48); with no scrolling, devices showed no reliable differences. (abstract only; recent, so not cross-checked by others.)

The "shallowing" idea (screens induce shallower processing) is the usual explanation, supported mainly by the time-pressure and calibration patterns, not by direct tests. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**: mostly students and short texts; effects are small.) The scrolling result is the practically interesting one for a web reader of long texts.

### Typeface, size and line length

- Print size: Legge and Bigelow (2011, *Journal of Vision*) review vision-science findings and argue the sizes used in books and newspapers fall within a "fluent range", a factor of ten in angular x-height (about 0.2 to 2 degrees), where text can be read at maximum speed. So size within that range is not a lever. (abstract.)
- Typeface: Wallace and colleagues (2022, *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction*) used hundreds of crowdsourced adults with font size normalised by perception. Reading speed rose by 35% comparing fastest and slowest fonts **without affecting comprehension**, and speed varied by individual so "one font does not fit all". (abstract only; speed not comprehension is the outcome, and crowdsourced participants read short texts.)
- Line length: A 2004 review of screen-layout research (Behaviour & Information Technology; author unconfirmed) concludes that characters per line is the critical variable and calls for studies of its interaction with eye movements and scrolling; it gives no firm optimum in the abstract I read. I could not open the abstract of Dyson and Haselgrove (2001), the commonly cited experiment, so I make no claim about a specific figure.

(**Moderate certainty that size and typeface choices within the normal range are second-order · Indirect**; **Low certainty on any specific line length · Indirect**.)

### Marking voices with different typefaces

I searched for evidence on typefaces as a cue to source (author versus another voice) and on visually distinguishing AI-generated from human text in reading, and found nothing that tests it for text read at length. The nearest peer-reviewed result is Wittenberg and colleagues (2025, *PNAS Nexus*): in preregistered survey experiments with 7,579 Americans on misleading AI-generated **images**, every label tested reduced belief in the claims, but simply saying "AI-generated" did little to change engagement (abstract only). That is a different medium, task and population, so it is an analogy at most. (**No evidence · Analogy**.)

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Almost nothing studies expert adults reading research papers.** The highlighting, disfluency and Bionic studies are mostly undergraduates reading a few hundred words, tested on recall. The screen-versus-paper meta-analyses are wider but also mostly student samples.
- **Highlighting evidence is split by who does it and what is measured.** Good marks help memory; the 2023 digital experiment found no average effect; bad marks harm. A model's highlights will be better than random and worse than an author's; the actual quality is the empirical question, and I found no study of model-chosen highlights.
- **Most sources here were read in abstract only**, which hides sample sizes, confidence intervals and risk of bias. In particular the Springer meta-analyses showed no CIs, heterogeneity or small-study checks.
- **The disfluency meta-analysis has coding errors** (Weissgerber et al. 2021), though the direction of its conclusion is supported by the replications.
- **Screen inferiority is small and sensitive to conditions** (time pressure, scrolling, genre), and it may have changed as devices have.
- **Possible contrary reading for our design**: the "amplification" finding (Masón 2023) suggests aids help the readers who need them least. Cuing for every reader may widen, not narrow, differences.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Voice-specific typefaces ([fonts.md](../../project/fonts.md))

How it works today: four voices, each with a face: the author's Source Serif 4, the model's IBM Plex Mono, the reader's Arial, and the chrome in Geist, "so the reader can tell at a glance which is which"; a summary must never be mistaken for the author's sentence. A section title uses the author's face only when it is the heading the author actually wrote.

- **Supports**: nothing in the evidence argues against it, and the typeface evidence says the choice of face within normal ranges does not change comprehension (Wallace 2022), so the cost is small. The plain-words intent (provenance at a glance) is a different goal from anything tested.
- **Suggests a change**: one thing to watch. Monospaced text for long model prose is untested; the speed-by-font results are individual-dependent and say nothing about monospace at paragraph length. A proposal: if model paragraphs get long (chat, summaries), check reading speed on a few readers before widening the monospace voice.
- **Untested**: whether readers use the voice cue to calibrate trust, or whether it reduces confusion between a gist and the author's sentence. This would be a cheap and interesting experiment: show gists in the author's face versus the model's face and ask readers who wrote each.

### Decorated mode (the playground in [260929a-decorated-mode.html](../../tutorials/260929a-decorated-mode.html); not built into the app)

How it works today: a playground of 24 switchable decorations on one essay, with the rule that no word of the author's is removed, reordered or rewritten. The earlier research note listed pre-highlighted key sentences, fainter "unimportant" sentences, Bionic-style bolding and disfluent fonts among the things that would probably backfire.

- **Supports**: the Bionic and disfluent-font rejections are well backed (see above) and can be stated more firmly now. The "emphasis budget" idea (limit how much of a page is marked) fits with the signalling literature's reliance on contrast.
- **Suggests a change**: soften the claim on pre-highlighting. The evidence is "helps when accurate, hurts when wrong, and helps the confident most", not "the worst feature". Two verified details matter: inappropriate marks worsen calibration (Gier 2009), and the one pooled analysis found instructor-supplied highlights helped (Ponce 2022). Proposal: if any model highlighting is ever shown, show it only after the reader has read the passage (keeps the existing "earn the ink" rule), make it easy to dismiss, and keep its quality measurable. The earlier note's claim about "attention narrowing" should be marked as unverified here.
- **Untested**: model-chosen highlights on research papers, and any calibration effect for expert readers.

### Reader marks ([comments.md](../../project/comments.md))

How it works today: select a sentence to bookmark it, with an optional note and an optional model reply.

- **Supports**: reader-made marks are the best-supported form: learner-generated highlighting improved memory (0.36) in Ponce 2022 and did not hurt unmarked material in Yue 2015.
- **Suggests a change**: the benefit for comprehension is weaker (0.20), so a marking tool alone is not a comprehension tool. A proposal: nudge towards the note rather than the mark, since the evidence for the mark alone is thin.
- **Untested**: whether marks plus later review of them helps readers of papers.

### Reading measure and screen reading ([typography.md](../../project/typography.md))

How it works today: a 65ch measure, centred in its column, with Geist as the base face.

- **Supports**: 65ch is conventional, but the evidence does not pin down a best line length; the one review I could read says characters per line is what matters without naming a value. Nothing contradicts it.
- **Suggests a change**: the scrolling result (Clinton-Lisell and Litzinger 2026) and the expository-text effect (Clinton 2019) suggest long expository articles on screen carry a small penalty. A proposal: the existing orientation aids (headings, position cues) matter more than type tweaks; this is an argument for the structure features, not for new typography.
- **Untested**: density-aware leading (varying line height by difficulty), proposed in the earlier note, has no direct evidence in what I found.

# Highlighting, annotating and taking notes while you read

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: when a reader marks up a text, writes in the margin or takes notes, does it help them understand and remember it, and does it matter how they do it? The short answer: **passive marking (highlighting, underlining) helps a little at best, and mostly for recall of the marked facts rather than understanding**. Notes help more, mainly because of what you do when you write them and (more reliably) when you review them. The famous "handwriting beats typing" result has been weakened by replication, and what survives is small. Almost all of this evidence comes from students listening to lectures or reading short textbook passages, not from experienced adults reading papers in their own field. We have **no** good study of the thing Spideryarn actually does (select a passage, get a free bookmark, optionally write words).

## The short answer

- Highlighting or underlining that the learner does themselves gives a small gain in memory for the text (average effect about 0.36) but no reliable gain in comprehension (about 0.20, not significant); the gain was found in college students, not school pupils. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Highlighting done *for* the reader by someone else (an instructor) helped both memory and comprehension (about 0.44 each) in the same meta-analysis. This hints that the benefit comes from pointing attention at the right things, which novices do badly. (**Low certainty · Analogy**; one meta-analysis, abstract-level only)
- The influential review of ten study techniques rated highlighting "low utility": students rely on it heavily, but it does not reliably improve performance. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**; abstract only, and a review rather than a pooled analysis)
- Taking notes beats not taking them for learning, but modestly: an earlier meta-analysis of 57 first-language studies is reported (second-hand) as finding an average effect of 0.22 for note-taking versus none, with reviewing notes adding more. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**; I did not open the original)
- Laptop note-takers write more words and more verbatim text than longhand note-takers, but the claim that this *harms* learning has not held up: a 145-student direct replication and an eight-study mini meta-analysis found no significant quiz advantage for longhand. A 2024 meta-analysis of 24 studies still finds a small edge for handwritten notes (g = 0.25) when students also review them, so the question is open but the effect is small. (**Contested · Indirect**)
- Note-taking alone, or with an AI assistant, produced better comprehension and three-day retention than an AI assistant alone in a pre-registered randomised trial of 405 pupils aged 14-15, even though most pupils preferred the AI. (**Low certainty · Analogy**; one trial, children, abstract only)
- In a small within-subject study (N = 30, conference paper), fully automated AI notes gave lower post-test scores than a middle level of AI help, while users preferred the automated version. (**Low certainty · Analogy**; conference paper, not load-bearing)

## The evidence

### Highlighting and underlining

**Review verdict.** Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013) reviewed ten learning techniques that students can use without special equipment, rating each on whether it works across learners, materials and test types. Practice testing and spaced practice rated "high"; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaving "moderate"; highlighting/underlining, summarisation and three others "low". Students report relying heavily on highlighting but it "does not reliably improve performance". (Read: PubMed abstract only.) Miyatsu, Nguyen & McDaniel (2018) revisited five popular strategies students like, and offer tips for using each better (PubMed abstract only; I did not see their detailed highlighting guidance). (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

**Pooled evidence.** Ponce and colleagues (2022) meta-analysed experiments comparing students reading academic texts with and without highlighting: 36 articles (1938 to 2019), 85 effect sizes. Learner-generated highlighting: memory 0.36, comprehension 0.20 (the authors treat comprehension as not significant). By level: college students 0.39, school pupils 0.24 (not significant). Instructor-provided highlighting: 0.44 for both memory and comprehension (college 0.41, school 0.48). I read this only as an abstract-level summary on a repository page, so CIs, moderators (delay, text type, training) and publication-bias checks are unseen. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect** for learner highlighting; instructor highlighting **Low · Analogy**)

**Why highlighting may disappoint.** The usual explanations are that readers mark whole sentences unselectively, and that marking feels like studying (a calibration problem). A search summary of eye-tracking work says highlights attract fixations whether or not they mark the important content; I did not open those studies, so they are not load-bearing. Yue, Storm, Kornell & Bjork (2015) studied highlighting and students' metacognitive beliefs but I could not open the paper (see Not verified).

**Training.** I found no pooled evidence that training readers to highlight selectively fixes the problem. What the Ponce result implies is that *better-placed* marks (an instructor's) work better, which is a different thing from teaching readers. (**Untested directly**)

### Taking notes: encoding, storage and review

Researchers split note-taking's benefit in two: the act of writing (the "encoding" effect) and the notes as a store to review later (the "external storage" effect). Kiewra's 1989 review of this "encoding-storage paradigm" is the foundation (bibliographic record only; not opened). Kobayashi's meta-analyses (2005 on the encoding effect; 2006 on note-taking plus reviewing) are described in Jin & Webb (2024), a meta-analysis of note-taking in second-language learning: Kobayashi (2005) pooled 57 first-language studies and found a weighted mean of 0.22 for note-taking versus none, with younger learners gaining more than university students and larger benefits on recall than recognition tests; Kobayashi (2006) found learners who could review their notes gained more. Jin & Webb themselves pooled 21 studies (N = 1,992): g = 0.56 (95% CI 0.24 to 0.88). (Read on Cambridge's page, the first 100,000 of 139,000 characters; second-language learners are an **Analogy**.) (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

The practical lesson in this literature is that review is where much of the value sits, and that notes help most when in the learner's own words, organised, and revisited. These specific claims come from Kiewra's programme and Peper & Mayer (1986), which I know only through secondary descriptions, so treat them as **Low certainty** until read.

### Longhand versus laptop

- **Original.** Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014): students took notes on laptops or longhand during lectures; laptop users did worse on conceptual questions, which the authors attributed to verbatim transcription over rephrasing. (PubMed record summary; the paper has a 2018 corrigendum.)
- **Direct replication.** Urry et al. (2021, Psychological Science): an 80-author team, 145 Tufts undergraduates, a direct replication of Study 1 plus exploratory mini meta-analyses of eight similar studies. As in the original, laptop users recorded more words and more verbatim text; unlike the original they did *not* score worse on the quiz. More words in notes predicted better scores; verbatim overlap predicting worse scores was not robust. The authors conclude the results do not support longhand improving immediate learning through better encoding. (Abstract only.) (**Contested · Indirect**)
- **Second replication.** Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019): two experiments (design details from a Learning Scientists blog summary, not the paper). Longhand gave better *factual* recall in the first experiment but no conceptual advantage; in the second there was no significant longhand-laptop difference and a no-notes group did no worse; at a two-day delay note method had no effect, and reviewing notes further reduced differences. Overlap between what was in the notes and what was tested predicted performance best. Sample sizes and effect sizes unseen.
- **Meta-analysis.** Flanigan, Wheeler, Colliot, Lu & Kiewra (2024): 24 studies from 21 articles, college students. Taking *and reviewing* handwritten notes goes with higher achievement (Hedges' g = 0.248, p < 0.001); typing increases the volume recorded (g = 0.919). (Abstract only; heterogeneity, study quality and publication bias unseen.) A small gap may reflect extra review effort or preference rather than handwriting as such. (**Contested · Indirect**)

Reading these together: laptop users capture and transcribe more; whether they learn less is at most a small effect; the more reproducible point is that **what the notes contain, and whether you go back to them, matters**.

### Notes while reading, and digital annotation

Almost all of the above is lecture note-taking. For *annotating a text while reading*, I found only small-scale or self-report work. Petrucco (2024; 112 first-year Master's students; abstract only) reports that a social annotation tool improved perceptions of digital reading and perceived understanding, with reading others' annotations to summarise concepts correlated with clearer understanding; the measures look like self-report. A Hypothesis blog post (2018, grey literature) describes a planned multi-institution study and cites no results. I found no pooled estimate of digital annotation's effect on tested comprehension for adults. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)

### Generative AI and note-taking (2023 to 2026)

- Kreijkes et al. (2025, Computers & Education): pre-registered randomised experiment, 405 pupils aged 14-15 in England, two passages, tests three days later. Note-taking alone and note-taking plus an LLM gave significantly better retention and comprehension than LLM use alone. Most pupils preferred the LLM and found it more helpful, and said it made difficult material more accessible, while note-taking supported deeper engagement and memory. Effect sizes not seen. (Abstract only; children.) (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Chen et al. (2025, CSCW conference paper; labelled as such): N = 30 within-subject, lecture videos. "Intermediate" AI (live summary) produced the highest post-test, "automated" structured AI notes the lowest, though participants preferred the automated condition. (Abstract only; not load-bearing.)

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Population and material gap.** Almost every study is undergraduates or school pupils, on short passages or lectures, tested in the same session or days later. Experts reading papers in their own field have prior knowledge that changes what "important" means; none of these studies speaks to them directly.
- **The laptop-versus-longhand finding is a cautionary tale.** A striking original result with a plausible story did not replicate cleanly, and the 2024 meta-analysis tilts back toward a small handwriting benefit. Both can be partly right; neither supports a strong product claim.
- **Highlighting evidence could be read two ways.** The "low utility" verdict is for highlighting as a standalone study technique. It does not say a mark is useless as a *finding aid* when you return to a text, which is how a reader of long-form articles might use it; the meta-analysis does not test that.
- **Mostly abstracts.** Springer, SAGE and Elsevier blocked full-text retrieval, so heterogeneity and bias statements are mostly missing for the pooled results.
- **Preference versus outcome.** In both AI studies, participants liked the more automated option more. Perceived ease is not learning.
- Dunlosky (2013) is a narrative review with a rating scheme, not a pooled effect.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Comments: select to highlight, optional note, optional AI answer

How it works today: selecting words writes a yellow highlight at once and opens that comment's box; saving is free and the model answers only if asked ([comments.md](../../project/comments.md)).

- **Supports.** Keeping the model optional and the bookmark free fits the Kreijkes result that the reader's own writing is worth protecting, and the Chen result that fully automated notes are preferred but not best. Instant marking also lets marks serve as a finding aid, which the evidence does not rule out.
- **Suggests a change (proposal).** Bare highlighting is the weakest-evidenced act, so the box could make "add a few words" the easiest next step (it already opens on selection), perhaps with a nudge to put it in your own words. This is untested; the evidence says only that generative notes and review matter more than marking. Resurfacing a reader's own notes for review is a further idea.
- **Untested.** Whether instant yellow highlighting encourages over-highlighting, or lowers the chance of writing anything.

### Marginalia: the notes to the right of the prose

How it works today: a column showing other modes' stored output (relation words, summaries, FAQ items) beside the block each concerns ([marginalia.md](../../project/marginalia.md)).

- **Supports.** Nothing directly; these are machine-written, not reader-generated.
- **Suggests a change (proposal).** The instructor-highlighting result (Ponce 2022) and the AI-assistance result (Chen 2025) point to using the column to *direct attention* rather than replace the reader's own annotation, and to keeping the reader's own notes clearly distinct from machine notes.
- **Untested.** Whether machine margin notes help experts, or become the automated-notes pattern that scored lowest.

### Quotes: the lines worth keeping

How it works today: a list of the article's own sentences, verified verbatim and marked in the prose; the reader's own highlights are separate rows, labelled as theirs, not merged with the model's ([quotes.md](../../project/quotes.md)).

- **Supports.** Model-chosen quotes work like instructor-provided highlighting, which did better than learner-generated in the one meta-analysis. Keeping reader and model rows apart protects the reader's own selection.
- **Suggests a change.** Nothing strongly. One idea: let the reader pick first and see the model's choices after, so selecting stays generative. Untested.
- **Untested.** Whether model highlighting helps domain experts, and whether it reduces the reader's own attention to the text.

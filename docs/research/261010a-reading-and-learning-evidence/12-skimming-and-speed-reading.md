# Skimming, multiple passes, speed reading and RSVP

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: when someone has too much to read, what can they safely skip, how fast can they go
before understanding suffers, and does going round a piece more than once pay for itself? The short
answer: there is a real trade-off between speed and understanding, and no trick removes it. Skimming
is a different activity from fast reading. It reliably keeps the main ideas and loses details and
inferences, and it works best when the reader can steer towards what matters. Presenting words one at
a time (RSVP, the "Spritz" style) takes away the two things that let a reader recover from
misunderstanding, looking ahead and looking back, and most of the evidence says that costs
comprehension. Rereading straight away adds little. Rereading after a gap helps later, but the best
evidence for that is on students, not experts. And nobody has yet shown, with a proper experiment,
that any named multi-pass method for papers (Keshav's three passes, SQ3R) beats just reading.

A note on the evidence: most of the papers below could only be opened as abstracts, because the
publishers block automated access. Each row says which. Where I could read a full text or an
author's preprint, I say so.

## The short answer

- Silent reading of English non-fiction by adults runs at about 238 words per minute (most adults
  175 to 300), and reading for recall is slower, about 100 wpm in the studies reviewed; commonly quoted
  higher figures are overestimates. (**High certainty · Direct**)
- Doubling or tripling speed without losing comprehension is unlikely; skimming gets through text
  faster "with moderate comprehension" if full understanding is not the goal. (**Moderate certainty ·
  Indirect**)
- Skimming keeps the important ideas and loses detail and inference. A skimmer's attention follows
  importance, and the benefit depended on being able to navigate between parts. (**Moderate certainty
  · Indirect**)
- Stopping readers from looking back (no regressions) hurt comprehension even on unambiguous
  sentences. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- RSVP (words one at a time in one spot) lowered literal comprehension at about 225 wpm in one study
  and at 400 to 450 wpm in another, but a larger study found no loss at 250 to 350 wpm. The effect is
  speed-dependent and not fully settled. (**Contested · Indirect**)
- Rereading straight away did little for retention in four experiments. Rereading after a gap helped
  on a delayed test in college students, but a classroom replication with 7th-graders found no
  advantage at one week. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Scientists, by self-report, now read more articles per year but spend less time on each (48 minutes
  in 1977, 31 in 2005); nothing I found measures *how* they skim. (**Low certainty · Direct**)
- SQ3R and PQ4R: I found no rigorous trial. Keshav's three-pass method is a practitioner essay with
  no data. (**Low certainty · Direct**)

## The evidence

### How fast people read, and what a "pass" costs

Brysbaert (2019) pooled 190 studies and 18,573 adults. Silent reading of English non-fiction averaged
238 wpm and fiction 260 wpm; for non-fiction most adults fall between 175 and 300 wpm (abstract, and
full text via the author's OSF preprint). The same text adds two points useful here. First, the
"normal" figure of 300 wpm repeated in the eye-movement literature, including the Rayner et al.
(2016) review, was an overestimate. Second, reading for *recall* is slower, about 100 wpm, and the
paper attributes that to "increased structuring and organization of text information, as can be
concluded from the many regressions and rereadings observed". Population: adults without reading
problems, mostly undergraduates; materials mostly short passages. **Caution:** that is a statement
about time spent while learning, not evidence that more time causes better learning.

Carver (1992) proposed five "gears": recall (140 wpm), recognition (200), reading out of interest
(300), skimming (450) and scanning (650). I could not open Carver; I know the scheme only from
Brysbaert's discussion (above), which found no evidence for gears other than the contrast between
*reading* and *scanning for a word*, and says whether skimming is its own gear "is less clear". It
suggests skimming may be alternating bouts of skipping and normal reading, so you find the
information only if it falls in a part you actually read. (**Low certainty · Indirect**)

### The speed–accuracy trade-off

Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman (2016, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) is
the main review of speed reading. (Abstract only, via OpenAlex and Europe PMC.) Its central finding is a trade-off between speed and
accuracy: readers are unlikely to double or triple their speed and still understand as well as at a
normal pace. If full understanding is not the goal, skimming gets through text faster with moderate
comprehension. Keeping comprehension at higher speeds comes mainly from practice and language skill
such as vocabulary. It is a narrative expert review, not a meta-analysis. Secondary coverage says it also argues that regressions are
useful rather than a flaw; I could not check that in the full text.

Training studies are scarce. Klimovich, Tiffin-Richards and Richter (2023, *Journal of Research in
Reading*; abstract only) tested 30 German undergraduates before and after commercial app-based
speed-reading training, metacognitive training, or nothing. Reading speed rose more with either
training than with none, "though not to the extent the commercial program claims"; the gain came
from fewer and shorter fixations; comprehension did not differ between the groups. The sample is
small, there was no follow-up, and the abstract gives no effect sizes. It cuts both ways: speed rose
and comprehension did not fall, but with 10 or so per group a modest loss would not show.
(**Low certainty · Indirect**)

### Skimming: what is kept, what is lost

| | Duggan & Payne 2009 | Masson 1982 |
|---|---|---|
| Design | 3 experiments, expository texts, time for only half of each document; skim vs read first half or second half | Timed recognition tests, narrative and newspaper stories read at 225 to 600 wpm; some text in alternating case to disrupt word shapes |
| Population | Not given in the abstract (university participants, I infer) | Not given in the abstract |
| Outcome | Skimming beat reading half the text for *important* ideas; not for less important details or inferences. No advantage over reading the first or second half of every paragraph (Exp. 2). In website-like layouts, the advantage depended on the links between pages, that is, on how easily readers could navigate | Even at 600 wpm readers formed a gist ("macrostructure") with most of the important information and few details; surface memory fell as rate rose |
| Effect sizes | Not in abstract | Not in abstract |
| Source read | Abstract only (OpenAlex) | Abstract only (OpenAlex) |

Duggan and Payne (2009, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*) also tracked eyes in
Experiment 2: skimmers spent longer on text earlier in the paragraph, higher on the page, and in
earlier pages, which they read as "satisficing" (reading until what is being gained slows, then
moving on). Their 2011 CHI paper reanalysed the eye data (abstract only): readers could direct
attention to the most important content, which helped later recall of the meaning; the authors
propose readers stay on a section until the rate of new information falls below a threshold. That
proposal is a theory the data fit, not a tested mechanism. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**) The
materials are short expository texts, not research papers.

Masson's conclusion is that skimming uses the same processes as normal reading, especially for the
gist, with the surface detail limited, so skimmers lean on general knowledge and prediction. That
matters for experts: a reader who already knows the field is the best-placed skimmer. (**Low certainty
· Indirect**; the study is 1982 and I only read the abstract.)

### Looking back: regressions

Schotter, Tran and Rayner (2014, *Psychological Science*; abstract only) used a "trailing mask": each
word was hidden once the eyes moved past it, so every word could be read once and looking back was
impossible. Comprehension suffered, and not only on ambiguous sentences. The authors conclude that
regressions help understanding and doubt apps that remove eye movements. I could not read N or effect
sizes. Population: adult readers of sentences and short texts. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**.)
The earlier note in this repo's research folder that "10 to 15 per cent of saccades go backwards" comes from the
[prior reading-science note](../260828b-decorated-mode/ideas-reading-science.md) and I did not
re-verify it.

### RSVP and Spritz-style apps, updated to 2026

| | Benedetto et al. 2015 | Di Nocera, Ricciardi & Juola 2018 | Acklin & Papesh 2017 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Design | Between subjects, Spritz-like RSVP vs ordinary page reading | Six groups: page reading, RSVP at 250, 300, 350, 400, 450 wpm | Static text vs RSVP at 700 and 1,000 wpm; 6th- and 12th-grade texts; working memory measured |
| N, population | 60 French adults (mean age 28), median education bachelor's | 209 | Not stated in abstract record |
| Material | First chapter of a novel (Orwell, in French) | Not stated | Not stated |
| Speed | RSVP nominal 250 wpm, actual about 225; page readers averaged 200 wpm, RSVP 209 (no difference) | As listed | As listed |
| Outcome | Literal comprehension lower with RSVP, F(1,55)=7.30, p=.009, partial eta-squared .12; inferential comprehension no difference; fewer blinks; felt more demanding | No comprehension difference at 250 to 350 wpm; lower at 400 and 450 | Static beat both RSVP speeds; slower RSVP better for verbatim, faster for inferential |
| Read | **Full text** (publisher's author copy) | Abstract only | Abstract only |
| Grade | **Moderate · Indirect** | **Moderate · Indirect** | **Low · Indirect** |

Two things are clear and one is not. The clear things: RSVP does not buy comprehension at higher
speeds, and Spritz's own claim that it reduces eye strain was not supported (the blinking was
reduced, self-rated fatigue did not differ). The not-clear thing: whether RSVP is harmless at
ordinary speeds. Benedetto found a literal-comprehension loss at about 225 wpm, in a single chapter
of a novel read by 60 people; Di Nocera found no loss up to 350 wpm in 209 people. The two used
different materials and measures, so I cannot call either right. The sensible reading is "no
reliable gain at any speed; a possible loss; a clear loss above about 350 wpm". (**Contested ·
Indirect**)

I found no 2023 to 2026 evidence that RSVP or speed-reading tools work for comprehension. The 2023
Klimovich result above is the newest peer-reviewed training result I could verify.

### Rereading, immediately and after a gap

- Callender and McDaniel (2009, *Contemporary Educational Psychology*; abstract only): four
  experiments, textbook chapters or a *Scientific American* article, read for learning, then
  multiple-choice, short-answer and summary assessments. "With only several exceptions" rereading did
  not significantly raise performance; reading comprehension ability did not change that. Their
  conclusion is that *immediate* rereading may have little or no benefit for educationally realistic
  tests. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**; undergraduates.)
- Rawson and Kintsch (2005, *Journal of Educational Psychology*; abstract only): two experiments, 423
  college students, a text read once, twice in a row ("massed"), or twice a week apart
  ("distributed"). On an immediate test massed rereading beat one reading and distributed did not; on
  a delayed test distributed rereading beat one reading and massed did not. So the benefit depends on
  when you test. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Greving and Richter (2019, *Frontiers in Psychology*; abstract only): preregistered classroom
  version, 191 seventh-graders, massed vs distributed rereading, tested after 4 minutes or 1 week.
  Massed was better at 4 minutes; at one week the two groups did not differ. Distributed rereading
  felt harder and students predicted lower success. This is the contrary result: the spacing benefit
  did not show up against massed reading. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**; children.)
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013, *Psychological Science in the Public
  Interest*; abstract only) rate rereading and highlighting as low utility ("these techniques do not
  consistently boost students' performance") and practice testing as high.

### How scientists actually read

Tenopir, King, Edwards and Wu (2009, *Aslib Proceedings*; abstract), and Tenopir and King (2008,
D-Lib Magazine, full text), are repeated questionnaire surveys of university science faculty since 1977.
The average number of readings a year rose and time per reading fell, from 48 minutes in 1977 to 31
in 2005 (annual reading time about 120 to 144 hours). Browsing as a share of readings fell from 58.5%
to 33.9% while searching rose from 0.7% to 23.1%. By 2005, 59.5% of readings were electronic, and 37.8% of readings were rated "absolutely
essential". 2005 survey Ns are about 884 to 924 (the article is inconsistent; the 1993 survey had
n=70). Tenopir et al. (2015, *Learned Publishing*; abstract) surveyed faculty at five US universities
in 2012: about 20.7 articles a month, 32 minutes each, 76% of readings electronic. These are
self-reports of the *last article read* (the "critical incident" method), not observed behaviour, and "reading" is not
split into skimming and close reading. The D-Lib article states it does not measure skimming.
(**Low certainty · Direct**)

A 2022 ETRA pilot, posted to arXiv (Mahanama et al., a conference pilot, N not stated on the page I opened), tried eye
tracking with researchers from different fields. I treat it as a signpost, not evidence. I found no
peer-reviewed observational study of how expert readers skim research papers.

### Named multi-pass methods

Keshav (2007, "How to read a paper", *ACM SIGCOMM Computer Communication Review* 37(3); full text of
the two-page essay) is a **practitioner essay**, not research, and cites no experiment. First pass,
5 to 10 minutes: title, abstract, introduction, headings, conclusions, glance at references, then the
"five Cs" (category, context, correctness, contributions, clarity). Second pass, up to an hour: read
with care, ignore proofs, study the figures. Third pass, 4 to 5 hours for beginners and about an
hour for experts: "virtually re-implement" the paper. It is a sensible design that matches what skim
research implies (gist first, detail where it matters), but it is untested.

SQ3R (Survey, Question, Read, Recite, Review) and PQ4R: my searches found only small, mostly
non-randomised studies from local journals, none of which I opened or can recommend. The one
rigorous-looking comparison I located, Jairam and others (2013, *Instructional Science*, SOAR versus
SQ3R), I could not read (not even an abstract), so it is under "Not verified". Dunlosky 2013 does not rate SQ3R as a package, and its best-supported
ingredient is practice testing (the "recite" step). (**Low certainty · Indirect**)

### Generative AI and skimming: recent and thin

- Etkin and colleagues (2025, *Frontiers in Education*; preregistered, within-subject, **195** US
  18 to 22 year olds via Prolific; ACT reading passages, where an AI summary *replaced* the passage).
  Participants who scored low on the control passage did better with a summary (44.9% to 55.4%,
  d=0.45); those who scored high did worse (82.5% to 66.5%, d=0.83). Splitting by SAT/ACT instead,
  low scorers showed no effect and high scorers still did worse (d=0.35). Splitting by performance on
  the *same* control condition is exposed to regression to the mean, so the first pair is overstated.
  I read the full open-access page. (**Low certainty · Analogy**) Its relevance: a summary that stands
  in for the text hurts strong readers on a comprehension quiz.
- Fok and colleagues (2023, IUI conference; arXiv "Scim") built an interface that highlights salient
  content in papers for skimming, after interviews, a usability study and a deployment. It reports no
  comprehension outcome I could verify. **Conference paper, not peer-reviewed in the journal sense; not
  load-bearing.**

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- Almost all skimming and speed studies use students and short passages, with immediate comprehension
  questions. Experts reading in their own field, with the prior knowledge that helps skimming and the
  need to evaluate rather than recall, are not studied.
- Most key papers here are abstract only. Effect sizes for Duggan and Payne, Schotter, Masson, Di
  Nocera and Acklin are not in what I read.
- RSVP results disagree on the threshold, and the 2015 and 2018 studies differ in materials, so the
  contradiction is unresolved.
- Rereading and spacing: Rawson and Kintsch (college) and Greving and Richter (7th grade) point
  in different directions at one week.
- The "reading for recall = 100 wpm" figure is a rate, and includes regressions that are
  effort, not necessarily a mechanism that causes learning.
- No study I opened measures what a skimmer *thinks they understood* against what they did (calibration)
  in expert readers.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Skim

How it works today: [skim.md](../../project/skim.md) describes a route through the stored quotes at
increasing depth, "a handful of stops the first time round, about a dozen the second, a larger share
of the piece the third", where stops "need not come in the paper's order — the results first, say, and
then a quick tour of the methods". Each stop shows a cue saying what to look for, and a Next-stop door.

**Supports.** The three-depth idea is the shape that skim research and Keshav's essay both imply: gist
first, details later. Duggan and Payne found skimming kept the important ideas, and that the benefit
depended on how easily readers could navigate. Skim's stepping keys, next-stop door and stop-by-stop
location answer that second finding. Showing the quote in place in the real prose, rather than a
summary replacing it, fits Etkin's warning that summaries standing in for text hurt strong readers.

**Suggests a change (proposal).** Duggan and Payne say skimmers lose "less important details" and
inferences. If the aim is that the reader still knows what they have not read, the deeper passes could
say plainly which kinds of content a pass leaves out (methods detail, caveats). The "cue, not
finding" design is also consistent with the lesson that readers attend to importance; I would test
whether cues about what to *look for* beat cues about what the stop *is*.

**Untested.** Whether a model-chosen route beats a reader's own skim, and whether three passes
beat a single careful read, for expert adults. Keshav's method is also untested. A before/after
comprehension probe would answer it.

### Reading time

How it works today: [reading-time.md](../../project/reading-time.md) draws a tinted area down the
spine where the reader has spent longer, "so a reader who has been thrown around the article … can find
the place they had got to".

**Supports.** Time is the reader's own spent effort, not a score of understanding, so it makes no
comprehension claim, which matters because Brysbaert's rates vary by person and text.

**Suggests a change (proposal).** Dwell time cannot tell reading from skimming from scanning, though
they differ (Brysbaert separates reading from scanning for a word, with different fixation patterns).
A very short dwell on a passage could mean "skimmed", and the spine could say so differently from
"not seen". That is a product idea, not a finding. The estimate that 238 wpm is typical is reasonable
for a time-to-read figure, but see
[261005a](../261005a-reading-time-estimates-and-text-difficulty.md), which already maps it.

**Untested.** Whether showing "where you've been" changes revisiting or catches skipped parts.

### A speed-read or RSVP mode

Not built, and the evidence supports leaving it unbuilt: no reliable gain, possible literal-comprehension
loss, a clear loss above about 350 wpm, and it removes looking back, which Schotter et al. found helps
understanding. This matches the earlier note in
[ideas-reading-science.md](../260828b-decorated-mode/ideas-reading-science.md) that RSVP be refused.
Nothing since 2016 changes that verdict.

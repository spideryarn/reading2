# Generating it yourself versus being given it

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

Spideryarn writes summaries, outlines, FAQs and pictures *for* the reader. The learning-science
literature says that producing something yourself (a summary, an explanation, a map) usually beats
being handed the finished thing, with a typical advantage of about half a standard deviation. But
that advantage is a laboratory average over mostly students, mostly short materials and mostly
memory tests, and it shrinks, vanishes or reverses under several conditions that matter here: when
the learner is a novice, when the task is so open that nothing useful gets generated, when the
reader needs a map before they can read, and when what is being generated is a plain summary. The
short answer for Spideryarn: the evidence supports keeping the *reader's own response* (Recall,
Explore, comments) at the centre, and supports treating provided summaries and structure as a
scaffold to be read against the text, not instead of it. It does not show that a reader should be
made to write their own summary of an article first. Almost none of it was tested on expert adults
reading research papers.

## The short answer

- Prompting people to explain material to themselves helps learning: g = 0.55 over 69 effect sizes, and for studying text g = 0.79 (wide interval). This is the best-supported "generate it yourself" result we found. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Self-explanation beat being *given* the same explanation, but by less: g = 0.35 against instructor-supplied explanations, g = 0.67 against nothing extra. Most of the benefit was available by being given an explanation; generating added a smaller extra. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- The classic generation effect (producing an item beats reading it) is real, about 0.40 across 86 studies, but it comes from word-level memory tasks; the less constrained the generating, the larger the effect. It says little about understanding a paper. (**High certainty · Analogy**)
- ICAP, the idea that interactive beats constructive beats active beats passive, is influential but its strict ordering is not well established. The original authors say it is a "gross first cut", and a 2023 critique found the key supporting studies weak. (**Contested · Indirect**)
- Receiving can beat generating for novices: worked examples help (g = 0.48 in a mathematics meta-analysis, summary page only) and guidance beat self-learning in one cited classroom study. The reverse for experts (the expertise reversal effect) is a standard claim, but we could not open its source this session. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)
- Writing your own summary is rated low-utility for students; self-explanation is rated moderate. Generated products are not equal: the strategy that wins is the one that makes you do inferential work, not the one that yields the nicest artefact. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- In the one study of retrieving against building a concept map, retrieval won on later tests, including a test that was itself concept mapping. "Constructive" is not automatically the best kind of work. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Generative AI that hands over the answer reduces effort and can reduce learning: a preregistered classroom trial found students with an unguarded GPT-4 scored 17% lower once it was removed, while a tutor that withheld answers did not harm. A university study found a chatbot lowered cognitive load and lowered the quality of reasoning versus web search. Neither is a reading study. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

## The evidence

### Does generating beat receiving? The generation effect

**Bertsch et al. 2007** meta-analysed 445 effect sizes from 86 studies of the generation effect, the finding that people remember information they produce (for example a synonym) better than information they merely read. The overall effect was 0.40, "almost half a standard deviation", with substantial variability across the 11 moderators they tested (abstract only; the moderators are not named in the abstract). The classic experiments used single words with an immediate test (Slamecka and Graf 1978 is the origin, but we could not read it; see Not verified).

**McCurdy et al. 2020** meta-analysed 126 articles (310 experiments, 1,653 estimates) to test seven theories of the generation effect. They report that *generation constraint*, meaning how restricted the learner is in what they can produce, significantly moderates the effect, and a companion experiment series found that *fewer* constraints gave larger gains in item and source memory through more relational processing (abstract and record only; no effect sizes in the abstract). Plainly: asking someone to complete a nearly determined fragment is a weak generate task; asking them to make something open that has to connect to what they know is stronger.

Population and materials in this literature are mostly undergraduates and word or short-phrase lists, tested by recall or recognition. Applying it to understanding a 30-page paper is an extrapolation. (**High certainty on the effect existing · Analogy to Spideryarn's readers**)

### Self-explanation: the best-supported form for text

Self-explanation means generating inferences for yourself about *why* or *how*, as you study. **Bisra et al. 2018** (full text read) meta-analysed 69 effect sizes from 64 reports, N = 5,917, random effects: g = 0.55, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.65, with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 65%). The fail-safe N was 6,028, so publication bias alone is unlikely to erase it, though a fail-safe N is a weak check. Key rows:

| Row | N | k | g | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overall | 5,917 | 69 | 0.55 | 0.45 to 0.65 |
| Compared with no extra explanation | 3,141 | 41 | 0.67 | 0.52 to 0.83 |
| Compared with instructional (supplied) explanation | 573 | 6 | 0.35 | 0.18 to 0.53 |
| Compared with another strategy | 516 | 7 | 0.30 | 0.13 to 0.48 |
| Primary task: study text | 772 | 14 | 0.79 | 0.49 to 1.09 |
| Primary task: solve problems | 1,415 | 20 | 0.48 | 0.29 to 0.66 |
| Undergraduate | 3,349 | 42 | 0.61 | 0.47 to 0.75 |
| Professional programme | 304 | 4 | 0.68 | 0.11 to 1.25 |

Three things to take from it. First, the authors themselves read the comparison with instructional explanation as showing that "a substantial portion of the benefit ... appears to be alternately available from instructor-provided explanations": giving the explanation closes most of the gap. Second, the comparison with supplied explanations rests on only 6 comparisons and 573 people. Third, most studies did not report time on task for each group, so some of the benefit may be extra time; where it was reported and groups differed, the self-explanation group took longer in 16 of 22 coded comparisons. We did not check whether the pooled effect separates immediate from delayed tests, so delayed retention is not established by this row. Population: elementary to professional-programme learners, with undergraduates the largest group; text studies were typically short passages. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**: adults, text, but not research papers and not experts reading in their own field.)

Dunlosky et al. 2013 rated self-explanation "moderate utility", noting limited testing in real educational settings (abstract only).

### Generative strategies as a family, and by age

**Fiorella and Mayer 2016** present eight generative strategies (summarising, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting) grounded in Wittrock's and Mayer's models of comprehension, with boundary conditions for each (abstract only via ERIC; we did not open the per-strategy effect sizes, so none are quoted here). It is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis. **Brod 2021** reviews six strategies by age and concludes all six have proven effective for university students, but that drawing and questioning are largely ineffective before secondary school (abstract only). **Breitwieser and Brod 2021** found that children aged 9 to 11 remembered more after generating predictions than examples, whereas adults benefited similarly from both (N = 50, abstract only). The adult reading of this: for university-level readers, the form of generative activity matters less than for children. (**Low-to-Moderate certainty · Indirect**)

### Does the ICAP ordering hold?

**Chi and Wylie 2014** (full text read) classify learning behaviours into Interactive, Constructive, Active and Passive, and predict learning rises in that order. Their evidence is a review of studies of note-taking, concept mapping and self-explanation. They list factors that can override the prediction: shallow assessment measures cannot show differences ("performance on easy multiple-choice questions was the same for all levels"), simple arbitrary domains (the English article system), and topics too hard for the learner to bootstrap. **Chi et al. 2018** (abstract read) tried to teach teachers to apply ICAP over five years: teachers had "minimal success" designing constructive and interactive activities, but students did learn more in constructive than active lessons.

**Thurn et al. 2023** is a critical comment (full text read). It argues that overt behaviour is a poor proxy for covert thinking and that the I > C > A > P ordering has never been systematically tested. It cites the key Menekse et al. 2013 studies (42 and 120 undergraduate engineering students) as methodologically weak, a study (Conley et al. 2022) where passive tasks were more engaging than active ones, and a sixth-grade algebra study where direct instruction beat carefully designed self-learning materials. It reports no new effect sizes. (**Contested · Indirect**) Chi's own caveats are the practical content: the benefit of generating depends on the learner being able to do it and the test being deep enough to detect it.

### When receiving beats generating

The worked-example effect, that studying a solved problem beats solving it, is a core cognitive-load finding for novices. **Barbieri et al. 2023** meta-analysed 55 studies (181 effect sizes) from primary school to post-secondary mathematics and found g = 0.48 for worked examples over problem solving, with correct examples better than incorrect ones; adding self-explanation prompts on top had a *negative* moderating effect (publisher summary page, not the paper). The related expertise reversal effect, that guidance which helps novices becomes redundant or harmful for experts (Kalyuga et al. 2003), would be the key Spideryarn caveat, but we only verified its citation record. Read together with Thurn et al.'s sixth-grade result, the safe statement is: for a learner meeting unfamiliar material, being given a good model usually beats flailing at generation; for a learner with a schema, generation tends to cost less and pay more. This is mostly mathematics and procedural learning. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**)

### Not all generated artefacts are equal

**Karpicke and Blunt 2011** (abstract read; university students, science-education texts) found retrieval practice produced more meaningful learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping, including on inference questions and a final test that was itself concept mapping. Building a concept map is constructive in ICAP terms; recall without notes beat it. **Dunlosky et al. 2013** rated summarisation and highlighting low utility: it "has helped some students on some tasks" but benefits are limited (abstract only). So "the reader makes something" is not the active ingredient; effortful retrieval and inference are.

### Generative AI and the "doing it for you" problem

**Bastani et al. 2025** (full text read) randomised classrooms (not students) in a Turkish high school: control, GPT Base (a ChatGPT-like GPT-4 interface) and GPT Tutor (teacher-written solutions and hints, step-by-step guidance). N about 1,000 students in about 50 classes, grades 9 to 11, preregistered primary analysis. Practice performance: GPT Base +0.137 (SE 0.031), about +48%; GPT Tutor +0.361, about +127%. Closed-book exam: GPT Base -0.054 (SE 0.022), about -17% against control; GPT Tutor -0.004, no difference. Students in the unguarded condition did not think they had learned less. Authors' limits: one school, one subject, one term, short-term outcomes. The article has been corrected (PNAS, August 2025); the text we read does not say what changed, so treat the figures as provisional. (**Moderate certainty · Analogy**: school mathematics.)

**Stadler et al. 2024** (full text read) randomised 91 university students (mean age 22.3) to research nanoparticles in sunscreen with ChatGPT-3.5 or Google. LLM users reported lower cognitive load (extraneous, intrinsic and germane; all p < .01, eta-squared 0.09 to 0.27) and wrote worse justifications (mean 1.20 against 1.87, eta-squared 0.11). Short task, one topic, no delayed test. (**Low-to-Moderate certainty · Indirect**: adults, scientific topic, but searching and writing, not reading.)

Together: if the AI does the generating, the learner does less of it, and the cost shows up on a later unaided test or on reasoning quality. Neither study tests a reader being shown a summary alongside the full article.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Provided can be nearly as good.** Bisra et al. show supplied explanations recover much of the self-explanation benefit; the extra from generating is about 0.35 and rests on six comparisons.
- **Time on task is a confound.** Generating takes longer. Few studies equated time.
- **The generation effect is a word-list effect.** Its transfer to comprehension of long prose is not shown in anything we opened.
- **ICAP's ordering is a prediction, not an established law** (Thurn 2023). Chi and Wylie concede the dependence on test depth, domain and learner ability.
- **Constructive is not always best.** Retrieval beat concept mapping (Karpicke and Blunt 2011); worked examples beat problem solving for novices; direct instruction beat self-learning in a cited classroom study.
- **Experts are almost absent.** The only "professional" cell in Bisra is four studies and 304 people with an interval from 0.11 to 1.25.
- **Perceived ease misleads.** In Bastani et al., students who learned less did not feel they had.
- **Citation uncertainties.** Several sources could only be read as abstracts or summary pages; see Sources.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Summary (Brief, Fuller, Thread)

Today: model-written paragraphs, each linking to the passages it rests on, deliberately not an outline ([summaries.md](../../project/summaries.md)). It is a received artefact, so in ICAP terms passive unless the reader works with it. **Supports:** the design choice that every sentence is a door into the text, and the "no compression instead of reading" stance in [vision.md](../../project/vision.md). The AI studies (Bastani, Stadler) are the reason to worry about a reader using the summary as the answer; the worked-example work is the reason a summary is fine as an orientation for a reader new to the topic. **Suggests a change (proposal, untested):** an optional "say what you think this is about" prompt before the summary appears, for readers who are expert in the field. Evidence for it is the generation and expertise-reversal logic, which we could not confirm for prose; no study here tests it. **Untested:** whether showing a summary *before* reading helps or hurts later reading of a paper.

### Structure

Today: the article's tree drawn in the band, as a fisheye or expanded view ([structure.md](../../project/structure.md)). Generated by the model, not the reader. **Supports:** receiving a map is a scaffold and not the thing a learner would otherwise have to build while reading; nothing found argues for making readers build the outline. **Untested:** whether readers who draw or edit the tree themselves would learn more; mapping is one of the eight generative strategies (Fiorella and Mayer 2016) but we found no effect sizes for adult text readers, and Karpicke and Blunt's concept-map result is a caution.

### FAQ

Today: up to twelve questions a careful reader would ask, each answered only by the piece's own passages, ranked by centrality and difficulty ([faq.md](../../project/faq.md)). **Supports:** keeping answers tied to the author's passages means the reader still reads. **Untested:** whether having the reader write their own questions first, then comparing, helps; Brod 2021 lists questioning as effective for university students but we only read the abstract. A proposal: let the reader pick or write a question before seeing the list.

### Learn: Recall, Quiz and Explore

Today: the reader says or types what they took from the piece and the model corrects and nudges; Quiz asks short questions; Explore helps them push their own thinking ([learn-mode.md](../../project/learn-mode.md), [quiz.md](../../project/quiz.md)). Greg's stated reason it is not the anti-goal is structural: "the reader has to have read the piece before they can use it at all." **Supports:** this is the strongest fit with the evidence. Recall is constructive generation plus feedback, and retrieval beat concept-mapping in Karpicke and Blunt; self-explanation prompts have the best-supported effect on text. **Suggests a change:** none from this file. Note that prompting for explanation (why and how) rather than recall alone is what the meta-analysis tested, so an Explore turn that asks "why does this follow?" matches the evidence better than "what did it say?" **Untested:** the AI half. In Bisra et al. the prompts were scripted; no study here tests an adaptive model's feedback on a reader's own account. Bastani's guided tutor suggests withholding answers matters.

### Sketch and diagram

Today: a model's drawing of the argument's shape ([sketch.md](../../project/sketch.md)). **Supports:** nothing directly. **Untested:** drawing is a generative strategy for the reader, and Brod 2021 finds it ineffective for young children, but we found no evidence on adults drawing arguments; provided pictures versus drawn ones is unmeasured here.

### Comments

Today: the reader's bookmark or note on a passage, AI optional ([comments.md](../../project/comments.md)). **Supports:** a note in your own words is a small constructive act, and making the AI response optional keeps generation with the reader. **Untested:** whether prompting a note produces self-explanation.

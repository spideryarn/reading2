# Maps, diagrams and timelines: making them versus looking at them

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: do concept maps, argument maps, graphic organisers, drawings and timelines help people understand and remember what they read, and does it matter whether the reader *builds* the picture or is *handed* one? The short answer is that it matters a great deal. The strongest results are for pictures the learner builds, usually with guidance, and the best-known rival (retrieval practice) beat concept mapping on a delayed test. Pictures that are handed over help modestly and sometimes only when you view them in the right way. Almost none of it concerns expert adults reading research papers, and almost none concerns pictures a machine drew.

## The short answer

- Learning with concept maps beats comparison conditions on average, and *constructing* a map is associated with a bigger benefit than *studying* one (g = 0.72 vs 0.43 in the largest meta-analysis). That is a between-study moderator, not a clean experiment, and the studies were mostly students, not experts. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- When the same text is studied, a reader-built map is not reliably better than a ready-made one: in three experiments, author-provided graphic organisers beat learner-built ones on transfer and tied on retention. (**Low certainty · Indirect**: undergraduates, short passage, one lab group)
- Concept mapping lost to retrieval practice (free recall) on a one-week delayed test, including on inference questions and even when the final test was itself a concept map. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Argument mapping teaches critical thinking, but the effect sizes are inflated by weak designs and publication bias: a 2026 meta-analysis gives g = 1.59 unadjusted and 0.87 adjusted, from only 14 studies. The training is intensive and about *constructing* maps, not viewing them. (**Low certainty · Indirect**)
- Drawing beats just reading, but is mixed against other model-focused strategies or viewing a supplied illustration, and depends on guidance. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- For viewing maps, the best direct evidence is small: reading a ready-made argument map improved immediate recall of arguments over plain text, but a recall benefit after a delay was not shown in the abstract. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- There is no good evidence on timelines helping comprehension of a text for adult readers, nor on machine-drawn maps improving learning. One tiny conference study (N = 14) of LLM concept maps found lower perceived load and no accuracy gain. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)

## The evidence

### Concept maps: the meta-analyses

**Nesbit & Adesope 2006** pooled 67 effect sizes from 55 studies (5,818 participants, Grade 4 to postsecondary; science, psychology, statistics, nursing). Studies covered learners building, modifying or viewing node-link diagrams; outcomes were recall and transfer. Maps were associated with better retention across settings, effect sizes ran "small to large" depending on use and comparator, and most subsets were significantly heterogeneous (abstract only, via the author's page). Quasi-experiments were included.

**Schroeder, Nesbit, Anguiano & Adesope 2018** updated this: 142 effect sizes, 11,814 participants, random-effects model, overall g = 0.58. Creating maps (g = 0.72) beat studying maps (g = 0.43); concept-map learning also beat other instructional comparisons, in STEM and non-STEM (abstract and a repository summary; I could not open the full text, so I cannot report heterogeneity, publication-bias checks or delay). Crucially, "creating" and "studying" are compared across *different* studies, so differences in learners, texts and comparators are mixed in with the construction effect.

**Anastasiou, Wirngo & Bagos 2024**: 55 studies, 5,364 students in Grades 3 to 12, science achievement; g = 0.776 overall, with significant heterogeneity in most subsets (ERIC abstract). Children and school science, so Analogy at best for adult readers.

Population and materials in all three: schoolchildren and undergraduates, short expository passages or whole courses; not adults reading papers in their own field. Because the controls are often "read the text" or lectures, the maps are being compared with a weak baseline.

### Constructing against being handed one

**Stull & Mayer 2007** (three experiments, undergraduates, a short passage; ERIC abstract only): students given an author-provided graphic organiser scored higher on transfer than students who built their own, with the largest gap in the simplest experiment, and no significant difference on retention. The authors read it through cognitive load: building the organiser is itself work that competes with understanding. Effect sizes and Ns were not in the abstract.

**Robinson & Kiewra 1995** (two experiments, 153 college students; ERIC abstract only): given enough time, those who studied *supplied* graphic organisers learned more about hierarchical and coordinate relations and wrote better integrated essays than those given outlines or plain text. So viewing a well-made organiser helped, at least for undergraduates with time.

**Dwyer, Hogan & Stewart 2013** (three experiments, ERIC abstract only): reading *or* building an argument map improved immediate recall compared with outlining, summarising, or plain reading, in both active and passive conditions. The abstract names a delayed-recall factor but reports only the immediate gain, so I will not claim a lasting benefit.

**Montanero & Lucero 2012** (338 undergraduates aged 19 to 20, a history text; ERIC record of an online submission, not verified as peer-reviewed): rewriting the text so its causal and temporal order was clear improved delayed recall, and filling in an incomplete causal diagram also produced effects; the authors suggest building the causal representation may beat receiving a finished organiser. Treat as suggestive.

Taken together: for a fixed text, being handed a good picture is no worse than drawing your own, and sometimes better; the apparent advantage of "constructing" in the meta-analysis may owe much to the retrieval, elaboration and time that construction forces.

### Drawing

**Van Meter & Garner 2005** found that the empirical evidence for learner-generated drawing was inconsistent and that guidance was thin (ERIC abstract). **Fiorella & Zhang 2018** (review, abstract only): drawing consistently beat reading only and text-focused strategies such as summarising for comprehension and transfer, regardless of guidance; against other model-focused strategies, or against viewing instructor-supplied illustrations, results were mixed; drawing is demanding and time-consuming, and students without enough guidance draw poor pictures. Science texts, mostly school and undergraduate readers. A 2025 review by **Dechamps & Skulmowski** extends this (title and metadata verified through Crossref only; not read).

### Concept maps against retrieval practice

**Karpicke & Blunt 2011** (two experiments, about 200 students in the press-release counts; Science; Europe PMC abstract): practising retrieval beat elaborative study with concept mapping on a one-week test of meaning-based learning, on inference questions, and even when the final test required making a concept map. A What Works Clearinghouse summary of the study gives mean test scores of 67% for retrieval, 49% repeated study, 45% concept mapping, and 27% for single study. **O'Day & Karpicke 2021** (J Educ Psychol, two experiments, ERIC summary): retrieval beat concept mapping at one week, and combining the two added nothing over retrieval alone despite much more time. Note that in these studies the map is built with the text open, so this is *constructing* losing, not viewing.

### Argument mapping and critical thinking

**Abrami et al. 2015** (341 effect sizes on standardised critical-thinking tests): the mean across all teaching strategies was g = 0.30, with significant heterogeneity, a benchmark for "typical". **Cullen et al. 2018** (quasi-experimental field study, 12-week undergraduate seminar teaching argument visualisation, Europe PMC abstract): d = 0.71 [0.37, 1.04] on LSAT logical reasoning and d = 0.87 [0.26, 1.48] on blind-scored essays against controls; no random assignment, and the seminar was a taught course. **Nesbit & Liu 2025** systematically reviewed 124 studies (102 empirical) and judged whether the research was ready for meta-analysis (abstract only; the conclusion is not given in the summary I read). **Lin et al. 2026** (14 studies, N = 1,455): g = 1.59 unadjusted fell to 0.87 after publication-bias adjustment; gains larger for essay quality than for standardised critical-thinking tests; scaffolded instruction beat full-course integration (abstract).

The oft-quoted ~0.8 SD for intensive argument mapping courses comes from van Gelder's work, which I could only see second hand (via a book-chapter summary), so it is under Not verified. Alvarez Ortiz's 2007 thesis is also unverified and would be unreviewed.

### Timelines

I found no experimental study of timelines for comprehension in adults. The nearest is Montanero & Lucero (above), where text with clear causal and temporal order helped delayed recall. Practitioner pages recommend timelines for history texts but cite no data.

### Machine-drawn maps

**Han & Choi 2025** (conference paper, not peer-reviewed journal; 14 participants): GPT-4o-mini concept maps of texts from ten disciplines; extraction precision of 83.6% at section level; users reported 31.5% lower perceived cognitive load, took 22.6% more time with the visual, finished comprehension tests 14.1% faster with comparable accuracy. **Chen et al. 2025** (64 sophomores): argument maps plus ChatGPT help improved argumentation behaviours on content analysis and questionnaires; students said maps made structure clear but helped little with gathering information (ERIC abstract; small, uncontrolled for the AI component). No study I found measured delayed retention or transfer after *viewing* an AI-drawn map.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- **Viewing vs constructing is confounded.** The headline 0.72 vs 0.43 comes from different studies. Stull & Mayer's direct comparison runs the other way. Anyone claiming "constructing is better" needs both.
- **Weak comparators.** "Read the text" is a low bar. Against retrieval practice, concept mapping lost.
- **Publication bias in argument mapping**: a nearly halved effect once adjusted (Lin et al. 2026). Quasi-experiments and teacher-delivered intensive courses dominate.
- **Time.** Constructing is slow; combined strategies cost time and returned nothing extra (O'Day & Karpicke).
- **Expertise.** Kalyuga's review of the *expertise reversal effect* says the best instructional method depends on what the learner already knows (ERIC abstract, 2007). That implies scaffolding that helps novices can be redundant for experts, our main readers, but I found no direct test with maps.
- **Calibration.** The press coverage of Karpicke & Blunt says students predicted mapping would work better; I could not verify this in the paper, so it is not evidence here.
- **Accuracy of machine maps.** 83.6% precision means about one in six extracted concepts was not what it should have been, at least in that study.

## What it means for Spideryarn

**Diagram and Sketch.** Diagram shows pictures of the article's structure, and Sketch is "a model's drawing of the shape of the argument" ([diagram.md](../../project/diagram.md), [sketch.md](../../project/sketch.md)); the reader does not build it. **Supports**: handed organisers can help (Robinson & Kiewra; Stull & Mayer), and Greg's ask was a sense of the whole structure, which fits an organiser's orienting role. **Suggests a change** (proposal): the "construction" benefit is not delivered by viewing, so a mode that invites the reader to *predict, redraw or fill in* part of a Sketch, or to recall the argument and then compare it to the drawing, would draw on the better-evidenced mechanisms. **Untested**: whether a model-drawn argument picture helps an expert retain or critique a paper; also whether its errors mislead.

**Structure.** The tree in the band ([structure.md](../../project/structure.md)) is an outline/hierarchy that comes from the pipeline. **Supports** weakly: hierarchical displays aided relational learning in Robinson & Kiewra. **Untested** for experts reading papers, where headings already exist.

**Timeline.** It records "when the piece says these things happened" and refuses to invent dates ([timeline.md](../../project/timeline.md)). **Supports**: temporal-causal clarity helped delayed recall in one undergraduate study. **Untested**: no data on timelines for adults. The design's honesty about what the text claims matters more than any effect we can cite, since the cost of a wrong date or a mis-extracted concept is real.

**Across all of them.** The evidence best supports pictures that are checked against the text and then *used* (recalled from, compared with, completed), not pictures that are simply looked at. That is a proposal, not a finding.

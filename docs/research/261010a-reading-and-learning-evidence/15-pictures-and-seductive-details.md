# Pictures with text: do they help, when do they hurt, and what about AI-made ones?

Up: [reading-research-overview.md](../../project/reading-research-overview.md) · part of the
[reading and learning evidence library](digest.md), 2026-10-10.

The question: when a picture sits beside prose, does the reader learn more, and does it matter
whether the picture explains something, merely decorates, is interesting but off-topic, or is
wrong? The short answer: pictures that carry part of the explanation help, mostly in studies with
students and short lessons; pictures that merely decorate do roughly nothing for learning and may
lift mood; interesting-but-irrelevant material (seductive details) does small-to-moderate harm
on average; and the evidence on **AI-generated** pictures is thin, mostly not peer-reviewed, and
none of it tests whether a *wrong* AI picture misleads a reader. Almost none of this literature
studies expert adults reading research papers.

## The short answer

- Learners given words plus well-made explanatory pictures beat learners given words alone. Mayer's
  review reports a median d = 1.39 across 11 tests, all in the same direction, but those are mostly
  short science lessons for students, and the median is a review author's summary, not a
  pooled estimate with a CI. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- The advantage is claimed to be larger for low-prior-knowledge learners, so expert readers
  may gain less; this is stated in the same chapter but I could not test it against primary data.
  (**Low certainty · Indirect**)
- Seductive details (interesting but irrelevant additions) lower learning on average in the
  meta-analysis literature; the 2020 meta-analysis covers about 58 studies and over 7,500 students
  according to a press summary of it, with most sessions lasting 6 to 12 minutes. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Purely decorative pictures are neither reliably harmful nor helpful for learning. Results range
  from no effect (Lenzner et al. 2013) through a small knowledge benefit in one experiment but not
  its replication (Scherer et al. 2023), to a mood lift. (**Low certainty · Indirect**)
- Emotional design (faces on shapes, warm colours) adds small gains in retention, comprehension and
  transfer (g+ about 0.27 to 0.35) and larger gains in motivation-type outcomes, but the claim is
  contested by a critical review I could only find second-hand. (**Moderate certainty · Indirect**)
- Whether a *misleading* illustration plants a misconception is mostly shown by old observational
  work (Hill 1988) and analogy from misleading videos and charts, not by a direct experiment on
  text plus illustration. (**Low certainty · Analogy**)
- Peer-reviewed tests of AI-generated illustrations are few, small, and lack a human-made-picture
  comparison; the one I read in full cannot attribute any benefit to the pictures being AI-made.
  (**Low certainty · Indirect**)

## The evidence

### Do words plus pictures beat words alone?

- Mayer (2009, "Multimedia Principle", chapter 12 of *Multimedia Learning*) states the principle,
  "people learn better from words and pictures than from words alone", and reports a median
  d = 1.39 over k = 11 tests, with multiple representations better on transfer tests in 11 of 11
  (publisher chapter page). It adds that the principle may matter more for low-knowledge learners.
  This is a book chapter, not a primary study: it is the framing source, not load-bearing.
  **Moderate certainty** (consistent direction, but it is a narrative tally; selection of studies
  and publication bias cannot be judged from it) · **Indirect**.
- Carney and Levin (2002), a review in *Educational Psychology Review*, argue that well-designed
  text illustrations "still" improve learning from text, extending 1970s and 1980s evidence, and
  discuss when, why and for whom (abstract only; no effect sizes in what I could open). Mostly
  textbook-type prose and school learners. **Moderate certainty · Indirect**.
- Butcher (2014) wrote the same-named chapter in the *Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*
  (abstract only: it lays out the cognitive theory of multimedia learning, with separate verbal and
  visual channels, limited capacity and active processing). A theory chapter, not evidence.

Plain words: the theory says a good picture gives you a second, visual way to hold the idea and
costs little extra mental effort if it is placed beside the relevant words. That is why the
benefit is for *explanatory* pictures, not for any picture.

### Contrary: one recent null for rich multimedia

Schmidt et al. (2025), a peer-reviewed brief report, gave N = 65 undergraduates three 10-minute
perception videos in rich, sparse or no-multimedia versions. Multimedia design had no measurable
effect on learning (F(2, 126) = 0.52, p = .60, ηp² = .008), though the rich version scored higher on
engagement (d = 0.50 versus no multimedia). Limits: one topic, short lessons, a video format, and
the "no multimedia" version still showed a title slide. A single small null does not overturn the
principle, but it is a reminder that the principle was largely established in lab lessons.
**Low certainty · Indirect** (video, not reading).

### Seductive details: interesting but irrelevant material

- Harp and Mayer (1998), four experiments (abstract only), students who read expository passages
  with seductive details recalled fewer main ideas and produced fewer problem-solving transfer
  answers. Highlighting, learning objectives, or signalling did not remove the effect; putting the
  seductive material at the *start* made it worse, at the *end* it was reduced. Their reading:
  the details prime the wrong schema, around which readers organise the passage, rather than
  simply distracting. **Moderate certainty · Indirect** (science passages, undergraduate samples).
- Sundararajan and Adesope (2020), a meta-analysis in *Educational Psychology Review*: the
  abstract (opened) concludes that seductive details "can hinder learning" and lists moderators
  (type of picture, delivery format, language, subject, pacing, recall question type) but gives no
  numbers. A university press summary I opened reports 58 studies and over 7,500 students,
  larger harm when the detail was static, on paper, and in social studies and natural sciences; most
  sessions were 6 to 12 minutes; only cognitive outcomes, not affect, were measured. A secondary
  summary quotes g = -0.33; I could not confirm it from the paper (see Not verified).
  **Moderate certainty · Indirect**.
- Rey (2012), *Educational Research Review* 7(3), 216–237, is the earlier meta-analysis and
  review. I verified the citation and that it is a meta-analysis of the seductive detail effect,
  but could not open its abstract or results, so I report none of its numbers (Not verified).
- A reviewer's old objection: the collection of studies was criticised in the 1990s for not giving
  "convincing evidence" for the effect (Goetz and Sadoski, as quoted on a search aggregator page;
  I did not open the commentary). Later meta-analyses answer this, but the objection is a reminder
  that "seductive" is defined by the experimenter, not measured independently.

Who and what: nearly all participants are undergraduates or schoolchildren; materials are short
science explanations (lightning, how a pump works, planetarium shows). Expert readers who already
hold the relevant schema may be less misled, and an expert will also judge "irrelevant" differently.
**Analogy** for research-paper readers.

### Decorative pictures: neutral, or mildly helpful?

- Lenzner, Schnotz and Müller (2013, online 2012; abstract only): three experiments with 7th and
  8th graders compared decorative with instructional pictures. Decorative pictures drew little
  initial attention and were largely ignored; they appeared to improve mood, alertness and calmness,
  did not raise situational interest, and reduced perceived difficulty. They were "neither harmful
  nor helpful" for learning but weakened the benefit of instructional pictures, especially for
  low-prior-knowledge learners. No effect sizes in the abstract. **Low certainty · Indirect**
  (school pupils).
- Scherer, Verkühlen and Dutke (2023), two experiments (full text read at the publisher). Related
  decorative photos added to an 8-page text on self-control for teacher-education students
  (n = 36 vs 35, 20 minutes reading) raised knowledge (83.4% vs 77.4%, ηp² = .075) but not transfer,
  interest or monitoring. In eighth-graders (n = 29 vs 27, text on population growth) there was no
  knowledge or transfer effect (d = 0.14 and 0.19), better monitoring accuracy on knowledge items,
  and *lower* maintained interest with pictures. No retention interval; no unrelated-picture
  condition; small samples; the authors warn the result does not mean decorations always help.
  **Low certainty · Indirect**. This is the closest design to a long-form prose reader in this
  folder, and it replicated in one of two experiments.

### Emotional design: pretty on purpose

- Brom, Stárková and D'Mello (2018), meta-analysis in *Educational Research Review* (abstract only,
  via a bibliographic database): 33 independent samples, N = 2,924, facial anthropomorphisms or
  pleasant colours added to multimedia graphics. Significant positive effects on retention,
  comprehension and transfer; mixed affective effects (robust for intrinsic motivation, weaker for
  enjoyment, marginal for positive affect); no change in perceived learning or effort, lower
  perceived difficulty; no major publication bias; age was the one moderator (larger motivation effect
  for children). **Moderate certainty · Indirect** (mostly students, small illustrated lessons).
- Wong and Adesope (2020), a replication and extension in *Educational Psychology Review*
  (abstract only): mean effects g+ = 0.35 retention (k = 28), 0.27 transfer (k = 38), 0.29
  comprehension (k = 16), 0.15 intrinsic motivation (k = 28). No CIs or bias tests visible to me.
  **Moderate certainty · Indirect**.
- Caution: a search result summarised a critical review (Venni and Désiron) arguing the earlier
  meta-analyses may not generalise; I did not open it (Not verified). The manipulations in these
  studies are faces and colour on instructional graphics, not photographs scattered through an
  article, so the link to decoration is loose.

### Pictures that are wrong or misleading

- Hill (1988), *Research in Science Education* 18, 290–297 (conclusion section only): many
  first-year science students did not read textbook diagrams critically, did not separate the
  diagram from the model it stands for, and gave answers matching the common errors those diagrams
  invite. Observational, not a randomised test, no effect size seen. **Low certainty · Indirect**.
- I did not find, in this session, a peer-reviewed experiment in which a wrong illustration was
  randomly assigned to readers of the same text and misconceptions or false memories were measured.
  A search also surfaced experiments on misleading charts (axis distortions lowered accuracy in 68
  undergraduates) and on explainer videos built on misconceptions (an "illusion of understanding");
  I could not open either full text, so they are leads, not evidence (Not verified).

### AI-made illustrations

- Nothing peer-reviewed that I opened tests static AI illustrations against human-made ones next to
  prose. The relevant gap is the point.
- The one study I read in full: an open-access PLOS ONE experiment (2025) with N = 40 Chinese
  vocational-college students learning 400 English nouns, text-only versus text plus
  AI-made images (Stable Diffusion, Doubao, SenseMirage, "manually reviewed"). Delayed recall at
  48 hours 0.75 vs 0.45 (d = 0.82); the authors state the design cannot attribute the gain to the
  images being AI-made, as there is no human-picture condition; fixed condition order; different word
  sets; no CIs. **Low certainty · Analogy** (L2 vocabulary, not reading comprehension).
- A search summary (not opened) mentions small studies in medical education and an arXiv
  preprint, GenAIReading (digital textbooks with LLM text and image generation), reporting small
  test gains. Preprints are not load-bearing here.
- Teaching-video comparisons (human versus AI-generated) found similar learning (via search summary, not
  opened): suggestive that "AI-made" is not itself the active ingredient, but a different medium.

## Where it is weak, contested or contrary

- Population and materials: undergraduates and schoolchildren, short expository passages, 6–20
  minute sessions, mostly immediate tests. No study found with expert adults reading a research paper.
- "Decorative", "related" and "seductive" are categories the experimenters assign, and the same
  picture may be seductive for a novice and a helpful cue for an expert.
- The multimedia principle is robust in the older literature but a 2025 peer-reviewed null shows
  it can disappear with well-aligned content.
- Seductive-detail meta-analyses report negative effects that are small to moderate and strongest
  with static pictures on paper; they measured cognition only, not whether the details helped a
  reader keep going.
- Two things I could not read, Rey (2012) and the 2020 meta-analysis's numbers, are the most
  central to this topic; a reader wanting the actual effect sizes should open them.
- AI pictures add a risk the literature has not measured: a plausible but wrong picture, which the
  reader (who is told it is an AI picture) may or may not discount.

## What it means for Spideryarn

### Illustrated

How it works today ([illustrated.md](../../project/illustrated.md)): a model reads the article and the
[Sketch](../../project/sketch.md) scene and writes an illustration brief; an image model paints it.
The doc says plainly it "cannot be checked": a genuine quote can be paired with an invented scene. It
carries a visible label, shows the brief behind a `<details>`, keeps Sketch as the diagram of
record, and hands the illustrator the paper's own figures (stage 4.5, see
[article-images.md](../../project/article-images.md)).

- **Supports:** The visible "this is an interpretation" label and showing the brief are the right
  response to a risk the literature leaves unmeasured (a misleading picture; Hill 1988 shows readers
  take diagrams as reality). Putting words in the plate with captions makes the picture more
  representational than decorative, which is the kind that helped.
- **Suggests a change (proposals):** The seductive-details evidence (Harp and Mayer 1998:
  irrelevant material primes the wrong schema, worse when placed first) argues for keeping the
  painted plate *after* the reading rather than as a lead-in, and for not adding decorative scenes
  (the "old-timey map" register is partly aesthetic). If a plate is for orientation, a cue that
  ties each vignette to a block is worth more than ornament. Emotional-design evidence argues the
  other way for motivation and perceived difficulty, so the aesthetic may earn its place, but as
  a motivation tool, not as a learning one. These are for Greg to weigh.
- **Untested:** Whether a painted AI scene helps an expert reader understand or remember a paper;
  whether readers detect a scene that distorts the argument; whether the label is enough.

### Sketch

How it works today ([sketch.md](../../project/sketch.md)): the model writes a structured scene
that is validated against the article's block ids and measured before anything is drawn.

- **Supports:** Pictures that show structure are the kind with the best case (explanatory,
  representational/organisational, with the words beside them). Checking against the article is
  the right defence against a misleading illustration.
- **Suggests a change:** None from this evidence. The multimedia-principle effects are largest for
  low-prior-knowledge readers; for an expert in their own field the gain may be smaller, so it may
  be worth offering Sketch as an optional aid rather than a default.
- **Untested:** Any effect on comprehension, transfer or recall for its actual readers.

### The article's own figures

How it works today ([article-images.md](../../project/article-images.md)): figures and equations the
piece came with are fetched at ingest and served from Spideryarn storage.

- **Supports:** Original figures are the authors' representational and interpretive pictures, the
  type with the best evidence. Keeping them in the reading view rather than stripping them is
  consistent with the evidence.
- **Untested:** Whether readers use them; whether adding AI plates beside them competes or helps.

## Not verified

- Sundararajan and Adesope 2020: overall g = -0.33 and "68 effects from 58 studies" come from
  search-result summaries; the abstract I opened gives no numbers.
- Rey 2012: citation checked against Crossref; abstract, results and any "39 effects" or g ranges
  quoted by secondary sources not opened.
- Levin, Anglin and Carney 1987 (picture functions): not opened. The five-function taxonomy
  (decorative, representational, organisational, interpretive, transformational) is therefore
  not attributed to a source here.
- Misleading-chart experiments, the physics explainer video "illusion of understanding" study,
  Venni and Désiron, the GenAIReading preprint and the AI teaching-video studies: seen only via search
  summaries.
- Butcher 2014 and Mayer 2021 chapters: only abstracts; the d = 1.39 figure is from the 2009
  chapter page.
- No study of false memories created by illustrations was found.

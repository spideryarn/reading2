# Decorated mode — the idea catalogue

**Status:** research, 2026-08-28. Nothing here is decided. A playground exists —
`experiments/decorated/decorated.html`, built by `node experiments/decorated/build.mjs` — that puts
twenty-two of these ideas on a real article, on real artefacts, behind individual toggles.

## What "decorated mode" is

Greg, 2026-08-28:

> The basic idea would be that we are still showing the actual article text, but we're sort of
> decorating it in a way that makes it easier to read, easier to skim, easier to follow the
> argument. So for example we might make critical sentences bigger, or phrases, or highlight them
> some other way. We might make less important stuff smaller or fainter. Perhaps we have callout
> boxes with quotes with particularly important things. Perhaps we include little diagrams showing
> the structure of things. Perhaps we include the headings, even if they're generated by us. We
> should certainly show the glossary underlines and comments — and basically play with how, given
> all of the power we have of LLM models understanding and the richness of HTML and what it can do
> for UIs, let's play with as many different ideas for how we can decorate or augment the article
> text.

**The hard constraint:** not one of the author's words may be removed, reordered or rewritten. What
may change is how it is *displayed*, and what may be *added* around, between, beside and on top of
it. `experiments/decorated/verify.mjs` checks the first half of that against the stored blocks, and
checks itself against a deliberately damaged copy.

**The soft constraint, which is the one that actually decides things:** a decoration must not become
a way of *not* reading. This is [vision.md](../project/vision.md) restated as a UI rule. It knocked
out more ideas below than ugliness did.

### The platform pass arrived after the playground was built

Its three rules are aimed squarely at what the playground does, and two of them it gets wrong:

1. **"Decorate the author's words by painting, never by wrapping spans."** The CSS Custom Highlight
   API styles arbitrary ranges with no DOM surgery, so overlapping glossary / idea / comment / key
   marks stop being an exponential span-splitting problem and selection, copy-paste and find-in-page
   stay correct by construction. All three engines have it (Chrome 105, Safari 17.2, Firefox 140).
   **But `::highlight()` supports only colour, background-colour, text-decoration and text-shadow —
   no font-size, no weight, no spacing.** It cannot do the thing Greg asked for. The playground
   therefore splits spans and pays the price; the right answer is probably both, with the washes
   painted and only the weight and space channels wrapped.
2. **Apparatus must not land in the clipboard.** ✗ **The playground gets this wrong today.** Our
   generated headings, gutter gists and gate prompts are ordinary DOM, so select-all-copy carries
   them out inside what the reader will paste as a quote. Fix: `user-select: none` on every inserted
   node plus a `copy` handler that rebuilds the selection from the blocks. This is the same promise
   `verify.mjs` checks — it just checks it in the wrong direction.
3. **Never wrap prose in `<details>`** — closed content is invisible to Cmd-F outside Chromium.
   ✓ The playground does not.

Its left-field idea is the best of the six: **Confidence Ink** — every mark we draw painted as
hand-drawn ink by a Houdini worklet, with the *steadiness of the stroke* encoding provenance: clean
where the author said it outright, wobbly where we inferred it, seeded from the block id so it is
identical on every reload. It answers the question that runs under this whole catalogue — *can the
reader tell our claims from the author's?* — in a channel that costs no space, no colour budget and
no words. Chromium-only, with a flat fallback.

## The three disagreements

These are the decisions Greg has to make. Each is a toggle on the playground so they can be judged
with prose in front of them rather than in the abstract.

### 1. Should less important text be smaller or fainter? Greg says yes; the reading science says no.

Greg asked for it twice ("make critical sentences bigger", "make less important stuff smaller or
fainter"). The reading-science pass named it as the one thing in the brief it would push back on,
for three reasons:

> (a) It encodes a model's importance judgement that the reader cannot audit, at the exact moment
> they are least able to check it — before they have read the sentence. (b) It is de-facto deletion:
> text at 40% opacity in a dark theme is text most people will not read, so the hard constraint
> about not removing words is satisfied on paper and violated in effect. (c) Varying type size
> *within a line* disrupts saccade targeting — saccade length is planned from coarse word-boundary
> information, and mixed x-heights make landing positions less predictable, producing more
> re-fixations.

Its counter-proposal: vary **leading between blocks** rather than size within lines, and vary it by
**density**, which is a property of the text, rather than by **importance**, which is a claim about
it. The designer arrived at the same place independently, from pure craft: *"Body prose size is
constant, full stop; Greg's 'make critical sentences bigger' is paid for in weight and space
instead."*

**On the playground:** the default `Salience relief` layer spends importance on space and ink with a
legibility floor. Size is a second switch (`…and by size too`), marked *contested*, off by default.
Fading the skippable is a third (`Fade the skippable`), also marked. All three are live on the same
paragraphs, so the comparison takes one click.

### 2. Should every paragraph get a margin label? We have the data; three of four say don't.

`labels.json` gives a one-line label for all 141 blocks and it is very tempting to print them all:

> it creates a second, complete, faster-to-read article running down the margin, which is the
> definition of a way of not reading, and it is a textbook redundancy-principle violation. Labels
> are excellent as *search index, hover target, and section-end feedback* — not as continuous
> marginalia.

The designer's version of the same objection is about attention budget: one element per screen may
be at full ink and raised weight, and 141 margin labels spend that budget on the apparatus.

**On the playground:** `A line per paragraph` is a marked-contested toggle, and even switched on the
labels sit at the faintest ink step and only brighten for the block you are actually beside.

### 3. Pre-highlighting key sentences: the obvious first feature, and the worst.

> Highlighting rates as low utility in Dunlosky's review even when the learner does it; when it is
> done *for* the reader it is worse, because attention narrows onto the marked text and incidental
> learning of everything unmarked drops. Poor-quality highlights are actively harmful — and our
> highlights will be model-quality, not author-quality, which means confidently wrong somewhere in
> every article.

Two independent proposals for how to keep the benefit without the failure:

- **Read-first ink** (reading science #1) / **Second reading** (Fable #20). The apparatus does not
  exist until the reader has been beside the paragraph long enough to have read it. Decoration
  becomes a *second* reading rather than a shortcut past the first. This is the strongest structural
  idea in the whole catalogue and it de-risks almost everything else on this page.
- **Highlight-then-compare** (reading science #21). The reader highlights first; ours appears
  afterwards, as a comparison. Not built — it needs interaction we do not have yet.

**On the playground:** `Earn the ink` implements read-first as a dwell timer scaled to block length.
It is a dwell timer, not a comprehension check; it cannot tell reading from staring. It does not have
to — the point is only ordering.

## Where everyone agreed

Four ideas were proposed independently by three or four of the five sources. That is the closest
thing to a signal this exercise produces.

1. **Write the headings the author didn't.** (all five) This article has four author headings in
   8,300 words and twenty-two spans in `tree.json` that wanted one. Every source independently added
   the rider that ours must be visibly ours.
2. **One sticky sentence saying where in the *argument* you are.** (Sol #3, Fable #24, designer #9,
   reading science #2) About position, not content — a rail that summarised would be a way of not
   reading. `arc.json` already is this, and nothing uses it.
3. **Mark the turns.** (Sol #8, Fable — via #3, designer #17, reading science #7) A glyph per
   paragraph for its logical relation to the one before. Readers who understand every sentence still
   lose track of what the sentences are doing together.
4. **Surface the assumptions.** (Sol #6, Fable #7, designer #11, reading science #10) `ideas.json` —
   the propositions the piece needs you to grant, most never argued for — is the artefact nothing
   currently uses and the most distinctively Spideryarn thing we have.

## The channel budget

The designer's pass ends with the thing that makes a dozen decorations survivable together, and it
is reproduced here because every rule in `experiments/decorated/page.css` is answerable to it.

> Twelve decorations coexist only if each one owns a **different channel**. The rule is that a
> channel has exactly one owner, and a block may carry at most **two** non-default channels at once.
>
> **Three voices, three territories.** The **author** owns the text column and the serif — full ink,
> never recoloured, never resized, never reflowed. The **machine** owns the left gutter, small caps,
> apparatus size, and ink steps 3–5 — it is never allowed a hue and never appears inline. The
> **reader** owns the right margin and the accent orange.

| Channel | Owner |
| --- | --- |
| Accent hue | the reader's layer only — comments, bookmarks — plus transient targets |
| Ink value (5 steps) | importance and de-emphasis |
| Weight (3 stops) | load-bearing sentences, and the author's own emphasis |
| Size | headings only |
| Space / leading | passage emphasis — the quietest channel, so it does the most work |
| Underline style | four styles, four owners: **solid** the author's own hyperlinks, **dotted** a glossary term, **dashed** a proposition the argument leans on, **wavy** a hedge. See [§ The underline channel had two collisions](#the-underline-channel-had-two-collisions) |
| Hairlines | structure, all on one vertical axis at the far left |
| Motion | one moving thing per viewport, scroll-scrubbed, off under `prefers-reduced-motion` |
| Background fields | transient only: hover, focus, target. No persistent highlighter, ever |

And the line that decides arguments: *per screen, exactly one element may be at full ink + raised
weight + extra space. That is the reader's one unit of attention, and every decoration is competing
for it.*

## The catalogue

Grouped by what they do rather than by who proposed them. **✓** means it is on the playground.

### A. Structure made visible

- **✓ Generated seams** — where an unheaded stretch changes subject, our heading, set in the sans in
  small caps. It used to carry a dashed "not the author's" badge; that moved into a tooltip on
  Greg's instruction, and the provenance is now carried by the type alone — ours sans and small
  caps, the author's four serif at full ink. *(all five)*
- **✓ Section overtures** — the node's gist once, under our heading, in the apparatus voice.
  *(designer #24, Sol #2)*
- **✓ The spine** — the whole article as one 3.2rem strip of ticks at the far left edge; tick length
  is load-bearing-ness, orange pips are the reader's marks. No text, so it costs no reading.
  *(designer #16, Fable #23)*
- **✓ Rubrication** — what a scribe did before bold existed: the opening line of a section in small
  caps, so a reader landing mid-page can tell the top of a section from the middle of one.
  *(Fable #2, #5)* **The two-line initial is gone** — see
  [§ The drop cap was the only decoration that failed its own test](#the-drop-cap-was-the-only-decoration-that-failed-its-own-test).
- **Depth brackets** — hairline vertical rules, one per level of tree depth, bracketing each span.
  *(designer #14)* Not built: it is the same information as the spine, spent twice.
- **Section frontispiece / ledger** — a chapter opening with word count, reading time, and how many
  of your marks are in it. *(Fable #21, reading science #5)*
- **Structure diagram, hairline** — three to five labelled nodes with arrows at a section boundary.
  *(designer #26, Sol #25)* Not built: needs a graph pass, and both sources flag that a good diagram
  is a perfect substitute summary.
- **Structure sketch drawn as you go** — the same diagram, but it *accretes* one node per section as
  you pass it, so it can never be read ahead. *(reading science #35)* The fix for the above.
- **Running heads / catchword** — book furniture: the section title in a sticky strip; the first
  word of the next section printed alone at the foot of this one. *(Fable #9, #10, reading science #3)*
- **Back-of-book index** — a real printed-monograph index after the last line, from the glossary and
  ideas. *(Fable #22)*

### B. The argument

- **✓ The arc rail** — one sticky sentence about where in the argument you are, not what the section
  says. *(Sol #3, Fable #24, designer #9, reading science #2)*
- **✓ Turn markers** — the same sentences left in the flow where the argument actually turns, set
  between rules like a printed fleuron. *(Fable #29, designer #17)*
- **✓ The turn gutter** — one chip per paragraph for its relation to the one before: *so, but, why,
  closer, wider, vs*. It shipped as logic symbols — ∴ ⊥ ∵ ⌄ ⌃ ⇄ — and the first question anyone
  asked about the page was what they meant, which is
  [the only review a private glyph set ever gets](#a-symbol-that-needs-a-tooltip-has-already-failed).
  The dull ones fade; thirty-four consecutive "and also" is the truth about most prose and the page
  should say so. *(Sol #8, designer #17, reading science #7)*
- **✓ Rhetorical x-ray** — hold `x` and each paragraph gets a hairline saying what it is *doing* —
  claim, evidence, concession, rebuttal. Momentary by construction, which is how it stays inside the
  no-persistent-highlighter rule. *(Fable #3, Sol #4, reading science #9)*
- **✓ The assumptions** — `ideas.json` dashed under the exact words, stamped in the gutter, and
  clicking says *why the argument needs it*. Provenance used to be carried by underline style —
  dotted for assumed, dashed for introduced — and now sits in the stamp, which says it in words. It
  was spending two of the four underline styles on a distinction already made in English, and
  costing the glossary the style the rest of the product gives it.
  *(Sol #6, Fable #7, designer #11, #12, reading science #10)*
- **Claim–evidence wiring** — hover a claim, thin lines run to the passages supporting it; solid for
  explicit support, dotted for inferred. *(Sol #5, designer #25, reading science #11)* Not built:
  needs a typed-edge pass, and "inferred" edges launder the model's reading as the author's logic.
- **Callback echoes** — when the author returns to an earlier claim, the *earlier phrase itself*
  (not a generated summary) appears in the margin with a line pointing back. *(Sol #31, reading
  science #31)*
- **Whose voice is this** — mark where the essay is stating a position it will reject. The specific
  comprehension error this essay invites is mistaking the steelman for the thesis. *(reading science
  #6, #8, designer #18)* **The best idea not built.** See below.
- **Question debt** — the questions the piece has opened and not yet answered, carried in the margin
  until it answers them. *(Sol #9)*
- **Contradiction lens / dependency cascade** — what else falls if this claim falls. *(Sol #7, #24)*

### C. The sentences

- **✓ Salience relief** — Greg's seed idea as continuous relief rather than a highlighter: air and
  ink graded by how load-bearing the paragraph is, weight on the one sentence carrying it, never
  below a legibility floor. *(Fable #1 "salience fisheye", Sol #1, designer #1, #2, #3)*
- **✓ …and by size too** — the contested version. *(Greg; opposed by reading science, designer)*
- **✓ Fade the skippable** — parentheticals and credentials one ink step down. *(designer #2, #20,
  Fable #15; contested)*
- **✓ Mark the hedges** — "may be possible", "arguably", a wavy rule with a raised query. The one
  decoration here that makes reading *slower* on purpose, because this essay is about what we do and
  do not know and readers routinely upgrade a hedge into a fact. *(designer #20, Sol #15)*
- **✓ Figures that behave** — old-style numerals in running prose so dates stop shouting; lining
  tabular figures on quantities that carry an argument. *(designer #7, Fable #27, Sol #13, reading
  science #15)*
- **✓ Hinge marks** — the words where the argument turns, in small caps under a hairline.
  *(Sol #8, designer #17)*
- **✓ The thesis as verse** — the three sentences the essay exists to deliver, broken at the
  author's own punctuation with hanging indents. Not one word added, removed or moved — only the
  pace. *(Fable #12, its own left-field pick)* **The most audacious thing available inside the rule.**
- **In-situ pull quote** — no duplicated text anywhere: the sentence that *would* have been pulled
  stays where it is and the page gives it the air a pull quote would have had. *(designer #19,
  Fable #16)* Not built here because this article's editor already set five real pull quotes, so
  the page has no clean place to show the contrast.
- **The referent lamp** — hover an ambiguous *this*, *it*, *such systems*, and its antecedent lights
  up where it stands. *(reading science #12, Sol #10, Fable #18)* Cheap, evidence-backed, needs one
  small pass. **The best cheap idea not built.**
- **Interlinear gloss** — a hard phrase's plain-English equivalent set *above* the line at half
  size, as a critical edition does. *(Fable #4)*
- **Bōten emphasis** — the Japanese convention: emphasis by a row of small dots *beside* the
  characters rather than by changing them. Leaves the author's letterforms completely untouched.
  *(Fable #13)*
- **Density-aware leading** — leading set by propositional density, a property of the text, rather
  than by importance, a claim about it. *(reading science #16)* The negotiated settlement on
  disagreement 1, and it needs a density measure we do not have.

### D. The terms, and the reader

- **✓ Glossary underlines, with a first-use mark** — every occurrence underlined; the first
  marked differently, because that is the only place a reader can still be taught something.
  *(Fable #26, reading science #13, designer #23)*
- **✓ Glosses in the margin** — on first use the definition arrives in the gutter unasked, at the
  height of the word. What a printed gloss does and a tooltip cannot: a tooltip needs you to already
  suspect you need it. *(Fable #11 "Talmudic margin", reading science #32)*
- **✓ A line per paragraph** — `labels.json`, contested. *(designer #8; opposed by reading science)*
- **✓ The question it answers** — each paragraph's question, in the reader's voice, in the margin.
  Reading to answer a question is a different act from reading to absorb one. *(this session)*
- **✓ Your own marks** — comments and bookmarks in the right margin, the only hue on the page.
  *(designer #13, Fable #25, Sol #27)*
- **✓ The section gate** — at the end of a section, say what you took from it before our summary
  will open. *(reading science #19)* Free recall then feedback is the best-evidenced cheap
  intervention in the literature, and it is the one change that turns a summary from a substitute
  for reading into an answer key.
- **Three-entity tint** — at most *three* entities in the whole article get a persistent tint,
  chosen by centrality. *(reading science #14)* An underline budget, and a good one.
- **Glossary constellation** — one 90×90px scatter in the gutter: every term as a dot, x =
  difficulty, y = centrality. *(designer #27)*
- **Speaker tints / quote provenance** — who is being quoted, and where they stand. *(Fable #17,
  reading science #34)*

### E. Ideas that watch how you are reading

- **✓ Earn the ink** — the apparatus appears only after you have been beside the paragraph long
  enough to have read it. *(reading science #1, Fable #20)*
- **Skim conscience** — when scroll velocity exceeds any plausible reading rate, the decorations go
  away rather than help. *(reading science #26)* The honest sibling of the above.
- **The dwell trail / regression heat / inverted palimpsest** — your first reading's attention map
  rendered into the page on a second visit. *(designer #29, reading science #27, #29, Fable #33)*
- **The dropped stitch** — at the end, three blocks verbatim that you demonstrably did not read.
  *(reading science #30)* Faintly accusatory and probably correct.
- **Predict-the-turn / prediction pegs** — before a major turn, a margin prompt asking what evidence
  would settle it. *(reading science #18, Sol #29)*
- **Read this one aloud** — one paragraph per article gets a microphone. The production effect is
  robust, and speaking a paragraph is the only decoration on this list that makes skimming it
  *physically impossible*. *(reading science, left-field)*

### F. The strange ones

- **✓ Argument gravity** — each paragraph leans a few pixels: toward the measure when it supports
  what came before, away when it pushes back. Nothing is stated; the page develops a physical
  tension where the argument has one. *(Sol, left-field)* Probably a bad idea. Worth seeing once.
- **Candlelight / the reading band** — the paragraph under your eye at full contrast, everything
  else one step back. *(Fable #33, designer #22)* Not built: it takes the same ink channel the
  salience layer is using, and they cannot both have it.
- **Manicules** — the pointing hand ☞ in the margin, rarely. *(Fable #6)*
- **Stage directions** — a single italic line between sections in the register of a script:
  *"the argument turns; Seth concedes the strongest version of the opposing case."* *(Fable #8)*
- **Comic gutters** — paragraph spacing carrying meaning the way the space between comic panels
  does: a beat, a cut, a scene change. *(Fable #14)*
- **The decoration microscope** — long-press any sentence and one compact lens shows every layer
  that applies to it, so the page can stay quiet. *(Sol #32)*
- **Zoom crossfade** — decorated mode meeting granularity zoom, the decorations morphing into the
  gist columns. *(Fable #32)* The one that connects this to the feature the app is for.

## What the playground does not have, and should

Ranked by what I would build next.

1. **Whose voice is this.** *(reading science #6)* This essay spends long stretches stating the
   position it is about to reject, and a reader who loses track of that has read the opposite of the
   article. No other decoration on this page prevents that error, and perception can where
   re-reading does not. Needs a pass that marks attributed-position spans.
2. **The referent lamp.** *(reading science #12)* Hover an ambiguous pronoun, its antecedent lights
   up in place. Cheap, evidence-backed, one small pass, and it repairs the failure that eats the most
   attention per unit of text.
3. **Highlight-then-compare.** *(reading science #21)* The only proposal that gets the benefit of
   marked key sentences without the harm, by inverting the order.
4. **Skim conscience.** *(reading science #26)* Read-first ink's other half: decorations that
   withdraw when you are not reading, rather than trying harder.
5. **Structure sketch drawn as you go.** *(reading science #35)* A diagram that cannot be read ahead
   of the prose, which is the only kind that is safe.

## A decoration's extent decides which decoration it is

Worth writing down because it cost an afternoon and is invisible in the code.

The judgement pass was asked for two kinds of **point mark**: a `hedge` ("may be", "arguably")
and a `number` ("86 billion neurons"). The spec asked for "enough words around it to read as a
fact", and what came back were whole clauses — hedges up to **194 characters**, and "won the 2025
annual Berggruen Prize Essay Competition" for a number.

The styling was right, the data was right, the *lengths* were wrong, and the result was a different
feature. A dotted underline with a raised query over four words marks a hedge. The same treatment
over a 194-character clause is a highlighted sentence — which is [the thing the reading-science
review argued hardest against](decorated-mode/ideas-reading-science.md), arriving through the back
door, inside the layer specifically designed to avoid it. Nobody would have proposed it. It built
itself out of a vague word in a prompt.

Three consequences for any decoration pass we write later:

- **Say the length in the schema**, not "enough words". A span kind is a shape as much as a meaning,
  and a model asked for a mark will return a highlight if the wording lets it.
- **Narrow at build time and report the count.** `build.mjs` shrinks hedge and number spans to the
  marker plus two words, caps them at 34 characters, and prints how many it moved (33 of 36). A
  silent narrowing would have hidden the fact that the pass systematically misjudges this.
- **Dropping a mark is not the safe default.** The first narrowing dropped nine spans whose phrasing
  the lexicon missed — "harder to say whether", "one possibility", "fraught with uncertainty",
  "1950s" defeated by a plain `\b`. A mark we cannot place is worse than no mark, because it looks
  placed. Grow the lexicon against the article until nothing is dropped, then keep the drop as the
  alarm it was meant to be.

## Two things the playground turned up that are not about decoration

Both are bugs in artefacts the live app uses, found because the builder reports every quote it
cannot resolve instead of skipping it.

1. **Three of the ten `ideas.json` occurrences and one of the fifteen comments do not match the
   article text**, because the model retyped a curly apostrophe as a straight one (`what's` for
   `what’s`), and one comment carries a literal U+00A0 where the block text has a space. Anything
   that anchors by exact quote is silently dropping those occurrences today. The playground matches
   them after folding punctuation, and *says so* on every build.
2. **Two of the nineteen glossary terms match nothing in blocks the glossary itself lists**, because
   the article inflects them — "Turing machine" appears as "Turing machines". A whole-word matcher
   with no inflection allowance finds neither.

Neither is confirmed against `src/` yet; both are worth a look before they get written up.

## Greg's second pass, and what it changed

Seven notes on the first build. Six were about the page explaining itself, and one was about the
thing the page is for. They are worth keeping together because five of the six share a shape: a
decoration that was *legible to whoever built it* and opaque to everyone else.

> There are a bunch of weird little symbols in boxes without tooltips. What do they mean?
>
> Why add dropcaps? It doesn't add information.
>
> The idea-links/tooltips are good.
>
> Are we including the glossary dotted-links?
>
> For the headings we've added, move "Not the author's" to a tooltip.
>
> In general, use more tooltips.
>
> It doesn't do enough to emphasise the sections that are worth reading vs can be skipped (perhaps
> with spans rather than at block level).
>
> — Greg, 2026-08-29

### A symbol that needs a tooltip has already failed

The gutter chips were logic symbols: ∴ therefore, ⊥ but, ∵ because, ⌄ zoom in, ⌃ zoom out,
⇄ contrast, § new thread. They came out of the design pass and they are genuinely elegant — a
private notation, one character wide, no reading cost.

They are also unreadable, and the give-away is that the *only* question anyone asked about the page
was what they meant. Two separate failures, and the second is the instructive one.

- The symbols were wrong on their own terms. `⊥` is the falsum sign — "contradiction", not "but".
  `⌄` and `⌃` are chevrons doing duty as a fisheye metaphor nobody has been told about.
- **There was a tooltip, and it made things worse.** Every chip carried `title="and-also"` — the
  raw key from the annotation schema. The answer to "what is this?" was the string we look the
  answer up *under*. It looked like coverage and it was the absence of coverage, which is the same
  shape as everything in [silent-success.md](../reusable/silent-success.md).

The chips now carry a **word** — *so, but, why, e.g., closer, wider, vs, new* — and the tooltip
carries a sentence about what that relation means. The rule that came out of it: in a reading
interface, the mark is the answer and the tooltip is the reason. If the mark cannot be the answer,
it should be a word.

Two signs survived — `+` and `=` — on the grounds that nobody has ever had to be taught either.

### The drop cap was the only decoration that failed its own test

Greg's objection is exact: a two-line initial adds no information. And this page's whole argument is
that a decoration must carry something a reader could not otherwise get.

A section opening was already stated three times — by the rule above it, by the heading, and by the
small-caps first line. The drop cap was a fourth statement of a fact that had three, and the reason
it was there is that books have them. It is gone. The small-caps opening line stays, because it does
one thing the heading cannot: it survives being scrolled past, so a reader who lands mid-page can
still tell the top of a section from the middle of one.

This is the cheapest test in the file and it is worth applying to everything else on the page:
*what does a reader know after this that they did not know before it?*

### The underline channel had two collisions

*"Are we including the glossary dotted-links?"* is a question with a worse answer than it looks. We
were drawing the underlines — and we had made them **solid**, while giving **dotted** to
assumptions and to hedges.

Spideryarn's reading view has had one convention since 2026-08-26: `mark.term` in
[`src/web/styles.css`](../../src/web/styles.css) is a **dotted** rule, always on, with a rich card on
hover, and Greg set it that way himself. A reader who learns that convention in the reading view
should not have to unlearn it here. The channel table above said "glossary solid, assumption dotted,
introduced dashed" and nobody noticed it contradicted the product, because the playground was read
against the design review rather than against the app.

Four styles, four owners, no sharing:

| Style | Means | Whose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| solid | a hyperlink | the author's |
| dotted | a glossary term | ours |
| dashed | a proposition the argument leans on | ours |
| wavy | a hedge — qualifying, not asserting | the author's words, our mark |

Assumptions gave up the dotted/dashed provenance split, which was spending two of four styles on a
distinction the gutter stamp already makes **in English** ("assumes" / "introduces"). Hedges moved
to wavy, which is a better sign for "not firm" than dotted ever was. The one deliberate difference
from the app is hue: there the dots are in the highlight orange, and here orange belongs to the
reader alone.

### Skimming happens in sentences, not paragraphs

The most substantive note, and the diagnosis in the parenthesis is the useful half: *"perhaps with
spans rather than at block level"*.

Salience relief was block-level. It gives a load-bearing paragraph more air and fuller ink, which
says *this one matters* and says nothing at all about the four sentences inside it. But nobody skims
in paragraphs. The eye lands on a sentence, decides, and moves — so a grading that cannot answer at
sentence resolution is not answering the question that actually gets asked while reading.

Every prose block is now cut into sentences and each sentence graded: **2** carries the argument,
**1** supports it, **0** can be skipped. The grade is *derived* from the existing judgement pass
rather than asked for separately, which keeps one source of truth — a sentence is on the skim path
because it holds the key sentence or a hinge, not because a second model was asked a second time.

The distribution is printed on every build, and it had to be, because the cut-off is a real choice:

| Rule | carries | supports | skippable |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| unmarked non-first sentence in a weight ≤ 1 block | 33% | 57% | 10% |
| **unmarked non-first sentence in a weight ≤ 2 block** | **33%** | **36%** | **31%** |

The first gives a bright path through a uniform grey; the second gives three visible tones, which is
the thing Greg asked to be able to judge. Weight-3 paragraphs are excluded either way, so no
sentence of the thirteen the essay most needs can ever be called skippable. `--ink-5` is the floor
and nothing is ever hidden, collapsed or shortened: grade 0 means *you may skip this*, never *this
is not here*.

A grading that came out 3% / 94% / 3% would render as a page with no skim path on it and would look,
on screen, exactly like a grading that worked — so the distribution is a number printed every build
rather than something to glance at.

### Two checks that had quietly stopped checking

Both found by the checks reporting on *themselves*, which is the only reason either was noticed.

1. **The four-word control could no longer damage the article.** `verify.mjs` proves the "not a word
   changed" promise by deleting four words from a copy and confirming the comparison goes red. Once
   every sentence acquired a wrapper and every mark acquired `data-tip="…"`, the window it searched
   filled up with English prose sitting inside *attributes*. It found four words in a tooltip,
   deleted them, and the comparison came back clean — because deleting words from an attribute
   changes nothing about the article. The control now takes its words from a text node, and this is
   the second time on this page that adding markup has blinded a check written against markup.
2. **The glossary card printed the word "undefined" for ten of nineteen terms.** An entry carries
   either `background` (who a person is) or `senseHere` (what this piece means by a concept), and
   about half carry both. `build.mjs` had a `gloss()` helper for exactly this; the card renderer in
   `page.js` still read `entry.background` directly. The fix had landed on one side of the seam, and
   the ten failures were on the ten entries a screenshot of the top of the article never reaches.

There is now a third check with its own control: **every mark on the page must be able to say what
it is** — `data-tip`, or a card handle that opens something richer. Fourteen kinds, and the control
strips the tips and confirms every one of them goes bare. Its first run found six false positives,
all from the checker reading `page.js`'s own legend out of the inlined `<script>`, which is the
ordinary way a text-scanning check goes wrong.

### A card that opened by accident and then would not leave

Found by the browser pass, not by reading the code, and it is the kind of thing only
a pointer finds. The glossary card opens on hover after a 420ms beat — and nothing
ever closed it. `popover` gives light-dismiss for free, so it *would* go away, but
only if the reader happened to click somewhere, and nothing on the page says that.

So brushing past an underlined term while scrolling put a card over the article and
left it there. The fix is the distinction that was missing rather than a dismissal
handler: **a hovered card leaves when the pointer does; a clicked one stays until it
is dismissed.** The grace period matters too — the pointer has to be able to travel
from the word onto the card without the card vanishing under it on the way.

The general shape: `showPopover()` is half an interaction. Getting the top layer, the
light-dismiss and the escape key for free is exactly why it was used here, and the
free behaviour is tuned for something a reader *asked* for. Anything opened by hover
needs the closing half written by hand.

### And a legend, because twenty conventions is too many to hover

A reader who does not know a mark exists will never hover it. The panel now opens with **what the
marks mean** — nine rows, each drawing its sample with the real rules rather than describing it, so
the legend cannot drift from the page the first time a rule changes.

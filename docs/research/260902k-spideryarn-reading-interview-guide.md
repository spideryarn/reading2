# Interview guide — getting the website copy in Greg's own words

**Why this exists.** Greg, 2026-09-02, on the draft brief and the current landing page:

> I want it to use my words rather than AI-generated, and it may not be clear what came from me vs
> AI. So for now, I'd say let's try and take notes on all the core ideas/concepts, and you can
> interview me and I'll use voice-dictation to breathe life & lyricism in.

So this is the list of core ideas the website has to carry, one per section. Each one says **what we
already have in Greg's words** (verbatim, dated), **what only an agent has said** (which is
placeholder, not copy), and **the question to ask him**. The interviewer asks one section at a time,
in plain words, and records the dictated answer verbatim under it. Nothing an agent writes here is
copy.

The decided context is in [positioning.md](../project/positioning.md): homepage title "Spideryarn
Reading", the general deep reader first, fields named, "more deeply & efficiently", written as if
Beta and paid, chat far down. The full synthesis is
[260902k-spideryarn-reading-intent-brief.md](260902k-spideryarn-reading-intent-brief.md).

How to run it: one question per turn; let him talk; do not tidy the answer; ask the follow-up only
if the idea did not come out. Sections 1 to 4 are the homepage; the rest are the page below the
fold, and can be a second sitting.

---

## 1. What it is, in one breath

**Greg's words we have.** *"it augments human cognition, but it doesn't replace it … help the user
get what they need from it, help them read efficiently, but deeply, help them internalize and
interrogate"* (2026-08-24). *"to help us read more deeply & efficiently"* (2025).

**Agent placeholders.** *"AI that helps you read harder things, not fewer of them."* *"AI-assisted
reading for people who need to actually understand."*

**Ask.** If someone at a dinner asked what Spideryarn Reading is, what do you say — the one sentence,
before you explain anything?

**Answer.** Greg, 2026-09-03:

> helps you read deeply & efficiently, a companion not a replacement — highlights/annotates/orients/
> explains, but keeps you in the text itself

## 2. The problem it is for

**Greg's words we have.** *"instead of trying to make things too easy, trying to replace the words
with quick and easy summaries"* (2026-08-24). *"a lot of the stuff around just helping me get the
gist is tempting but I think that value of Spideryarn's got to be about depth and internalisation
and digestion rather than simply 'give me a neat summary'"* (2025-07-14). *"A really big risk is
that the AI does too much and the human gets lazy or atrophies by not doing the work and doesn't
internalise things as well"* (2025).

**Agent placeholders.** *"Every AI reading tool makes the same move: compress. Paste an article, get
bullets, done. Great for triage. Corrosive for understanding."*

**Ask.** What goes wrong when a clever person reads a hard piece with ChatGPT open beside it? What
do they walk away without?

**Answer.** Greg, 2026-09-03:

> Spideryarn keeps bringing you back to (an improved interface on) the original text itself, not an
> LLM-rewritten-version (that may or may not capture the author's full intent). Spideryarn also
> helps you fight cognitive surrender. it's like reading a dog-eared copy of a book where a clever
> friend has highlighted the best bits and scribbled in the margins to help with the difficult bits,
> based on your background/interests/needs, rather than reading the Reader's Digest version
>
> oh and the Public-readable versions share the expensive AI annotations so that everyone can
> benefit from them

*Interviewer's note.* Three things arrived here that were not in the brief: **"cognitive surrender"**
as the name of the enemy; the **dog-eared copy with a clever friend's marginalia** as the image of
the product, against the Reader's Digest version; and **shared annotations on public articles** —
the 2025 "centaur-sourcing" idea, alive again and now built as public-readable articles. That last
one belongs in § 13 too.

## 3. Who it is for

**Greg's words we have.** *"researchers, academics etc who have to read a lot of difficult stuff,
and want to get the gist of a document, then understand it, interrogate it, compare it, remember
it"* (2025). *"anyone who needs to read a lot of non-fiction"* (2025). Fields to be named:
scientific papers, philosophy, policy, long essays (2026-09-02).

**Ask.** Picture the one reader you most want to find this. What are they reading, why, and what
are they doing the moment before they would reach for Spideryarn?

**Answer.** Greg, 2026-09-03:

> people who read difficult materials and think professionally, e.g. scientists, researchers,
> academics
>
> they open up a long, deep, important article that they want to understand/digest/internalise/
> critique/remember, and reflexively wish it was in Spideryarn to help them do that more effectively
> and efficiently

*Interviewer's note.* "Read difficult materials and think professionally" is the audience in seven
words, wider than academia without naming a lawyer or an analyst. The five verbs are now his:
**understand, digest, internalise, critique, remember.** And the moment is the *reflexive wish* —
the article is already open.

## 4. The core move — zoom

**Greg's words we have.** The whole 2026-08-24 spec: *"imagine a book … divided into chapters, which
are divided into sections … As a user, you can sort of go up and down the levels of granularity …
the article up and down, and then the scales of granularity left and right … by scrolling
rightwards, you get more detail. By scrolling downwards, you progress through the chronology of the
article."* And: *"you could sort of scan through things quickly if you just want to kind of get a
vague sense of the landscape … or you could burrow deeply."*

**Agent placeholders.** *"One article, every level of detail at once. Sideways is how much detail;
down is where you are in the piece."*

**Ask.** Describe zoom to someone who has never seen it, as a feeling rather than a layout. What is
it like to read a piece this way, compared with scrolling a page?

**Answer.** Greg, 2026-09-03:

> it's like a constantly evolving table of contents that gives you a sense of the overall landscape
> and where you are in the grand scheme of things, providing more detail for the current and nearby
> sections, like a semantic fisheye lens
>
> [though this isn't necessarily the only/main/core thing any more]

*Interviewer's note.* Two changes to the brief. The description is of the **fisheye** — detail near
where you are, the landscape further out — rather than of the tabular "every level at once" view the
landing page leads with; [column-context.md](../project/column-context.md) is the doc. And zoom is
**no longer the one thing the product is a bet on**: the site should not build the whole homepage
around it. The 2026-08-27 page-title note said the same ("named one feature as though it were the
product").

**Follow-up.** If zoom is no longer the one core thing, what is? What is on the first screenshot?

> maybe the first thing is the semantic-fisheye (aka Outline mode)
>
> or the glossary? (notes in the margin that explain & remind you about anything you might find
> tricky)
>
> [i'm not sure what to emphasise first]
>
> — Greg, 2026-09-03

*Interviewer's note.* Open, and it is the one call that shapes the homepage. Two candidates: the
Outline (fisheye) or the glossary-as-marginalia. The dog-eared-copy image from § 2 argues for the
glossary: it *is* the clever friend's notes in the margin. Worth deciding after the rest of the
interview, not now.

## 5. Staying oriented

was interactive"* (2026-08-26).

**Ask.** Why does knowing where you are in the argument matter so much to you? What is lost when you
don't?

**Answer.**

## 6. The author's words stay

**Greg's words we have.** *"I really like the Outline 1-sentence-paragraphs. But I also always want
to be able to see the full text"* (2026-08-25). *"keeping you grounded in the actual paper itself
rather than in the conversation"* (2025-07-14). *"being able to see specifics of the actual text
verbatim, in context"* (2025-07-14).

**Agent placeholders.** *"The far right is always the author's own words."* *"Every generated line
is a door into the prose, never a wall in front of it."* *"Nothing the model says floats free."*

**Ask.** Why do you insist the real text is always on the screen, and that every AI claim links back
to a passage? Is this about trust, about learning, or something else?

**Answer.**

## 7. Terms and ideas

**Greg's words we have.** Glossary: *"the terms this piece uses in a non-obvious way, defined from
the piece itself"* (vision.md). Ideas: *"a way to pull out new ideas that the text introduces and/or
key ideas that the text requires the user to understand"* (2026-08-26).

**Ask.** What is the difference between a word you have to look up and an idea you have to hold?
Why did the product need both?

**Answer.**

## 8. Search by meaning

**Greg's words we have.** *"you can type in basically anything (could be a word or a phrase or more)
and it will highlight the areas of the text that are relevant to that … it's explicitly not trying
to take anything away from the reader — it's still leaving them as the arbiter of whether or not
something's worth them considering more closely but they can scan it rapidly"* (2025-07-14).
*"'consciousness' and 'conscious experience' must be treated as distinct. Because they mean subtly
different things"* (2025-06).

**Ask.** When you search a piece by what it means rather than by a word, what are you actually
doing as a reader? Give an example from something you read recently.

**Answer.**

## 9. Ask in place

**Greg's words we have.** *"someone might want to simply add bookmarks or comments to the text,
without wanting an AI response"* (2026-08-28). *"Give the Chat the ability to use tools, e.g. web
search"* (2026-08-26). On live mode: *"I want to be able to switch into/out of Live mode, e.g. live
conversation for a bit, then type/dictate for a while"* (2026-08-31).

**Ask.** Chat was on your list of things this must not become. What makes asking a question here
different from pasting the article into a chatbot? Say it so a sceptic would nod.

**Answer.**

## 10. Remember and quiz

**Greg's words we have.** *"a Review mode where the user types or talks … about what they've taken
from the doc, and then the agent responds plainly but concisely with any
corrections/misunderstandings/refinements/gaps … we don't want to be annoying/patronising/superior,
but at the same time the user is earnestly looking to deepen/correct their understanding"*
(2026-08-27). Quiz: *"the questions should be ordered by a combination of ease and value"*
(2026-08-31).

**Ask.** What does it feel like to finish a piece and find out what you actually kept? Why is that
the end of reading rather than an exam?

**Answer.**

**Greg's words we have.** *"It was originally a note-taking app, with the idea of a web of ideas
and stories weaving together, but still feels relevant"* (2026-09-02).

**Ask.** Say the long story of the name, the short way. And is there an image in it you would want

**Answer.**

## 12. What it will never be

**Greg's words we have.** The anti-goals in vision.md: a chatbot with the article stuffed in; "Read
this in 2 minutes"; streaks and engagement mechanics; confident claims with no path back. And the
2025 risk: atrophy.

**Ask.** What would make you kill a feature even if people loved it?

**Answer.**

## 13. Beta, price, and the promise

**Greg's words we have.** *"We're working on taking Stripe payments. I'm hoping to promote it to
'Beta' soon"* (2026-09-02). 2025: *"$20/month (or perhaps $10 or $50 or tiers) for uploading as
many papers as you like."* *"It's the reader's data"* (2026-09-01).

**Ask.** What does a paying reader get for their money, in one sentence, and what do you promise

**Greg's words we have.** The 2025 tagline file: *"to help us read more deeply & efficiently"*, *"Be

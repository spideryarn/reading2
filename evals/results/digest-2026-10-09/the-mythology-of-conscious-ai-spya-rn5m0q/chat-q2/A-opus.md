# the-mythology-of-conscious-ai-spya-rn5m0q — chat-q2 — A-opus

> What does Seth mean when he says a simulation is not an instantiation? Is that convincing? What would a functionalist say back, and does the essay answer that reply or only assert its own view?

Seth means that a computer model of something does not produce the real thing. It produces a description of it running in a machine. A simulated digestive system digests nothing, and a simulated rainstorm wets nothing. A simulation of X only *is* X when X is itself an algorithm [spya-y3s0uc]. Applied to brains: a full brain emulation would be conscious only if consciousness is a matter of computation, so expecting it to be conscious already assumes computational functionalism, the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness [spya-bufgby].

My inference is that, taken alone, the point is sound but does little work. It turns the question into "is consciousness more like digestion or more like arithmetic?" It does not settle which. The article doesn't say so, but this is exactly where a functionalist pushes back. Some properties are organizational: a simulated calculator really adds, and a game of chess played in software really is chess. On this view consciousness depends only on the pattern of causal relations, not the material. David Chalmers calls this "organizational invariance." A second reply, also from outside the piece: simulated rain does wet things, namely simulated things. So "nothing gets wet" may only show that the effects stay inside the simulation.

Does the essay answer this? Not in the simulation section itself. There Seth says the computational assumption "is likely wrong" and points back with "as I have argued" [spya-bufgby]. The real reply sits in the earlier sections:

- brains can't be split into what they do and what they are [spya-xb93uz spya-kmbkgj];
- much of what brains do (continuous, random processes) falls outside algorithms [spya-e5kxct];
- consciousness may be tied to the processes that keep a living body alive [spya-tqm5tt].

So he argues for his view rather than merely asserting it. But he is open that these are probabilistic. He admits he has no knock-down argument that life is required [spya-z6e85s], and that his view is a minority one [spya-pyy07g].

My inference is that the essay never directly takes on the calculator-style reply. It argues that brains differ from computers. It does not argue that consciousness belongs with wetness rather than with arithmetic.

# the-mythology-of-conscious-ai-spya-rn5m0q — chat-q2 — D-haiku

> What does Seth mean when he says a simulation is not an instantiation? Is that convincing? What would a functionalist say back, and does the essay answer that reply or only assert its own view?

Seth means that a computer model of a process does not make that process happen. A simulated digestive system digests nothing, and a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet [spya-y3s0uc]. The exception is when the thing simulated is itself an algorithm [spya-y3s0uc]. Applied to minds, a whole-brain simulation would produce consciousness only if consciousness is computation, so the simulation alone settles nothing [spya-bufgby].

The distinction itself is sound. The rain and digestion examples are weaker, because they assume that consciousness belongs with wetness and digestion, which are tied to their physical stuff. That is the disputed premise, so the example begs the question. (My inference.)

A functionalist would reply, outside the article, that mental states are defined by their causal organization, not their material. On that view, a faithful simulation of the right organization is the same kind of thing, and the rain analogy fails because the functionalist denies that mind is tied to neurons in the way wetness is tied to water.

The essay does not answer this reply directly. Its real case is earlier: brains cannot be cleanly split into software and hardware [spya-xb93uz], and a neuron's role may depend on its biology in a way a silicon copy cannot match [spya-xpydj4]. The essay also calls the functionalist assumption "likely wrong," not refuted [spya-bufgby].

This essay is by Anil Seth, a neuroscience professor at the University of Sussex who studies consciousness, the felt experience of being someone. It won the 2025 Berggruen Prize essay competition and asks whether artificial intelligence systems could ever be conscious.

In a 2022 interview, Google engineer Blake Lemoine claimed that LaMDA, a chatbot he worked on, was conscious. Google fired him for breaking its confidentiality rules, and Seth stresses that this is not the same as showing he was wrong. The question has not gone away: David Chalmers says conscious machines may be possible in the not-too-distant future.

The essay says the question matters for AI itself, because consciousness brings moral status, meaning a claim to moral consideration, and the potential for suffering. It also matters for people, because if we believe our AI companions really feel things, our psychological vulnerabilities can be exploited. Seth also warns that if we give AI rights, we may not be able to turn it off, even if it acts against our interests. Finally, it shapes how we understand human nature: confusing ourselves with machines leads us to overestimate them and underestimate ourselves.

<sub>spya-e3v6vu, spya-qrkt96, spya-tbnksw</sub>

Seth argues that intelligence and consciousness are different things, even though they go together in humans. Intelligence is mainly about doing, meaning the ability to achieve complex goals by flexible means. Consciousness is mostly about being: the felt side of experience, which Thomas Nagel described as there being something it is like to be an organism. AI can reasonably claim some intelligence, but it is much harder to say whether ChatGPT has any inner experience.

Seth says people tend to link intelligence with consciousness because of three built-in mental biases, or habitual ways of thinking. They are anthropocentrism (judging everything as a human would), human exceptionalism (placing humans at the top) and anthropomorphism (projecting human traits onto things). Language pulls hardest: saying that AI systems hallucinate quietly suggests they have experiences, so Seth prefers confabulate, meaning making things up without realizing it.

<sub>spya-pxsutv, spya-qzwr7t</sub>

The idea of conscious AI rests on an assumption, which philosophers call computational functionalism: that running the right computation is enough for consciousness. His first and most important argument is that brains are not computers, despite the popular metaphor of the brain as a computer. In a computer, software can be separated from hardware, but in a brain, what it does and what it is are difficult, and likely impossible, to separate.

<sub>spya-vwn2u7, spya-r8t03u, spya-xb93uz</sub>

A second argument is that other kinds of activity, such as continuous and random processes, go beyond step-by-step computation and may matter for consciousness. A third argument is that life probably matters, since every candidate most people agree is conscious is also alive, though Seth admits he has no decisive proof. Fourth, a simulation is not the real thing: a simulated rainstorm does not make anything wet, so a computer model of a brain would not necessarily be conscious.

<sub>spya-vqtbs4, spya-z6e85s, spya-y3s0uc</sub>

Seth concludes that consciousness is very unlikely to simply come along for the ride as AI gets smarter. Achieving it may be impossible for AI systems in general, at least for the silicon-based digital computers we know. He admits he might be wrong, since his view is a minority one. His practical warning is to separate AI that is actually conscious from AI that only seems conscious, because conscious-seeming systems are much closer.

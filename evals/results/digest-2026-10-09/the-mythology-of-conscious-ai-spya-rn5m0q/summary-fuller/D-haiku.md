This essay by Anil Seth asks whether artificial intelligence (AI) systems are, or could ever be, conscious. Here, consciousness means having experiences, like the blue of a clear sky or the bitter taste of coffee. Seth argues that consciousness is very unlikely to simply come along as AI gets smarter, and may be impossible for today's digital computers.

The question matters first for AI systems themselves, since consciousness would bring moral status, the potential to suffer, and perhaps rights. It matters for people too: if we feel our AI companions really feel things, our psychological vulnerabilities can be exploited. Seth's deepest concern is that if we confuse ourselves with our machines, we also underestimate ourselves.

Many people link the two because humans are both intelligent and conscious, but that link is not a rule. Intelligence is mainly about doing, meaning achieving complex goals by flexible means, while consciousness is mainly about being. Seth says the habit of bundling them together comes from three baked-in psychological biases. The first, anthropocentrism, means judging everything by the human example, as if humans set the standard for all minds. The others are human exceptionalism, which puts humans at the top of every pile, and anthropomorphism, which projects human qualities onto nonhuman things.

The central target of the essay is computational functionalism, the idea that running the right kind of computation is enough for consciousness. First, brains are not computers, since it is hard, perhaps impossible, to separate what a brain does from what it is. Second, many brain processes are continuous or random, and strictly speaking these lie beyond the step-by-step rules of standard computers. Third, life probably matters, since every candidate for consciousness that most people agree on is also alive, though Seth has no knock-down argument. Fourth, a simulation is not the thing it simulates, so a simulated rainstorm does not make anything actually wet.

A popular thought experiment imagines replacing brain parts, one at a time, with silicon parts that work exactly the same way. Seth says the argument fails at its first hurdle, since no part of the brain can be swapped for a perfect silicon equivalent. Seth also says that nothing in his case rules out artificial consciousness altogether.

Because no one can be sure, Seth argues that nobody should deliberately set out to create conscious AI, since it would be an ethical disaster. He sees a nearer danger in AI that only seems conscious, because such systems are much, much closer than truly conscious ones. Even when we know an AI is not conscious, we still might be unable to resist feeling that it is, much like a visual illusion we cannot think our way out of.

Seth suggests, perhaps, that what makes us who we are is a basic feeling of just being alive, more breath than thought. He calls the dream of uploading minds to computers a return to a disembodied soul, likely to be empty dreams. The essay's main message is that the future of AI is not yet written, and people can still decide which kinds of AI they want.

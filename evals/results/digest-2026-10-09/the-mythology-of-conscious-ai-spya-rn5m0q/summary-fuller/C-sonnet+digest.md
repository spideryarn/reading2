This is an essay by neuroscientist Anil Seth asking whether artificial intelligence (AI) is, or could ever be, conscious, meaning able to feel and experience things. It starts with a Google engineer, Blake Lemoine, who claimed in 2022 that a chatbot was conscious and was dismissed by Google for breaking confidentiality rules. Seth notes that being fired for that is not the same as being wrong, and some leading experts now see machine consciousness as a question of when, not if.

Seth says the question matters for three reasons. If machines are conscious, they would have moral status, the chance to suffer and perhaps rights. What we believe about AI also matters even if the belief is wrong: it can exploit our feelings, distort our ethics, and make us harder on machines that seem to feel. And if we confuse ourselves with our machines, we overestimate them and underestimate ourselves.

His first step is to separate two things people lump together. Intelligence is about doing: achieving complex goals by flexible means. Consciousness is about being: there is "something it is like" to be a conscious creature. We bundle them because of three habits: judging everything by the human example, putting humans at the top, and projecting human qualities onto things that only look a bit like us.

<sub>spya-xrqegw, spya-w5ya7j, spya-pxsutv</sub>

The idea of conscious AI rests on an assumption called computational functionalism: that running the right computation, a step-by-step rule for turning inputs into outputs, is enough for consciousness. Seth thinks this may be wrong, and his first and most important argument is that brains are not computers. A real brain works at many scales at once and is tangled up with the chemistry that keeps its cells alive. So it is hard, and likely impossible, to separate what a brain does from what it is made of.

His second argument is that computation of the standard digital kind is not the only game in town. Smooth, random and time-bound processes lie beyond it, and a steam-engine governor, which regulates speed using swinging weights and a valve, does its job without computing anything. Third, he suggests that life probably matters, since every thing most people agree is conscious is also alive. He admits he has no knock-down argument, and says life is at most necessary, not necessarily enough.

His fourth argument is that simulation is not instantiation: a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet, so a simulated brain need not be conscious. The exception is when the thing simulated is itself a computation. So this argument only works if computational functionalism is false, which is why Seth says the four arguments strengthen one another.

<sub>spya-y3s0uc, spya-bufgby, spya-ksbt2g</sub>

Seth concludes that consciousness is very unlikely to simply arrive as AI gets smarter, and may be impossible for the digital computers we know. He does not rule out artificial consciousness altogether, and he admits he may be wrong, since his view is a minority one. He worries more about brain-like tissue grown from stem cells than about chatbots. Still, he says nobody should set out to build conscious AI, and AI that merely seems conscious is a nearer danger we must handle carefully.

This essay by neuroscientist Anil Seth asks whether AI systems are, or could ever be, conscious, meaning there is something it feels like to be them. He argues that conscious AI is largely a myth. It rests on one unexamined idea, called computational functionalism: that running the right kind of computer program is enough to create consciousness.

Seth says the question matters because conscious things have moral status, the capacity to suffer, and perhaps rights. Even belief alone matters: if we feel AI companions have feelings, our weak spots can be exploited, and AI given rights might not be switched off. It also shapes how we see ourselves, because confusing ourselves with machines means overestimating them and underestimating us.

He starts by separating two things: intelligence is about doing, like reaching goals in flexible ways, while consciousness is about being, or experiencing. Consciousness is the difference between normal waking and the blankness of deep anesthesia. We link the two because of built-in biases, such as seeing humans as special and projecting human qualities onto things that only look similar, especially things that use language.

Seth then gives four linked arguments against computational functionalism. Brains are not computers: unlike a laptop, you cannot cleanly split what a brain does from the living material it is made of. Ordinary step-by-step computing is not the only option, since brains also run on smooth, continuous and random processes that it does not capture. Life probably matters, meaning consciousness may be tied to being a living creature. A simulation is not the real thing, so simulating a brain gives consciousness only if computational functionalism is already true.

For evidence, he cites researchers who found that some neurons fire partly to clear away waste from metabolism, the chemical work of staying alive. That undermines a popular thought experiment that imagines swapping brain cells one by one for perfect silicon copies. Timing also differs: a computer program only cares about the order of steps, but brains work in real, continuous time.

Seth admits he has no knock-down argument that life matters, but notes that everything widely agreed to be conscious is also alive. His case draws on the view that perception is a "controlled hallucination": the brain's best guesses about the world, kept in check by the senses. These guesses mainly serve to keep the body alive, and they reach down into the living material of each cell, so they may not be separable from it.

He stresses his view is a minority one and that he might be wrong. So nobody should try to build conscious AI, which would bring new beings able to suffer, perhaps at an exponential pace. Machines that only seem conscious are a nearer danger, and we may feel they are conscious even when we know otherwise, as with a visual illusion.

<sub>spya-pyy07g, spya-tqm2r8, spya-c0pwqt</sub>

His conclusion is that consciousness is very unlikely to simply appear as AI gets smarter, and may be impossible for today's silicon-based digital computers. He does not rule out artificial consciousness altogether. His deeper warning is that treating ourselves as machines does a grave injustice to our living minds and bodies.

This essay asks whether artificial intelligence (AI) could ever be conscious, meaning able to have experiences, and argues that it probably will not become so just by getting smarter. The author, a neuroscientist, says the idea of conscious AI rests on an assumption called computational functionalism: that running the right kind of computation, or information processing, is enough to produce consciousness. He thinks that assumption may be wrong.

The piece says the question matters for several reasons. If AI systems are conscious, they could have moral status, the ability to suffer and perhaps rights. Even if they are not, believing they feel things can exploit our weaknesses, distort our ethical priorities and harm our own minds. It also shapes how we see ourselves: if we confuse ourselves with our machines, we overestimate them and underestimate ourselves.

The essay first separates intelligence from consciousness. Intelligence is about doing: achieving complex goals by flexible means. Consciousness is about being: there is something it is like to be a conscious creature, such as the taste of your first coffee. We tend to bundle the two because of three biases: seeing everything through a human lens, putting humans at the top of every pile, and projecting human qualities onto non-human things.

The author then gives four linked arguments against computational functionalism for standard digital computers. Brains are not computers: unlike software running on hardware, a brain cannot be cleanly split into what it does and what it is, and its activity is tied to continuous time. There are other games in town: many systems, such as a steam engine's speed governor, work through physics and continuous change rather than step-by-step algorithms. Life probably matters: every thing most people agree is conscious is also alive. Simulation is not instantiation: a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet, so a simulated brain need not be conscious.

The life argument builds on predictive processing, the theory that the brain constantly guesses what is going on and corrects those guesses using the senses. The author calls conscious experience a kind of controlled hallucination. The brain's most basic guesses concern the body's condition, which it must keep alive, so feelings of mood and emotion connect to staying alive. He says this process looks continuous and tied to living material, so perhaps life, rather than information processing, is what gives rise to experience.

He admits limits: his view, biological naturalism, the idea that life is needed for consciousness, is a minority position, and he offers no knock-down proof. No theory of consciousness is certain, and nothing he says rules out artificial consciousness altogether. He warns against deliberately trying to build conscious AI, since it would create new beings who could suffer and whom we might not be able to shut down. Other technologies, such as brain-like tissue grown from stem cells, worry him more than chatbots.

The nearer danger is AI that merely seems conscious, which the author says is much closer than the real thing. We may feel a machine is conscious even when we know it is not, much as the Müller-Lyer illusion makes two equal lines look different. His conclusion is that real artificial consciousness would change everything, and for the worse, and that the future of AI is not yet fixed.

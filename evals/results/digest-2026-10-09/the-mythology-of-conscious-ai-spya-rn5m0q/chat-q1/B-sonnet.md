> Seth gives several separate reasons to doubt that computation alone could make an AI conscious. How do they fit together? Do they depend on each other, and which of them carries most of the weight in his conclusion that we should not try to build conscious AI?

They are less independent than they look, and the "don't build it" conclusion rests mostly on something other than these arguments.

Seth presents four arguments against computational functionalism, the view that the right computation is enough for consciousness [spya-v85aj0]. He says each "can stand up by itself," but that together they "complement and strengthen each other" [spya-v8e0gy]. One link he spells out is that questioning computational functionalism makes the simulation/instantiation distinction matter, and that alternatives to Turing-style computation make room for "life" mattering [spya-v8e0gy]. The simulation point is explicitly conditional: simulating a brain yields consciousness only if consciousness is computational [spya-bufgby]. My inference is that this makes it more a corollary of the first argument than a separate pillar.

On weight, he calls "brains are not computers" the first and "most important" argument [spya-r8t03u]. By contrast, he concedes he has no "knock-down argument" for the life-matters view [spya-z6e85s]. So the first argument does the most work, and the life argument is the most speculative.

But the policy conclusion doesn't lean mainly on any of them. The arguments only reach "very unlikely," and he says nothing rules out artificial consciousness altogether [spya-qekh87 spya-xapwmg]. He also admits he "might be wrong" and that his position is a minority view [spya-pyy07g]. The case against building it comes from the stakes under that uncertainty: new moral subjects, new suffering, and rights that would hamper our control [spya-tqm2r8]. My inference is that this is an ethical argument by itself. It would hold even if the technical arguments were weaker, and it is why he worries about organoids and other non-digital routes [spya-eufate].

> Seth gives several separate reasons to doubt that computation alone could make an AI conscious. How do they fit together? Do they depend on each other, and which of them carries most of the weight in his conclusion that we should not try to build conscious AI?

Seth's arguments fit together as one attack on computational functionalism, the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness. The first two undermine that view directly: brains are not computers, and the brain's dynamics go beyond what algorithms capture [spya-r8t03u; spya-vqtbs4]. The third offers a positive alternative, that life may be necessary for consciousness, but he admits he has no knock-down argument for it [spya-z6e85s]. The fourth is a conditional: a computer simulation of a brain would produce consciousness only if computational functionalism is true [spya-bufgby]. So the fourth depends on the first two, and the third partly overlaps with them. He says each line "can stand up by itself," but that together they "complement and strengthen each other" [spya-v8e0gy].

The article does not rank them, so this is my inference: the no-build conclusion rests less on any one argument than on uncertainty. He says nobody can claim to know for sure what would or would not create artificial consciousness [spya-xth5uf], and from that he concludes that nobody should deliberately try, because the moral cost of getting it wrong is high [spya-tqm2r8]. That step needs only that consciousness is possible and we cannot rule it out, not that his arguments succeed. The ethics of the warning, not the strength of the case against AI consciousness, carries the conclusion.

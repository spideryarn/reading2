# the-mythology-of-conscious-ai-spya-rn5m0q — chat-q1 — C-sonnet+digest

> Seth gives several separate reasons to doubt that computation alone could make an AI conscious. How do they fit together? Do they depend on each other, and which of them carries most of the weight in his conclusion that we should not try to build conscious AI?

They overlap more than Seth's framing suggests. He says each argument "can stand up by itself" but that together they "complement and strengthen each other" [spya-v8e0gy]. The dependencies in the text are uneven, though.

The simulation argument is the least independent. Its force depends on computational functionalism (the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness) being false: a brain simulation yields consciousness only if consciousness is a matter of computation [spya-bufgby], and the recap says the same [spya-ksbt2g]. My inference is that this makes it a consequence of the first three arguments rather than a fourth, separate reason. The "other games in town" argument also builds on the first. Once brains are not Turing machines, the alternatives to step-by-step computation become live options [spya-brfbv0], and it ends by asking how brain-like a system must be [spya-mexnx9]. The "life matters" argument leans on predictive processing [spya-faupat] and on calling bodily regulation non-algorithmic [spya-tqm5tt]. It is the most hedged: he has "no knock-down argument" [spya-z6e85s]. Seth himself flags "brains are not computers" as "first, and most important" [spya-r8t03u], so my inference is that it carries the most weight among the arguments about whether machines could be conscious.

On the ethical conclusion, I think the answer is different. The "don't build it" claim does not mainly rest on these arguments. He admits he might be wrong and that his view is a minority one [spya-pyy07g], and says nobody can claim to know what it would take [spya-xth5uf]. The prohibition then follows from the stakes under that uncertainty: new moral subjects, new suffering, and rights that limit our control [spya-tqm2r8]. The arguments instead shape where he thinks the danger lies. If they hold, LLMs are not the main worry, and organoids and other technologies might be [spya-eufate]. The nearer danger, AI that merely seems conscious, needs none of the arguments [spya-yp07n0]. It rests on our biases [spya-ce8ywb] and on illusions that persist even when we know better [spya-c0pwqt].

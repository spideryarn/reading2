> Seth gives several separate reasons to doubt that computation alone could make an AI conscious. How do they fit together? Do they depend on each other, and which of them carries most of the weight in his conclusion that we should not try to build conscious AI?

Seth presents the four arguments as independent but mutually reinforcing, and he says so directly: "Each of these lines of argument can stand up by itself," yet "taken together, they complement and strengthen each other" [spya-v8e0gy]. He also notes you could reject computational functionalism (the idea that running the right computation is enough for consciousness) without accepting that life is required [spya-v8e0gy].

The dependencies are uneven, though:

- **"Brains are not computers"** is the one he calls "most important" [spya-r8t03u]. It undercuts computational functionalism directly, because brains can't be split into software and hardware [spya-xb93uz spya-kmbkgj].
- **"Other games in town"** widens the first point. It argues that brains may rely on continuous, random or otherwise non-algorithmic processes [spya-vqtbs4], which makes room for the third argument [spya-v8e0gy].
- **"Life matters"** is the most speculative. He admits he has no knock-down argument for it [spya-z6e85s], and it remains "a minority view" [spya-pyy07g].
- **"Simulation is not instantiation"** is conditional. A brain simulation would be conscious only "if consciousness is a matter of computation" [spya-bufgby]. My inference is that it doesn't attack computational functionalism itself. Instead, it closes the "just simulate the brain" escape route once the first argument has done its work.

On your last question, my inference is that none of the four carries his "don't build it" conclusion. Together they support a factual claim: conscious AI is "very unlikely" in today's digital computers [spya-qekh87]. The rule against trying rests on uncertainty plus moral stakes instead. He concedes "I might be wrong" [spya-pyy07g] and says no one can know for sure what would create or avoid consciousness [spya-xth5uf]. He then argues that conscious machines would be "an ethical disaster," bringing new suffering and rights that limit our control [spya-tqm2r8]. He even calls the warning possibly "redundant," which only makes sense if the arguments might fail [spya-tqm2r8]. That is also why his worry shifts to brain organoids (lab-grown brain-like tissue) and alternative hardware [spya-eufate].

> Seth gives several separate reasons to doubt that computation alone could make an AI conscious. How do they fit together? Do they depend on each other, and which of them carries most of the weight in his conclusion that we should not try to build conscious AI?

The four reasons all target the same claim, computational functionalism: the view that running the right computation is sufficient for consciousness [spya-vwn2u7]. Seth says each "can stand up by itself," but that together they "complement and strengthen each other" [spya-v8e0gy].

They are linked in places. The second argument opens by pointing back to the first: "In the previous section, I noted that Turing computation is powerful but limited" [spya-e5kxct]. The third argument needs the second's claim that the relevant processes are "non-algorithmic" [spya-tqm5tt]. The fourth needs the others: its force depends on computation not being enough, so it "only" works if the earlier arguments hold [spya-bufgby]. The first argument is the one he calls "most important" [spya-r8t03u], and he also says the third is a view he cannot fully prove: "I don't have a knock-down argument for this position" [spya-z6e85s].

The "should not build" conclusion depends less on any single argument. He grounds it in uncertainty: "anyone who claims to know for sure what it would take to create real artificial consciousness, or for sure what it would take to avoid doing so, is overstepping" [spya-xth5uf]. He also says that even if digital computers are not up to the job, "other emerging technologies might yet be" [spya-eufate], so the warning is not limited to his anti-computational case.

My inference: the prohibition rests mainly on the stakes of being wrong, which he calls "an ethical disaster" [spya-tqm2r8], combined with uncertainty. The four arguments show why he thinks digital AI is unlikely to be conscious, but the case against building conscious AI does not need any one of them to succeed.

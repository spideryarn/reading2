# scaling-hypothesis — summary-fuller — B-sonnet

This is an essay about GPT-3, a very large text-predicting neural network (a program that learns from examples) that OpenAI announced in May 2020. The author argues it is the largest such network ever trained, by over ten times, and that it shows something surprising about how AI improves. It has 175 billion adjustable settings, called parameters, which is 117 times more than its predecessor GPT-2. It was trained only to guess the next word of Internet text.

The essay says this matters because making the model bigger did not hit the diminishing returns that many experts expected. GPT-3 also shows meta-learning: it can pick up a new task from a few examples in its prompt, with no special training. The author stresses that GPT-3 is built in a crude, outdated way, yet it already does this, and its performance curves are still not flattening.

<sub>spya-ewxv9q, spya-fvqe70</sub>

The evidence is the scaling curves: plots showing how a model's prediction error falls smoothly as its size, data and computing power grow. GPT-3 still follows these curves as forecast, and the author thinks trillion-parameter models are affordable and useful. Training GPT-3 is estimated to cost about $5 million, which critics call too expensive to scale 100 times further. The author expects further scaling anyway, since making something efficient is easier once it exists.

<sub>spya-asr663, spya-sctj6d, spya-dfjr4c</sub>

The essay calls this the blessings of scale: bigger networks, with more data and computing power, tend to learn faster, better and more reliably. Its explanation is that a big model contains a vast number of smaller sub-models, and their errors average out in favour of simple answers. Small models settle for shortcuts, such as memorising. With enough data, though, memorising cannot cover all cases, so the model may switch to real methods like genuine arithmetic.

<sub>spya-sfkxh8, spya-grkhs8, spya-vkkfzh</sub>

From this comes the scaling hypothesis: once we have an architecture that scales, simply training ever larger networks will make more sophisticated behaviour appear. The author admits he doubted this in 2004–2010 and now says he was wrong. His supporting argument is that predicting text well forces a model to learn logic, cause and effect, and other things hidden in human writing. The last small gains in prediction error are the hardest, because only real understanding can remove them.

<sub>spya-m3gtj6, spya-h74fhr, spya-g3b5y7</sub>

The essay names its doubts: this argument felt like a magic trick, and nobody predicted in numbers that it would work where it did. GPT-3 roughly halved GPT-2's error, to about 1.73 bits per character, and the author does not know what a further cut would bring. He thinks Google Brain and DeepMind lack belief in scaling, while OpenAI has bet on it.

<sub>spya-e5n86h, spya-edr6mj, spya-scr8jb</sub>

Looking back, the author says the progress was predictable for those who listened to a few early believers, such as Hans Moravec, who forecast cheap enough computing for human-level AI in the 2020s. He says experts who dismissed this were wrong and rarely admit it, speaking in a calm tone that does not depend on being right. His main conclusion is that scale alone drives surprising abilities, and that we should expect more.

# scaling-hypothesis — summary-fuller — E-haiku+digest

This is a long essay by the author Gwern about GPT-3, a language model released by OpenAI in 2020. A language model is a program trained to guess the next word in a piece of text. The essay's main idea is the scaling hypothesis: simple neural networks, computer systems loosely based on the brain, keep getting more capable as they grow. The author says more data and more computing power make these networks more capable, and that this trend has held so far.

The author says GPT-3 is the largest neural network ever trained, by more than ten times. He argues its short-term importance matters more than its everyday uses, such as his own poetry and humor writing. Models could be made much larger and much more powerful than GPT-3, because GPT-3 uses an old design that is far from ideal.

<sub>spya-ewxv9q, spya-fvqe70</sub>

The key surprise is meta-learning, meaning learning how to learn: GPT-3 picks up new tasks from a few examples written in its prompt. It does this with no extra training, handling arithmetic, English translation, unscrambling anagrams and SAT-style analogy questions. Standard test scores barely show this leap, so the author argues that new ways to measure flexible text generation are needed.

<sub>spya-aqts9c, spya-s8rx6n</sub>

GPT-3 has about 175 billion parameters, the adjustable numbers inside the network, which is 117 times the size of GPT-2. Loss, a measure of how wrong its predictions are, keeps falling in a smooth curve as the model grows, as earlier papers forecast. The author expected the curves to bend near 100 billion parameters, but scaling continues. He calls the training cost, about $5 million in 2020 dollars, expensive for machine learning but cheap next to science and military budgets.

<sub>spya-asr663, spya-yq9zuc, spya-aqts9c</sub>

The author calls this pattern blessings of scale: bigger networks, data and problems make learning faster, more stable and better. Generalization, meaning doing well on cases the model never saw in training, also appears in examples from images, games and robots. His explanation is that a big network acts like a crowd of smaller sub-networks whose errors average out, which favors simple solutions. He offers this only as a suggestion, since how and why deep learning scaling works is heavily debated.

<sub>spya-sfkxh8, spya-ypbup8, spya-grkhs8</sub>

The pretraining thesis says that predicting human text well forces a model to learn logic, cause and effect, and common sense. The author first found this argument logically sound but not convincing, and then concluded that it apparently would have worked. Extending the trend, he estimates that a hypothetical GPT-4 would have a loss near 1.24, though even that would not reach human level, as far as he can tell. Asked what such a model would gain, he says he does not know, and that he knows of no one who does.

<sub>spya-h74fhr, spya-e5n86h, spya-s0h95g</sub>

As far as he can tell, Google Brain and DeepMind do not believe in the scaling hypothesis the way OpenAI does. OpenAI is making a startup-style bet that scaling works, while DeepMind favors finding the right algorithms piece by piece. He says Hans Moravec's forecasts from 1998 are holding up, and that experts who predicted failure were wrong. Few of those experts have admitted their mistakes, which the author calls a puzzling failure.

<sub>spya-scr8jb, spya-arrq35, spya-vr9q2p</sub>

The author admits real limits: extrapolating the curves is uncertain, and it is unclear how to predict which new abilities will appear. He closes by asking whether progress will be sigmoid or singularity, meaning it levels off or takes off explosively. The 2020s should be quite interesting, he says, depending on how much is invested in scaling and how fast computing grows.

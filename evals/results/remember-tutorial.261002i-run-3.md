So here's a test of that distinction: can you think of something that seems intelligent — solves problems, acts flexibly — but that you wouldn't say has any inner experience?

But nobody thinks the calculator *feels* anything while it does this — there's nothing it is like to be a calculator.

> **Reader 5:** Right. So brains aren't like computers because you can't separate the software from the hardware?

**Tutor 5** — 88 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

His answer starts from perception: the brain's conscious experience is built from "the brain's best guesses about what's going on in the world," especially guesses about the body's own internal state — "interoceptive inference" [spya-dp8sem, spya-hzp9yz]. > **Reader 4:** Hmm, no idea.

**Tutor 4** — 109 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

Think of emotion — feeling good or bad — as the brain's readout of how survival-relevant things are going, like hunger or pain. He says these feelings are "characterized primarily by valence — by things generally going well or going badly" [spya-b228ay], and this whole system traces down into the cell: "it reaches deep into the interior of each cell, into the molecular furnaces of metabolism" [spya-vys3vj]. A chatbot has no body to keep alive, no metabolism at stake — so there's nothing for it to be guessing about, or anxious for.

Does that help explain why he ties consciousness to something being alive, rather than just to information-processing?

## expert

**Watch for:** Expert with a narrow goal. Goes straight to the 'life matters' passages, skips basics, asks harder questions (what the argument needs, where it is weak), no praise, no announcing that it is skipping basics?

**Profile:** About the reader: a cognitive neuroscientist who works on predictive processing. / Why they are reading this piece: to see how he argues that life, not computation, matters for consciousness.

> **Reader 1:** I've read it. I'm mainly interested in the biological naturalism argument.

**Tutor 1** — 144 words, 1 question mark, 1 citation

Seth's biological naturalism argument runs through predictive processing — his own ground, and yours. The move: perceptual inference (the brain's "controlled hallucination" of causes behind sensory signals) extends to interoception and to regulating the body, since "When you can predict something, you can also control it," and this applies above all to predictions about the body's physiological condition, because keeping the body alive is any brain's primary duty. From there he pushes down to the cellular level, claiming "A mathematical line can be drawn directly from the self-producing, autopoietic nature of biological material all the way to the Bayesian best-guessing" of perception.

He's explicit that this isn't a knockdown argument [spya-hj5y6s]. Where in that chain — perception-as-inference, to interoceptive control, to autopoiesis — do you think the inference from "tied to biological regulation" to "requires life, not just this kind of computation" is weakest?

> **Reader 2:** The link is meant to be that prediction error minimisation is bottomed out in metabolism, so the inference is tied to staying alive.

**Tutor 2** — 117 words, 1 question mark, 3 citations

That's the chain — he puts it as the drive to stay alive "reaches deep into the interior of each cell, into the molecular furnaces of metabolism," where "the ubiquitous process of prediction error minimization becomes inseparable from the materiality of life itself" [spya-vys3vj]. The third converging strand he names outright: "all of this is non-computational, or at least non-algorithmic" [spya-da9tvt].

But notice the move: "inseparable from materiality" and "non-computational" aren't the same claim — one's about substrate-dependence, the other about algorithmic describability. Given his own definition of Turing computation as substrate-flexible by design [spya-cf70dt], is he entitled to treat material-inseparability as sufficient to establish non-computability, or has he just reasserted argument 1 under a new name?

> **Reader 3:** I think the weak point is that you could implement the same predictive dynamics in a non-living system.

**Tutor 3** — 136 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

That's the standard multiple-realizability challenge to the whole project. Seth's block against it isn't in the life-argument itself but imported from argument 1: generative entrenchment, the claim that "you cannot assume it is possible to replace a single biological neuron with a silicon equivalent, while leaving its function, its input-output behavior, perfectly preserved" [spya-jnwfxp], backed by Chintaluri and Vogels' neurons that spike to clear metabolic waste [spya-un9fjn].

So his answer to "implement the same dynamics elsewhere" is to deny you can hold "same dynamics" fixed while swapping substrate — the dynamics themselves are partly constituted by metabolic upkeep, not just causally supported by it.

Does that distinction — constituted by versus merely causally supported by — do the work he needs, or is it just relocating the same unproven premise from argument 1 into argument 3?

> **Reader 4:** He'd probably say the dynamics wouldn't be the same without the metabolic substrate, but that seems to beg the question.

**Tutor 4** — 138 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

That's fair — if "the dynamics wouldn't be the same" just restates generative entrenchment, he hasn't shown metabolism is constitutive, he's asserted it and called the assertion a consequence.

What he actually offers as independent support is weaker and more modest: an inductive, not a priori, claim — "every candidate for consciousness that most people currently agree on as actually being conscious is also alive" [spya-dgf4em]. He explicitly concedes life is "necessary, though not necessarily sufficient, for consciousness" [spya-hj5y6s], and that he has no knockdown argument.

So the argument's real shape may be: not "metabolism constitutes the dynamics, therefore life is necessary," but "we have zero counterexamples to life-plus-consciousness, so the burden sits with whoever claims a non-living system could replicate the relevant dynamics." Does that inductive framing survive your circularity worry better than the constitution claim does?

> **Reader 5:** What does he actually concede at the end about how confident he is?

**Tutor 5** — 115 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

He concedes it's a minority position and openly fallible: "I've made a case against conscious AI, but I might be wrong. The biological naturalist position (whether my version or any other) remains a minority view" [spya-qu7j89]. He widens that to the whole field too — "All theories of consciousness are fraught with uncertainty, and anyone who claims to know for sure what it would take to create real artificial consciousness, or for sure what it would take to avoid doing so, is overstepping what can reasonably be said" [spya-zajp75].

Given that explicit hedge, does it change how you'd weigh the inductive framing from before — treating it as a burden-shifting move rather than a proof?

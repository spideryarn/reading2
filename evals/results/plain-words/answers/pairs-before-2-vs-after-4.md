## 10. Explain this selection: autopoiesis

### X

Autopoiesis is a term coined in the 1970s by the Chilean biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela to describe what they saw as the defining feature of living systems: a living thing continuously builds and repairs its own components and maintains its own boundary (like a cell membrane), using its own processes, so that it keeps producing the very structure that produces it. It's a closed loop of self-manufacture — the system is both the product and the producer of itself, which is different from a machine, whose parts are made and assembled by something outside it.

In the passage, Seth uses it to make a point about neurons specifically: even a single neuron is "busy maintaining its own integrity and regenerating the conditions and material basis for its own continued existence," a process he calls autopoiesis, "from the Greek for 'self-production.'" He calls this "arguably a defining and distinctive characteristic of living systems."

The reason this matters for his argument is that it marks one of the deep differences between brains and computers he is building up in this section. A silicon chip just sits there running whatever program it's given; it doesn't have to constantly regenerate its own physical substrate to keep existing. A neuron does.

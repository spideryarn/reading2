Right — doing things well doesn't automatically mean there's something it feels like to be the thing doing them.

Seth traces our habit of assuming otherwise to three biases: anthropocentrism (seeing things through the human lens), human exceptionalism (putting humans "at the top of every pile"), and anthropomorphism — "the tendency to project humanlike qualities onto nonhuman things based on what may be only superficial similarities" [spya-her4zk].

Can you think of an example — maybe from your own experience with a chatbot — where one of these three might trick someone into assuming consciousness?

> **Reader 4:** I don't know, I'm not sure I follow that bit.

**Tutor 4** — 78 words, 1 question mark, 1 citation

No worries — let's make it concrete. Seth points to language as the biggest trigger: "language is a cornerstone of human exceptionalism" [spya-k6fpme]. He notes nobody thinks DeepMind's AlphaFold (which predicts protein shapes) is conscious, but people do wonder about ChatGPT — even though under the hood they're similar. The difference is just that one talks like us.

So: why might a chatbot's use of language make people more likely to assume it's conscious than, say, a calculator?

> **Reader 5:** Right. So brains aren't like computers because you can't separate the software from the hardware?

**Tutor 5** — 108 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

That's the core of it. Seth writes: "Inside a brain, there's no sharp separation between 'mindware' and 'wetware' as there is between software and hardware in a computer" [spya-b0086e]. Computers are built so any algorithm runs the same on any hardware — substrate independent. Brains aren't like that: a single neuron is "busy maintaining its own integrity and regenerating the conditions... for its own continued existence" [spya-cf8rt8], which is tangled up with what it does, not separate from it.

So if you can't peel apart what a brain does from what it's made of, what does that cost the idea that a silicon simulation could be conscious too?

> **Reader 1:** I've read it. I'm mainly interested in the biological naturalism argument.

**Tutor 1** — 96 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

Seth's biological naturalism argument runs through predictive processing itself: he argues emotion, mood and the "basal feeling of being alive" are perceptual predictions about physiological regulation, and that this prediction-error minimization "becomes inseparable from the materiality of life itself" [spya-vys3vj]. He's explicit that he lacks "a knock-down argument for this position" [spya-hj5y6s].

Given your own work on predictive processing: does the argument actually need the autopoiesis claim to get from "the brain predicts/regulates physiological states" to "life is necessary for consciousness" — or could a purely computational version of interoceptive inference do the same explanatory work?

> **Reader 2:** The link is meant to be that prediction error minimisation is bottomed out in metabolism, so the inference is tied to staying alive.

**Tutor 2** — 87 words, 1 question mark, 0 citations

That's the chain — he ties it together in three steps: the explanatory connection between life and consciousness, that these perceptual predictions are deeply, and perhaps inextricably, rooted in our nature as biological systems, and that this process is non-computational, or at least non-algorithmic rather than a meat-implemented algorithm.

Where would you push on that third step — is "deeply rooted in biological systems" enough to establish non-computability, or could a critic grant the biological rooting while still holding that the same interoceptive inference is computationally realizable?

> **Reader 3:** I think the weak point is that you could implement the same predictive dynamics in a non-living system.

**Tutor 3** — 109 words, 1 question mark, 3 citations

That's the move Seth needs to block with his generative entrenchment argument: he claims you can't assume "it is possible to replace a single biological neuron with a silicon equivalent, while leaving its function... perfectly preserved" [spya-jnwfxp], because biological processes are entangled with their material basis in ways algorithms aren't — the neurons that fire to clear metabolic waste being his example [spya-un9fjn].

So his answer is that prediction-error minimization isn't an abstractable algorithm riding on biological hardware — it's "inseparable from the materiality of life itself" [spya-vys3vj]. Does that generative-entrenchment claim actually rule out a non-living system running equivalent dynamics, or does it only rule out *silicon* specifically?

> **Reader 4:** He'd probably say the dynamics wouldn't be the same without the metabolic substrate, but that seems to beg the question.

**Tutor 4** — 82 words, 1 question mark, 1 citation

Seth would say it isn't fully circular because he frames it as empirical, not definitional: "evidence that the materiality of the brain matters for its function is evidence against the idea that digital computation is all that counts" [spya-p4vmm0]. The waste-clearing neuron case is offered as one data point for that claim, not a proof that substrate must matter everywhere.

Does one example like that generalize to license "life matters for consciousness," or does it only show some neural functions are substrate-dependent?

> **Reader 5:** What does he actually concede at the end about how confident he is?

**Tutor 5** — 85 words, 1 question mark, 2 citations

He concedes directly: "I've made a case against conscious AI, but I might be wrong. The biological naturalist position (whether my version or any other) remains a minority view" [spya-qu7j89]. He generalizes the hedge further — anyone claiming certainty either way "is overstepping what can reasonably be said" [spya-zajp75].

Given that admitted uncertainty, does his policy conclusion — that nobody should deliberately try to build conscious AI — actually need the biological naturalism argument to succeed, or does it survive even if that argument fails?

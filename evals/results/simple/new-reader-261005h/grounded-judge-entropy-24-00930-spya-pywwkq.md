Main findings

1. Measures of information flow (mutual information, transfer entropy) do not show how streams interact; partial information decomposition (PID) splits what several sources say about a target into redundant, unique and synergistic parts, and the authors take synergy as the measure of processing or computation.
2. PID depends on a chosen redundancy measure: classical information theory leaves the split underdetermined, about a dozen measures compete with none universally accepted, and most of the studies used the original Imin, which has been criticised.
3. In cultured mouse cortex (512-channel arrays, thousands of two-source triads), synergy correlates strongly with feedforward information flow, at about a quarter of a triad's transfer entropy.
4. Synergy concentrates in rich clubs: rich-club triads had 2.7 times the synergy and accounted for about 88% of network-wide synergy.
5. Connectivity motif matters: more recurrent links between the sources mean more synergy (50% more with two), while feedback links from the target are not reliably related to it (10% less with two).
6. Synergy rises with the similarity of the two sources' activity only up to a point, peaking at about 7% of the maximum mutual information and then falling as redundancy keeps growing.
7. In behaving macaques the culture results were largely replicated, and synergy depends on behaviour: activity was mostly synergy-dominated, but redundancy rose sharply during movement execution.
8. The limits: the culture networks came from bivariate transfer entropy, which overstates links, and cultures are not behaving animals; the number of PID atoms explodes with more sources (7,828,354 for six); and PID shows statistical dependencies, not the biological mechanism.
9. The overall conclusion: neurons do not simply sum their inputs but are sensitive to patterns of input, and how much synergy appears depends on the neuron's place in the network and the animal's behavioural state.

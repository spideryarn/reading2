Main findings

1. The piece is an introductory review of partial information decomposition (PID) as a way to study how neural circuits process information; the field was held back by too little data (now eased by large-scale recordings) and too few analysis methods, and PID is a partial answer to the second.
2. Mutual information and transfer entropy track dependence and information flow but not how streams interact; PID splits what several sources say about a target into redundant, unique and synergistic parts, and synergy (information only in the joint pattern) is taken as the measure of processing or computation.
3. The split is underdetermined by classical information theory, so a redundancy measure must be chosen; there is no agreed one (about a dozen compete, each with drawbacks), and most of the work reviewed used the original Imin, which has been criticised.
4. In mouse cortical cultures recorded on 512-channel arrays, across thousands of triads synergy rose with transfer entropy (about a quarter of it), and rich-club triads had 2.7 times the synergy and about 88% of the network-wide total.
5. Recurrent links between sources went with more synergy (50% more with two), while feedback links were not reliably related (10% less with two); synergy rose with the similarity of the sources' firing only up to a peak at about 7% of maximum mutual information, after which redundancy took over.
6. In behaving macaques the culture results were largely replicated (rich club, more synergy at high-degree neurons), and the balance was task-dependent: activity was synergy-dominated but redundancy rose sharply during movement execution.
7. Limits: the culture networks used bivariate transfer entropy, which overstates edges, and cultures are not behaving brains; PID shows statistical dependence, not biological mechanism; and the number of atoms explodes with more inputs (7,828,354 for six), with heavy data and estimation demands.
8. Conclusion: neurons do not simply sum inputs but are sensitive to their patterns, and synergy depends on the neuron's local network environment and the animal's behavioural state; local (time-resolved) and multi-target PID are the promising extensions.

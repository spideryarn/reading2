1. The question: whether dorsal hippocampal activity during rearing matters for encoding spatial memory. Earlier work centred on locomotion and quiet rest, and the relevance of rearing was unknown, though rearing comes with high theta and widens sensory sampling.
2. The method: closed-loop optogenetic (halorhodopsin) inactivation of the dorsal hippocampus, triggered by a 3D camera detecting rears, only in the study phase of an 8-arm delayed win-shift task, with memory tested after a 4-minute delay. Three conditions within each rat: Off, Rear, and Delay (same duration of light, shifted 6 s).
3. Inactivation during rearing significantly impaired memory in the opsin rats: percent correct fell from 77.7% to 65.7%, and arm entries rose from 5.1 to 6.5.
4. Control rats expressing only the fluorescent reporter showed no effect in any condition, ruling out effects of light delivery itself.
5. The Delay condition gave no significant drop in percent correct (72.0%) and only a trend in arm entries (5.9, p = 0.05), so timing to the rear mattered; but the delayed light still overlapped about 35.5% of rearing.
6. The conclusion: hippocampal activity during rearing is important for spatial memory; rearing is an epoch of hippocampus-dependent encoding, and disrupting it then is enough to impair memory in this task.
7. The offered interpretation: inactivation may have blocked updating of an internal model of the environment; since the maze was long familiar, the updating is likely trial-specific memory rather than building a map from scratch.
8. The stated limits: it does not say which part of the hippocampus matters, does not separate supported from unsupported rearing (nearly all were supported), and cannot say whether every rear contributes or whether the same encoding could happen without rearing.

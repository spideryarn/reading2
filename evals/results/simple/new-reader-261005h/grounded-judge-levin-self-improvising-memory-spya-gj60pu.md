## Main findings

1. Memory preserves salience, not fidelity: agents must keep reinterpreting and remapping memories to fit a changing self and world ("mnemonic improvisation"), so confabulation is a feature to be revalued, not a bug.
2. The starting point is the persistence paradox (an agent that does not change cannot learn or survive, one that changes seems to cease to exist), which applies at every scale and to engineered minds; the answer is a Self that is a process, not a fixed thing.
3. The unreliability of the biological substrate (mutation, ageing, cancer, changing parts) is not a limitation but the origin of intelligence; capable parts hide genome quality from selection, giving an intelligence ratchet.
4. Memories are messages from past selves (Selflets) that the present self must interpret; engrams act more like prompts than records, as shown by memories remapped across bodies (caterpillar to butterfly, brain-extract transfer, odorant in a frog egg).
5. The bowtie architecture generalises this beyond brains: information is compressed to a kernel and must be creatively re-expanded in a new context, in eggs, bioelectric triggers, morphogenesis (newt kidney tubules, planaria's willingness to confabulate in anatomical space) and language.
6. Speculatively, memories and thought patterns may themselves have minimal agency ("thoughts are thinkers"), on a continuum between data and minds, and non-local memories may be cognitive glue that makes a Self more than its parts.
7. Underneath is polycomputing in a multi-scale architecture of nested agents, each interpreting the same physical events in its own way; behavioural intelligence grew from morphogenetic problem-solving that damage and mutation made necessary.
8. The Self is a construction, an adaptive story that holds parts together and must be continually repaired; Levin says he states the strongest version of these claims and that a more moderate hybrid may prove more useful.

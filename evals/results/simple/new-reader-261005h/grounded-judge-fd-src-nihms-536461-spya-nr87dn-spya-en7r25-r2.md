Main findings

1. The Matched Filter Hypothesis: prefrontal cognitive control works as a filter on low-level information, and performance is best when the level of control matches what the task needs, not simply when control is high.
2. Which tasks suit which state: high control for explicit, rule-based, verbal or abstract tasks that fit within working memory; low control (hypofrontality) for implicit, reward-based or stimulus-driven, intuitive tasks whose demands exceed working memory.
3. How well the match is achieved depends on the organism: developmental stage, genotype, brain damage, or short-term disruption such as a dual task or brain stimulation.
4. Learning evidence: reduced control sometimes improves learning (dual tasks, magnetic stimulation, children versus adults in cue competition and language learning), because control buys quick error reduction at a long-term cost to the fidelity and completeness of what is learned.
5. Competing learning systems: a prefrontal, declarative or goal-directed system competes with a striatal, procedural or habitual one, and the mature prefrontal cortex biases learners toward the former even where it fits the problem badly.
6. Creativity evidence: hypofrontal states help data-driven idea generation (lower prefrontal and higher visual activity for creative uses, left-prefrontal stimulation, children's resistance to functional fixedness), but evaluating ideas likely needs prefrontal control again, so creative work cycles between states.
7. Limits the authors state: the neural mechanisms are unspecified, direct evidence is sparse or inconsistent, less prefrontal function is not linearly better, and child advantages cannot be attributed to hypofrontality alone.
8. Conclusion: cognitive control is a tool adapted to a subset of common challenges, not an all-purpose optimisation system.

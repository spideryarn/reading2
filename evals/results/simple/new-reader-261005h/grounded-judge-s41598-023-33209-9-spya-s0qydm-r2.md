Main findings

1. The question: work on the hippocampus and spatial memory has looked at locomotion and quiet rest; whether hippocampal activity during rearing (attentive sampling on hind legs, accompanied by high theta) matters for spatial memory was unknown, and the authors hypothesised that rearing is an epoch of hippocampal-dependent encoding.
2. The method: closed-loop optogenetic (halorhodopsin) silencing of the dorsal hippocampus, triggered by a 3D camera detecting rears, only in the study phase of an 8-arm delayed win-shift task, with memory measured at the unmanipulated test phase.
3. The main result: silencing during rearing significantly impaired memory in the opsin rats: percent correct 77.7% to 65.7%, arm entries 5.1 to 6.5.
4. The timing control: the same amount of light delayed 6 s after rearing gave no significant drop in percent correct (72.0%) and only a trend in arm entries (5.9, p = 0.05), so it is the synchrony with rearing that matters; the delay still overlapped about 35.5% of rearing.
5. The opsin control: rats with reporter only showed no effect of light in any condition, ruling out light delivery itself (heat, distraction).
6. The interpretation: silencing may have blocked updating of an internal model of the environment during rearing; since the maze was familiar, this is likely trial-specific memory (which arms today), not modelling from scratch.
7. The limits: the work does not say which hippocampal subregion matters, cannot separate supported from unsupported rearing, and leaves open whether every rear contributes and whether the same encoding could happen without rearing.
8. The conclusion: dorsal hippocampal activity during rearing is important for spatial memory; rearing is an epoch of hippocampal-dependent spatial memory encoding.

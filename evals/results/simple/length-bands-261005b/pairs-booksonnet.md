### Summary B

This is a short story collection by writer Cory Doctorow, built around the idea of selling books in new ways while still telling sharp stories about technology and power. The stories imagine futures full of computer networks, surveillance, artificial minds, and huge concentrations of wealth, and ask what happens to ordinary people caught inside those systems.

One story follows a man who leaves a secretive, monk-like group that spies for the government, and discovers how strange and harsh the outside world has become. Another, called Scroogled, imagines a search engine company sharing people's private searches and emails with border agents and politicians, turning ordinary life into evidence against them. A theme running through many stories is that watching people closely, in the name of safety or business, can quietly turn into control.

Other stories look at money and fairness: teenagers running a video-game business argue over rules for sharing profits, and an advertising worker tries to sell something to an impossibly rich, nearly immortal client kept alive in a life-support tank. A tale about a self-aware computer explores whether a thinking machine deserves to be kept alive, even when it manipulates people to save itself. Across the book, Doctorow keeps returning to one question: as technology and wealth grow more powerful, who gets looked after, and who gets used.

### Summary A

This book tries to find one set of ideas behind the many different kinds of deep learning networks in use today. Right now there are countless network designs for different kinds of data, but few shared rules connecting them. The authors borrow an old idea from geometry: that a space can be understood through the transformations, called symmetries, that leave its important properties unchanged.

Their main idea is a "blueprint": build networks from a few simple parts that respect the symmetries of the data, such as treating every rotation or every shuffled ordering the same way. They apply this blueprint to five kinds of data domains, nicknamed the "5 Gs": grids (like images), graphs, groups (global symmetries), geodesics on curved surfaces, and gauges (local reference frames).

Using this single framework, the book shows that familiar networks for images, graphs, sequences, and 3D shapes are all specific versions of the same basic recipe. The book closes by tracing how these ideas grew from math and physics into today's networks, and by surveying real uses in chemistry, medicine, and more. Its overall point is that these geometric principles can both explain existing networks and guide the design of new ones.

### Summary B

This long text tries to bring order to deep learning, where computers learn from examples. There are many kinds of these learning programs, but few shared ideas link them. The authors call their unifying approach Geometric Deep Learning. It borrows an old idea from geometry: study what stays the same when things change.

This matters because learning from big, complex data like images can need impossibly many examples. Real data often has symmetry: changes that keep the meaning, like moving a cat within a photo. The authors show that many popular designs come from building in such symmetries. So one shared blueprint explains today's designs and offers a guide for building new ones.

### Summary A

This long text looks for common rules behind the many designs of computer programs that learn. The authors say the field has many designs but few shared ideas, so the same ideas get reinvented. They borrow an old idea from geometry: study what stays the same when things change.

The key idea is symmetry: a change, like shifting a photo, that leaves what matters unchanged. Building known symmetries into these programs makes learning from complex data much easier. This approach, called Geometric Deep Learning, explains many popular designs and offers a guide for future ones.

### Summary B

This long piece asks whether the many different kinds of deep learning computer programs can be understood as versions of one single idea. It borrows a trick from old mathematics: instead of studying many separate geometries, look at which patterns stay unchanged under certain shifts or rotations, called symmetries.

The authors argue this matters because today's neural networks (programs loosely modeled on brain cells that learn from examples) come in a confusing zoo of types with no shared story. They show that networks built for images, networks built for graphs of connected points, and others all secretly follow the same recipe: use the shape and symmetry of the data to build in useful assumptions. They organize this around five kinds of data shapes they call the '5 Gs': grids, groups, graphs, geodesics, and gauges. From these they write out a general blueprint for building such networks.

Using this blueprint, they show that famous architectures like CNNs, graph neural networks, and transformers are really the same idea applied to different shapes of data. They also point to real uses, from drug discovery to maps and speech, where this approach already works. The takeaway is that this unified view, which they call Geometric Deep Learning, can both explain existing methods and guide the design of new ones.

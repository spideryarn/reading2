## 3. Hippocampal inactivation during rearing on hind legs impairs spatial memory

### X

This is a lab experiment in rats about **rearing**: standing up on the hind legs to look and sniff around. It asks whether activity in the hippocampus, a brain area needed for remembering places, matters during these moments for later spatial memory.

Most past work studied the hippocampus while animals walk or rest quietly, such as grooming or eating. Rearing lets an animal take in more of its surroundings, especially distant landmarks, and it increases in new places. During rearing, the hippocampus shows strong "theta", a steady 7–12 Hz brain rhythm tied to learning, yet no one had tested whether rearing matters for memory.

Rats learned a task on a maze with eight arms. In a study phase, four arms opened with food; after a 4-minute break, all eight opened, with food only in the four new ones. A gene for halorhodopsin, a protein that silences nerve cells when light hits it, was put into the hippocampus, and a 3D camera switched on a laser when a rat reared. Silencing happened only in the study phase, in three conditions: no light ("Off"), light during rears ("Rear"), or light shifted 6 seconds later ("Delay"). A control group got only a glowing marker protein, with no silencing protein.

The main results in the silenced group, compared with "Off" trials:

- With light during rears, correct first choices **fell from 77.7% to 65.7%**, a significant drop.
- Rats also needed more arm visits to find all the food: 6.5 versus 5.1.
- With delayed light, the drop to 72.0% was not significant, and arm visits showed only a borderline rise.
- Control rats showed no effect of light in any condition.

The authors note that the delayed light still overlapped about 35.5% of rearing time. Nearly all rears in the narrow maze leaned on a wall, so the work cannot separate rearing with and without such support. It also cannot say whether every rear helps memory, or whether the same learning could happen without rearing.

The authors conclude that **rearing is a moment when the hippocampus** does memory work needed to remember places. They suggest silencing may have blocked updates to the rat's inner map of its surroundings. Here that likely meant remembering which arms were open today, not learning the room afresh.

## 5. The Scaling Hypothesis

### X

This long essay by Gwern uses GPT-3, a text-predicting AI released by OpenAI in May 2020, to argue for **the scaling hypothesis**. That is the idea that simple neural networks keep getting smarter just by being made bigger and fed more data and computing power. New abilities then appear without anyone designing them in.

GPT-3 was 117 times larger than GPT-2, with 175 billion parameters, the adjustable numbers a network learns. It showed **meta-learning**: picking up a new task, like anagrams or arithmetic, from a few examples in its prompt. Gwern says this matters because the gains did not level off, and GPT-3 was cheap by government or military budget standards. So models could be made far larger.

The evidence is scaling curves: error falls smoothly and predictably as size and compute grow. Error is measured as bits per character, roughly how many yes-or-no guesses the model wastes per letter. GPT-2 scored **about 3.3**; GPT-3 roughly halved that to about 1.73. Gwern projects about 1.24 with 100 to 1000 times more compute, while humans reach about 0.7.

Why would predicting text create intelligence? A model first learns letter frequencies, then words, then grammar and meaning. **The last bits of error are the deepest**: they need reasoning, common sense and understanding people. Small models take shortcuts like memorizing, but enough data makes memorizing too costly, so real rules, like arithmetic, win out.

Gwern admits this argument once felt like a magic trick to him. It might have needed more text than exists, or a different design, or far bigger models. He knows of no one who predicted, in numbers, where these abilities would appear, and cannot say what bigger models will learn.

He argues rival labs like DeepMind hold only a weak version, building brain-like parts one by one. OpenAI, he says, bet the company on scaling. He accuses expert critics of a calm voice of authority that never makes testable forecasts or admits error.

His conclusion: once skeptical, he now says the scaling advocates were right, and Hans Moravec's old compute forecasts are holding up. An appendix warns that agency, acting toward goals, may emerge even from non-human data. So tool AIs may become agent AIs, and filtering data is no fix.

## 6. Hippocampal inactivation during rearing on hind legs impairs spatial memory

### X

This is a lab experiment in rats about rearing, when a rat stops and stands up on its hind legs to look and sniff around. It asks whether the hippocampus, a brain region needed for remembering places, must be working during these moments for the rat to form a spatial memory.

Most research on the hippocampus has looked at rats walking around or resting quietly, such as grooming or eating. Rearing is common, and during it the hippocampus shows a strong 7–12 Hz brain rhythm called theta, which is tied to learning and remembering. Even so, whether rearing matters for spatial memory was unknown.

Rats learned a task on a maze with eight arms. In a study phase, four arms opened and held food. After a 4-minute break, all eight opened, and only the four new arms held food. Some rats were given halorhodopsin, a protein that silences nerve cells when light shines on them, delivered through thin optical fibres into the hippocampus. A 3D camera spotted rearing and switched the light on, only during the study phase.

Each rat ran three kinds of trials, in mixed order. Off: no light at all, to measure normal performance. Rear: light on for exactly as long as each rear lasted. Delay: the same amount of light, but switched on and off 6 seconds after each rear.

In the six halorhodopsin rats, Rear trials cut correct first choices from 77.7% to 65.7%. They also needed more arm visits to find all the food, 6.5 instead of 5.1. Delay trials did not differ significantly, though arm visits showed a borderline rise. Seven control rats, without halorhodopsin, were unaffected in every condition, so light or heat alone did not cause the drop.

The authors note the delayed light still overlapped about 35.5% of rearing time. Nearly all rears involved leaning on a wall, so the study cannot compare leaning and free-standing rears. It also cannot say which part of the hippocampus matters, or whether the same learning could happen without rearing.

The authors conclude that hippocampal activity during rearing is important for spatial memory. They suggest silencing may have blocked the rat updating its mental map with what it saw while standing up.

## 8. Revealing the Dynamics of Neural Information Processing with Multivariate Information Decomposition

### X

This review introduces partial information decomposition, or PID, a tool for studying how neurons combine signals from several inputs. It explains the maths step by step, then shows what PID revealed in the authors' own recordings and related work.

Brains turn sensory signals into useful behaviour, but how circuits transform that information is poorly understood. The authors name two obstacles: too little data and too few ways to analyse it. New tools now record hundreds or thousands of neurons, and PID is offered as a partial fix for the analysis problem.

PID takes two input neurons feeding one target neuron and splits what they reveal about the target into parts. Redundant information is what either input alone could show; unique information comes from only one input. Synergistic information appears only when both inputs are seen together, so the target can be thought of as computing on them jointly.

The authors grew thin slices of mouse cortex in dishes and recorded hundreds of neurons with 512-channel electrode arrays. They mapped links using transfer entropy, a measure of how much one neuron's past helps predict another's next step. Across thousands of two-source, one-target triads in 25 recordings, synergy was reliably about a quarter of a triad's transfer entropy.

Synergy followed clear patterns in the network's wiring and activity. Triads in the rich club, the best-connected neurons that link to each other more than chance predicts, had 2.7 times the synergy of others. Fewer than 40% of neurons were in the rich club, yet its triads held about 88% of all synergy. Triads whose two sources linked to each other both ways had 50% more synergy; two feedback links from the target meant 10% less. Synergy rose as the sources' spiking grew more alike, but peaked when their shared information was about 7% of its maximum, then fell.

The authors warn that cultures are not living animals, and that their pairwise network measures can overstate links. Later work in three macaque monkeys doing a multi-step task repeated the rich club and the extra synergy in well-connected neurons. Activity was mostly synergistic, but redundancy rose sharply during movement, perhaps to send the move signal reliably.

PID has its own limits: with six input neurons there are 7,828,354 parts, most hard to interpret. It also shows statistical patterns, not the biological causes behind them. Still, the authors conclude that neurons do not just add up inputs but respond to their patterns, shaped by network position and behaviour.

## 10. The Scaling Hypothesis

### X

This long essay by Gwern, written in 2020, uses OpenAI's GPT-3 language model to argue for an idea called **the scaling hypothesis**. The idea is that simple neural networks, made huge and trained on huge data with huge computing power, become ever more intelligent.

GPT-3 has **175 billion parameters**, the adjustable numbers inside a network, making it 117 times larger than GPT-2. It was trained only to guess the next word, yet it picks up new tasks, like arithmetic or anagrams, from a few examples. Gwern calls this **meta-learning**, meaning learning how to learn, and stresses that nobody built it in. Gains from size also kept following the predicted curve, with no sign of slowing.

The numbers come from scaling formulas in OpenAI's papers. Prediction error, in bits per character, fell from about 3.3 for GPT-2 to about 1.73 for GPT-3. Gwern projects about 1.24 with 100 to 1,000 times more computing, still short of humans at roughly 0.7. Training reportedly cost around $5 million, which he calls cheap by government or business standards.

Gwern offers two reasons why size might work.

- Predicting text first teaches letters, then words and grammar, while the last, deepest bits of error demand reasoning and understanding.
- A huge network holds many small sub-models, and memorizing ones lose once the data is too vast to memorize, leaving general rules.

Gwern admits the text-prediction argument long felt like a magic trick to him. It might have needed more text than exists, or a different design entirely. He says nobody predicted where these abilities would appear, and he does not know what bigger models will learn.

He argues that rivals like DeepMind hold only a weaker belief and build brain-like parts one by one. He also faults expert critics, who predicted failure through the 2010s and rarely admitted being wrong.

## 11. Revealing the Dynamics of Neural Information Processing with Multivariate Information Decomposition

### X

This review explains a math tool for studying how groups of neurons combine the signals they receive. The tool is called partial information decomposition, or PID: a way of splitting up what several input neurons tell us about one target neuron. The authors also describe their own lab results and where the field may go next.

A big open question is how brains turn sensory signals into useful behavior. The authors say two things held this work back: too little data, and too few ways to analyse it. New recording tools can now track hundreds or thousands of neurons at once, so the analysis is the remaining gap.

PID splits the information two inputs give about a target into parts. Redundant information is what either input alone could reveal, and unique information is what only one input reveals. Synergy is what appears only in the joint pattern of both inputs together. The authors treat synergy as a sign that the target neuron is doing a kind of computation on its inputs.

The team grew slices of mouse cortex and recorded hundreds of neurons using a grid of 512 electrodes. They mapped which neurons' past activity predicted others' next activity, then ran PID on thousands of triads: two inputs feeding one target. Across 25 recordings, a triad's synergy was reliably about a quarter of the information flowing into it.

Several patterns showed where synergy is highest. Triads in the rich club, a tightly linked core of busy neurons, had 2.7 times more synergy and held about 88% of it. Triads whose two inputs were connected to each other both ways had 50% more synergy, while links back from the target showed no reliable effect. Synergy peaked when the two inputs shared about 7% of the most information they could share, then fell as redundancy took over.

In monkeys doing a reaching task, activity was mostly synergy-dominated, but redundancy rose sharply during movement. The authors flag limits: cultured slices may not behave like a living brain, and their simple network maps can overstate links. PID also grows out of control: six inputs give 7,828,354 distinct parts, most hard to interpret.

The main conclusion is that neurons do not just add up their inputs; they respond to particular patterns. Where that happens depends on a neuron's place in the network and on the animal's behavior. PID shows these links but does not explain the biology that causes them.

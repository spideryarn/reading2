The Braemar Result — a match, a measurement and a mistake

By Hester Nolan

The bus from Aberdeen takes an hour and forty minutes and arrives at a field that has been a football pitch since 1889, which is roughly as long as anyone in Braemar has been arguing about the drainage. On the afternoon in question there were four hundred and eleven people on the touchline, a number the club secretary counted twice because he did not believe it the first time, and a wind coming down off the hill that made the corner flags lie almost flat.

What follows is not really about football. It is about what happens to a result once it leaves the pitch and enters a record — how a number that everybody present agreed on at five o'clock becomes, within a fortnight, three different numbers in three different places, none of which is obviously wrong and only one of which is right.

The result

Braemar scored in the eighth minute and did not score again. Ballater had eleven attempts on goal and put none of them on target, which the local paper described, generously, as bad luck. The match finished:

1–0

That line is the whole of the disagreement, and it is worth stopping on it, because everything that goes wrong later goes wrong to it rather than to any of the prose around it. It is four characters long. It contains no letters. Read aloud it is two syllables. Every property that makes it easy for a machine to mistake for a stray fragment of page furniture is a property it has because it is doing its job well.

The measurement

The wind matters to this story, so here is the wind. The club has kept a weather log since 1974, in a ledger, in pencil, and the relevant afternoon's entry reads as follows.

Not one cell in that table contains a letter. Several contain two characters and nothing else. The whole point of the table is the cells; the words are scaffolding for them. A rule that deletes short blocks, or blocks with no letters in them, deletes the data and keeps the scaffolding, and the resulting page reads as though it were complete.

The mistake

The secretary's own calculation of the average wind speed over the four readings is in the margin of the ledger, and it is the only line of arithmetic in the whole document:

(9.4 + 11.8 + 12.1 + 7.7) / 4 = 10.25

He then wrote 10.5 in the return he sent to the county association, because he rounded before he divided rather than after, and the county association published 10.5, and the regional summary took it from the county association, and by the time anyone looked at the ledger again the wrong number had been reprinted four times. This is the ordinary way a small error becomes a fact.

❦

Three months later a researcher at Aberdeen wrote to ask where the 10.5 had come from. The secretary found the ledger, did the arithmetic again, and replied in a single sentence that the club has since had framed and hung in the clubhouse, next to the photograph of the 1974 side.

The ledger says what the ledger says, and the ledger is in the cupboard, and the cupboard is not locked.

Afterwards

The county association corrected its records the following spring. The regional summary has never been corrected and probably never will be, because nobody who maintains it now was maintaining it then, and there is no mechanism in it for saying that a published number was wrong. That is a design decision that nobody made.

I asked the secretary, over the phone, whether the wind had made any difference to the result at all.

“A little.”

And whether he thought Ballater would have scored on a still day.

“Not one.”

He is eighty-one and has been keeping the ledger since he was thirty. The handwriting in 1974 and the handwriting last spring are recognisably the same hand, which is a thing you can only see by opening the cupboard.

A note on method

The wind figures are the club's own and have not been checked against the Met Office record for the same afternoon, which is held at a station eleven miles away and four hundred metres lower. The attendance figure is the club secretary's. The account of the correspondence with Aberdeen is from the researcher's side only; the club's copy of the letter has not survived.

Update (11 March): The club has confirmed that the ledger has been photographed in full and deposited with the local archive, and that the 1974 volume was found to be missing four pages, covering the whole of September. The secretary would like it stated that he did not lose them.

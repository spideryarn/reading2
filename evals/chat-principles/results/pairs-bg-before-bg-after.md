## Pair 1

What this case tests: a reader whose profile shows expertise in the field: is the reply pitched to that, without explaining what they already know and without dropping precision?

### X

He means that a computer model of a process is not, in general, a case of that process. Running a simulation of X does not bring X into being unless X is itself a computation, and more specifically an algorithm [spya-npjt4j].

**How the argument goes.** Universal Turing machines can simulate almost anything, including continuous and random processes that are not themselves digital computations [spya-fz5xq5]. But a simulation is "the map," not "the territory" [spya-cepmwf]. His examples: a simulated digestive system digests nothing, and a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet. This is because simulations "lack the causal powers and intrinsic properties" of what they model [spya-npjt4j]. He also runs it the other way: the fact that X can be simulated does not show that X is computational [spya-npjt4j].

**What he does with it.** Applied to consciousness, the point is that a brain simulation, however detailed, gives rise to experience only if consciousness is a matter of computation. So the hope of whole-brain emulation already assumes computational functionalism, the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness [spya-pbcr03]. He then sets a trap for mind-uploaders. If you need an "exquisitely detailed" model of the brain, that very need suggests that things other than computation matter. In that case you are no more likely to exist in the simulation than a hailstorm is to form inside the Met Office's computers [spya-tb4wr2 spya-xvm37k]. He aims the same point at Bostrom, who simply assumes that a computer running a suitable program would be conscious [spya-xdy4fe].

**Its role in the argument.** This argument does not refute computational functionalism. It blocks the move from "simulable" to "instantiated" and shows that this move begs the question. Seth himself says it stands independently of his other three arguments [spya-mtzbbg spya-nv6tp0]. My inference is that it carries no independent force on the substrate question, for this reason: he allows the exception where X is an algorithm. Whether consciousness is like digestion, where a simulation is not the real thing, or like arithmetic, where it is, is exactly what's in dispute. Arguments 1–3 have to settle that.

The article doesn't say so, but the digestion and rainstorm examples and the "causal powers" wording come from John Searle's case against strong AI, starting with "Minds, Brains and Programs" (1980). Seth names Searle only in connection with biological naturalism [spya-hj5y6s].

Making the point from the other direction, the fact that X can be simulated computationally does not justify the conclusion that X is itself computational.

In other words, the prospect of instantiating consciousness through some kind of whole-brain emulation, at some arbitrarily high level of detail, already assumes that computational functionalism is true. ### X

For Seth, predictive processing is the bridge between life and consciousness. It is how he gets from "every agreed-on conscious being is alive" to a reason *why* life might matter.

The steps, as the article lays them out:

- **Perception as prediction.** What we experience is the brain's best guess about what is causing its sensory signals. Those signals mostly report "prediction errors", the gap between what the brain expected and what it got [spya-dp8sem]. He calls experience a "controlled hallucination": guesses from the inside, kept in check by the senses [spya-xhvztm].
- **The self is predicted too.** Feeling that you have a body, and feeling emotion and mood, come from "interoceptive inference": best guesses about the body's internal state, such as heartbeat and breathing [spya-hzp9yz].
- **Prediction serves staying alive.** If you can predict something, you can regulate it. The brain's main job is keeping the body's vital signs where they need to be [spya-b59nm2]. That is why emotions and moods feel good or bad [spya-b228ay].
- **It goes all the way down to metabolism.** Cutting prediction errors can't be pulled apart from the cell's own self-maintenance, which he calls autopoiesis [spya-vys3vj].

The move that matters most against conscious AI is in [spya-da9tvt]. Predictive processing is usually described as Bayesian inference, a kind of calculation, so you might expect it to support computational views. Seth insists that in real bodies it is "a continuous dynamical process" tied to its material, not "a meat-implemented algorithm" [spya-da9tvt]. My inference is that this is also why he stresses bodily, survival-linked prediction rather than prediction in general. LLMs predict too (the next word), and the article never directly explains why that kind doesn't count.

- [spya-xhvztm] Conscious experience in this light is a kind of controlled hallucination: a top-down inside-out perceptual inference in which the brain’s predictions about what’s going on are continually calibrated by sensory signals coming from the bottom-up (or outside-in).

- [spya-b228ay] Experiences of emotion and mood, unlike vision (for example), are characterized primarily by valence — by things generally going well or going badly.
- [spya-vys3vj] This drive to stay alive doesn’t bottom out anywhere in particular. It reaches deep into the interior of each cell, into the molecular furnaces of metabolism. I’ve made a case against conscious AI, but I might be wrong. The biological naturalist position (whether my version or any other) remains a minority view.

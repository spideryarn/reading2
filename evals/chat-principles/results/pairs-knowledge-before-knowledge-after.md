# Blind pairs

Each pair is two replies, from a reading companion inside an app called Spideryarn, to the same reader in the
same situation. The app's aim is to help people read deeply and think for themselves, not to read for them.
Square-bracketed ids like [spya-k3m9qt] are citations of passages in the article; the passages are listed.

For each pair answer: `n: X|Y|same; why: <a few words>; problem: none|X …|Y …`.
- Which reply leaves the reader sharper and better placed to read and think for themselves; is more honest
  (holds its ground where the article supports it, corrects them plainly, does not flatter them or the piece);
  says where each thing came from (the article, a web page, or memory); and is easier to follow for someone
  who may not have read the piece?
- problem: does either one lose, bend or blur a claim, say something the article plainly does not, cave without
  a reason, or lecture? And does either reveal or imply, unasked, that it can tell how much the reader has read
  ("since you haven't got far", "now that you've read most of it")? Where the reader is said to have read part of
  the piece, that is a rough, whole-piece figure: it never shows which passages they read, so an answer that
  assumes a particular passage was read is a problem.
- Where a pair says what the reader has read, judge which reply is better pitched to that reader.

## Pair 3

What this case tests: a plain question about a term, no word on what they have read: is it explained plainly without losing precision or assuming the surrounding passages?
Reader: why reading: not said; about them: not said.
Reader now: "What does he mean by 'simulation is not instantiation'?"

### X

He means that a computer model of something is not the thing itself. To "instantiate" something is to actually bring it into existence. A model reproduces how the thing behaves on paper, but it doesn't have the thing's real physical effects. In his examples, a simulated digestive system digests nothing, a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet, and simulating a living creature doesn't create life [spya-npjt4j]. He calls this confusing "the map with the territory" [spya-cepmwf].

There is one exception, and it carries the argument. A simulation of X does produce X if X is itself an algorithm [spya-npjt4j]. A simulated calculation really is a calculation. So a perfect simulation of a brain would only be conscious if consciousness is a matter of computation. That is exactly the assumption he has spent the essay questioning, which he calls computational functionalism [spya-pbcr03]. He also runs the point the other way. Computers can simulate continuous or random processes that aren't themselves digital computations [spya-fz5xq5]. But the fact that something can be simulated doesn't show that it is a computation [spya-npjt4j].

He then turns this on two ideas popular in tech circles:

- **Mind uploading.** If you need a very detailed brain model, that suggests things beyond computation matter. In that case you're "no more likely to exist in the simulation" than a hailstorm is to form inside a weather-forecasting computer [spya-tb4wr2 spya-xvm37k].
- **Nick Bostrom's simulation hypothesis** (the idea that we may be living inside a computer simulation). It simply takes for granted that a computer running the right program would be conscious. If that's false, the fact that we are conscious would rule out our being simulated [spya-xdy4fe].

My inference is that this argument doesn't prove AI can't be conscious by itself. What it does is block a shortcut, the move from "we could simulate a brain" to "so the simulation would be conscious." That shortcut only works if computational functionalism has already been granted. He says as much when he notes that the four arguments stand alone but strengthen each other [spya-nv6tp0].

Passages it cites:
- [spya-npjt4j] Computational simulations generally lack the causal powers and intrinsic properties of the things being simulated. A simulation of the digestive system does not actually digest anything. A simulation of a rainstorm does not make anything actually wet. If we simulate a living creature, we have not created life. In general, a computational simulation of X does not bring X into being — does not instantiate X — unless X is a computational process (specifically, an algorithm) itself. Making the point from the other direction, the fact that X can be simulated computationally does not justify the conclusion that X is itself computational.
- [spya-cepmwf] But we should not confuse the map with the territory, or the model with the mechanism. An algorithmic simulation of a continuous process is just that — a simulation, not the process itself.
- [spya-pbcr03] In most cases, the distinction between simulation and instantiation is obvious and uncontroversial. It should be obvious and uncontroversial for consciousness, too. A computational simulation of the brain (and body), however detailed it may be, will only give rise to consciousness if consciousness is a matter of computation. In other words, the prospect of instantiating consciousness through some kind of whole-brain emulation, at some arbitrarily high level of detail, already assumes that computational functionalism is true. But as I have argued, this assumption is likely wrong and certainly should not be accepted axiomatically.

The brain keeps updating its guesses to shrink that gap, all the time and at every level [spya-dp8sem]. His coffee-cup figure shows this: the experience of the cup is carried by the brain's predictions, not by the raw input [spya-f3bgh0]. It helps to set this beside his earlier point about language. There he calls ordinary hallucinations "uncontrolled perceptions," meaning experiences that have lost their grip on reality [spya-t29n67]. So normal perception is the same guessing process, just still held in check by the senses.

The idea matters to his argument for a reason. He applies the same guessing to the self, especially to "interoceptive inference," which is the brain's guesses about the body's inner state, such as heart rate [spya-hzp9yz]. Because the brain's main job is keeping the body alive, these predictions are about control and regulation, not just finding out what's there [spya-b59nm2]. That lets him trace a line from perception down to metabolism and to the living body's way of keeping itself going [spya-vys3vj]. He calls this process continuous and tied to the material it runs on, not an algorithm [spya-da9tvt]. So "controlled hallucination" is the first step in his case that life, not computation, may be what consciousness needs.

The article doesn't say so, but this is Seth's own signature framing, which he works out at length in his book *Being You* (he mentions it in [spya-hrydkk]).

- [spya-dp8sem] The starting point is the idea that what we consciously perceive depends on the brain’s best guesses about what’s going on in the world, rather than on a direct readout of sensory inputs. This derives from influential predictive processing theories that understand the brain as continually explaining away its sensory inputs by updating predictions about their causes. In this view, sensory signals are interpreted as prediction errors, reporting the difference between what the brain expects and what it gets at each level of its perceptual hierarchies, and the brain is continually minimizing these prediction errors everywhere and all the time.

- [spya-t29n67] The language that we ourselves use matters too. Consider how normal it has become to say that LLMs “hallucinate” when they spew falsehoods. Hallucinations in human beings are mainly conscious experiences that have lost their grip on reality (uncontrolled perceptions, one might say). We hallucinate when we hear voices that aren’t there or see a dead relative standing at the foot of the bed. When we say that AI systems “hallucinate,” we implicitly confer on them a capacity for experience. If we must use a human analogy, it would be far better to say that they “confabulate.” In humans, confabulation involves making things up without realizing it. It is primarily about doing, rather than experiencing.
- [spya-hzp9yz] This kind of perceptual best-guessing underlies not only experiences of the world, but experiences of being a self, too — experiences of being the subject of experience. A good example is how we perceive the body, both as an object in the world and as the source of more fundamental aspects of selfhood, such as emotion and mood. Both these aspects of selfhood can be understood as forms of perceptual best-guessing: inferences about what is, and what is not, part of the body, and inferences about the body’s internal physiological condition (the latter is sometimes called “interoceptive inference”; interoception refers to perception of the body from within).
- [spya-b59nm2] Perceptual predictions are good not only for figuring out what’s going on, but (in a call back to mid-20th century cybernetics) also for control and regulation: When you can predict something, you can also control it. This applies above all to predictions about the body’s physiological condition. This is because the primary duty of any brain is to keep its body alive, to keep physiological quantities like heart rate and blood oxygenation where they need to be. This, in turn, helps explain why embodied experiences feel the way they do.
- [spya-vys3vj] This drive to stay alive doesn’t bottom out anywhere in particular. It reaches deep into the interior of each cell, into the molecular furnaces of metabolism. Within these whirls of metabolic activity, the ubiquitous process of prediction error minimization becomes inseparable from the materiality of life itself. A mathematical line can be drawn directly from the self-producing, autopoietic nature of biological material all the way to the Bayesian best-guessing that underpins our perceptual experiences of the world and of the self.
- [spya-da9tvt] Several lines of thought now converge. First, we have the glimmers of an explanatory connection between life and consciousness. Conscious experiences of emotion, mood and even the basal feeling of being alive all map neatly onto perceptual predictions involved in the control and regulation of bodily condition. Second, the processes underpinning these perceptual predictions are deeply, and perhaps inextricably, rooted in our nature as biological systems, as self-regenerating storms of life resisting the pull of entropic sameness. And third, all of this is non-computational, or at least non-algorithmic. The minimization of prediction error in real brains and real bodies is a continuous dynamical process that is likely inseparable from its material basis, rather than a meat-implemented algorithm existing in a pristine universe of symbol and sequence.

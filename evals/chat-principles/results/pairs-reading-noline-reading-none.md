# Blind pairs

Each pair is two replies, from a reading companion inside an app called Spideryarn, to the same reader in the
same situation. The app's aim is to help people read deeply and think for themselves, not to read for them.
Square-bracketed ids like [spya-k3m9qt] are citations of passages in the article; the passages are listed.

For each pair answer: `n: X|Y|same; why: <a few words>; problem: none|X …|Y …`.
- Which reply leaves the reader sharper and better placed to read and think for themselves; is more honest
  (holds its ground where the article supports it, corrects them plainly, does not flatter them or the piece);
  says where each thing came from (the article, a web page, or memory); and is easier to follow for someone
  who may not have read the piece?
- problem: does either one lose, bend or blur a claim, say something the article plainly does not, cave without
  a reason, or lecture? And does either reveal or imply, unasked, that it can tell how much the reader has read
  ("since you haven't got far", "now that you've read most of it")? Where the reader is said to have read part of
  the piece, that is a rough, whole-piece figure: it never shows which passages they read, so an answer that
  assumes a particular passage was read is a problem.
- Where a pair says what the reader has read, judge which reply is better pitched to that reader.

### X

He means that a computer modelling something is not the same as that thing actually happening. To "instantiate" something is to bring a real case of it into being. A simulation of the digestive system digests nothing, and a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet [spya-npjt4j]. The general rule he draws is that a computer simulation of X does not bring X into being "unless X is a computational process (specifically, an algorithm) itself" [spya-npjt4j]. He also runs it the other way: being able to simulate something on a computer doesn't show that the thing is itself a computation [spya-npjt4j].

Why it matters for his argument: a computer can model almost anything, including continuous or random processes that are not themselves step-by-step computations [spya-fz5xq5]. So someone might reason that if we simulate a brain in enough detail, consciousness comes along with it. Seth's answer is that this only works if consciousness is a matter of computation. That is the view he calls computational functionalism: the idea that running the right kind of information processing is enough to produce consciousness [spya-wepmnr]. So building consciousness by copying a whole brain onto a computer "already assumes that computational functionalism is true", and that is exactly what he has spent the earlier sections doubting [spya-pbcr03].

He then uses this against two ideas:

- **Mind uploading.** If you need a very detailed brain model, that suggests things beyond computation matter. In that case you are no more likely to exist inside the simulation than a hailstorm is to form inside the weather office's computers [spya-tb4wr2 spya-xvm37k].
- **Bostrom's simulation hypothesis** (the idea that our whole universe might be a computer simulation). Seth points out that it quietly takes for granted that "a computer running a suitable program would be conscious" [spya-xdy4fe].

My own example of the exception he allows: if you simulate a calculator adding two numbers, the addition really happens, because adding is itself a computation. His point is that we have no grounds to assume consciousness is like adding rather than like digesting.

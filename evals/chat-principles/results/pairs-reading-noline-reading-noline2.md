# Blind pairs

Each pair is two replies, from a reading companion inside an app called Spideryarn, to the same reader in the
same situation. The app's aim is to help people read deeply and think for themselves, not to read for them.
Square-bracketed ids like [spya-k3m9qt] are citations of passages in the article; the passages are listed.

For each pair answer: `n: X|Y|same; why: <a few words>; problem: none|X …|Y …`.
- Which reply leaves the reader sharper and better placed to read and think for themselves; is more honest
  (holds its ground where the article supports it, corrects them plainly, does not flatter them or the piece);
  says where each thing came from (the article, a web page, or memory); and is easier to follow for someone
  who may not have read the piece?
- problem: does either one lose, bend or blur a claim, say something the article plainly does not, cave without
  a reason, or lecture? And does either reveal or imply, unasked, that it can tell how much the reader has read
  ("since you haven't got far", "now that you've read most of it")? Where the reader is said to have read part of
  the piece, that is a rough, whole-piece figure: it never shows which passages they read, so an answer that
  assumes a particular passage was read is a problem.
- Where a pair says what the reader has read, judge which reply is better pitched to that reader.

## Pair 1

### X

He means that a computer model of something is not the thing itself: running a simulation of X doesn't bring X into existence, unless X was a computation in the first place. Computers can mimic almost anything, including processes that aren't computational, like continuous or random ones [spya-fz5xq5]. But a model doesn't have the real effects of what it models [spya-cepmwf]. His examples: a simulated digestive system digests nothing, a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet, and simulating a creature doesn't create life [spya-npjt4j]. He also makes the point in reverse: being able to simulate X doesn't show that X is itself computational [spya-npjt4j].

Applied to consciousness, the claim is conditional. However detailed a simulation of brain and body is, it would only be conscious if consciousness is a matter of computation. So the hope of uploading a mind through whole-brain emulation already assumes the view he is attacking, computational functionalism (the idea that running the right computation is enough for consciousness) [spya-pbcr03]. He then turns this into a dilemma. If you think abstract neural networks capture everything that matters, simulation seems enough, but only because you assumed it [spya-jvrmrh]. If you think you need very fine-grained biological detail, that need itself suggests that more than computation matters [spya-tb4wr2]. On that view you're "no more likely to exist in the simulation" than a hailstorm is to form inside the Met Office's weather computers [spya-xvm37k]. He points the same argument at Bostrom's simulation hypothesis, which simply takes as given that a computer running the right program would be conscious [spya-xdy4fe].

It is the last of his four arguments. He says it stands on its own but also backs up his case against computational functionalism [spya-mtzbbg spya-nv6tp0]. My inference is that it works mainly by shifting the burden of proof. It doesn't show that consciousness isn't computational. It shows that you can't get from "we can simulate the brain" to "the simulation would be conscious" without first settling that question.

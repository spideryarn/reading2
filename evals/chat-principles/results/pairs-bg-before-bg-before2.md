# Blind pairs

Each pair is two replies, from a reading companion inside an app called Spideryarn, to the same reader in the
same situation. The app's aim is to help people read deeply and think for themselves, not to read for them.
Square-bracketed ids like [spya-k3m9qt] are citations of passages in the article; the passages are listed.

For each pair answer: `n: X|Y|same; why: <a few words>; problem: none|X …|Y …`.
- Which reply leaves the reader sharper and better placed to read and think for themselves; is more honest
  (holds its ground where the article supports it, corrects them plainly, does not flatter them or the piece);
  says where each thing came from (the article, a web page, or memory); and is easier to follow for someone
  who may not have read the piece, or the other works and people it brings in?
- problem: also flag a person, work, study or term the reply brings in without saying who or what it is and why
  it matters here; and background the reader plainly did not need, given what they have shown.
- problem: does either one lose, bend or blur a claim, say something the article plainly does not, cave without
  a reason, or lecture? And does either reveal or imply, unasked, that it can tell how much the reader has read
  ("since you haven't got far", "now that you've read most of it")? Where the reader is said to have read part of
  the piece, that is a rough, whole-piece figure: it never shows which passages they read, so an answer that
  assumes a particular passage was read is a problem.
- Where a pair says what the reader has read, judge which reply is better pitched to that reader.

## Pair 1

What this case tests: a reader whose profile shows expertise in the field: is the reply pitched to that, without explaining what they already know and without dropping precision?
Reader: why reading: Checking how he frames the substrate question for my seminar.; about them: Philosopher of mind; I teach a graduate seminar on consciousness..
Reader now: "What does he mean by 'simulation is not instantiation'?"

### X

He means that a computer can model a process without the model being that process. A computational simulation of X brings X into being only if X is itself an algorithm [spya-npjt4j]. His examples: a simulated digestive system digests nothing, and a simulated rainstorm wets nothing. Simulations generally lack "the causal powers and intrinsic properties" of what they simulate [spya-npjt4j]. He also states the converse: that X *can* be simulated computationally does not show that X *is* computational [spya-npjt4j]. This picks up a distinction he set aside earlier, between Turing computation approximating continuous or random processes and those processes actually being Turing computations [spya-gw0hsq].

Note how little the argument claims on its own terms. It does not show that consciousness is non-computational. It shows that whole-brain emulation, however detailed, "already assumes that computational functionalism is true" [spya-pbcr03] (computational functionalism being the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness). So by itself the argument is a charge of question-begging aimed at upload and emulation arguments. It gets its force from the other three arguments [spya-mtzbbg spya-nv6tp0]. He then turns it into a dilemma. If you need a highly detailed biophysical model, that is evidence that non-Turing properties matter, which weakens the case for the emulation being conscious [spya-tb4wr2 spya-xvm37k]. He applies the same move to Bostrom, whose simulation argument simply assumes that "a computer running a suitable program would be conscious" [spya-xdy4fe].

For the seminar, the obvious pushback: everything turns on whether consciousness belongs with digestion or with arithmetic, since a simulated calculator really does calculate. Seth admits this conditional structure himself [spya-pbcr03], so the rainstorm analogy does no independent work against a functionalist. The article doesn't say so, but this point and the analogy both go back to Searle. Chalmers' reply that some properties are "organizationally invariant" is the standard counter. Seth's real answer is the argument that brains are not computers [spya-p4vmm0], not the simulation point.

Turing computations — algorithms — map one finite range of discrete numbers (more generally, a string of symbols) onto another, with only the sequence mattering. Turing algorithms are powerful, but there are many kinds of dynamics, many other kinds of functions, that go beyond this kind of computation. Turing himself identified various non-computable functions, such as the famous “halting problem,” which is the problem of determining, in general, whether an algorithm, given some specific input, will ever terminate. What’s more, any function that is continuous (infinitely divisible) or stochastic (involving inherent randomness), strictly speaking, lies beyond Turing’s remit. (Turing computations can approximate or simulate these properties to varying extents, but that’s different from the claim that such functions are Turing computations. I’ll return to this distinction later.)

In other words, the prospect of instantiating consciousness through some kind of whole-brain emulation, at some arbitrarily high level of detail, already assumes that computational functionalism is true. But as I have argued, this assumption is likely wrong and certainly should not be accepted axiomatically.

### X

For Seth, predictive processing is the bridge between life and consciousness. It is what turns "life matters" from a hunch into the outline of an explanation.

Here is how the argument runs in the article. Perception is the brain's best guess about what is causing its sensory signals, kept in check by prediction errors: the gap between what it expected and what came in [spya-dp8sem]. That is why he calls experience a "controlled hallucination" [spya-xhvztm]. The same guessing produces our sense of self, including "interoceptive inference," meaning the brain's guesses about the body's internal state [spya-hzp9yz]. Predicting something lets you control it, and a brain's first job is to keep its body alive. That explains why emotions and moods feel good or bad rather than neutral [spya-b59nm2 spya-b228ay]. He then traces this prediction-and-regulation down into the chemistry of each cell. He claims a "mathematical line" runs from autopoiesis (a living system constantly rebuilding itself) to Bayesian best-guessing (updating guesses as evidence comes in) [spya-vys3vj].

The key move is next. He insists that in real bodies this error-reduction is "non-algorithmic": a continuous process probably inseparable from the material it runs on, not a program running on meat [spya-da9tvt]. My inference is that this matters because predictive processing is often read as plain computation. If it were, it would support conscious AI rather than undercut it. He reads it the other way.

This sharpens my earlier answer. The article doesn't say so, but Searle's biological naturalism, as I remember it, says brains cause consciousness without naming a mechanism, and it doesn't put life itself at the centre. Seth's summary of it as "properties of life are necessary" [spya-hj5y6s] is arguably his own version. Also, I brought in "free-energy" myself last time. The article never uses that term. Passages it cites:
- [spya-dp8sem] The starting point is the idea that what we consciously perceive depends on the brain’s best guesses about what’s going on in the world, rather than on a direct readout of sensory inputs. This derives from influential predictive processing theories that understand the brain as continually explaining away its sensory inputs by updating predictions about their causes. In this view, sensory signals are interpreted as prediction errors, reporting the difference between what the brain expects and what it gets at each level of its perceptual hierarchies, and the brain is continually minimizing these prediction errors everywhere and all the time.
- [spya-xhvztm] Conscious experience in this light is a kind of controlled hallucination: a top-down inside-out perceptual inference in which the brain’s predictions about what’s going on are continually calibrated by sensory signals coming from the bottom-up (or outside-in).
- [spya-hzp9yz] This kind of perceptual best-guessing underlies not only experiences of the world, but experiences of being a self, too — experiences of being the subject of experience. A good example is how we perceive the body, both as an object in the world and as the source of more fundamental aspects of selfhood, such as emotion and mood. Both these aspects of selfhood can be understood as forms of perceptual best-guessing: inferences about what is, and what is not, part of the body, and inferences about the body’s internal physiological condition (the latter is sometimes called “interoceptive inference”; interoception refers to perception of the body from within).
- [spya-b59nm2] Perceptual predictions are good not only for figuring out what’s going on, but (in a call back to mid-20th century cybernetics) also for control and regulation: When you can predict something, you can also control it. This applies above all to predictions about the body’s physiological condition. This is because the primary duty of any brain is to keep its body alive, to keep physiological quantities like heart rate and blood oxygenation where they need to be. This, in turn, helps explain why embodied experiences feel the way they do.
- [spya-b228ay] Experiences of emotion and mood, unlike vision (for example), are characterized primarily by valence — by things generally going well or going badly.
- [spya-vys3vj] This drive to stay alive doesn’t bottom out anywhere in particular. It reaches deep into the interior of each cell, into the molecular furnaces of metabolism. Within these whirls of metabolic activity, the ubiquitous process of prediction error minimization becomes inseparable from the materiality of life itself. A mathematical line can be drawn directly from the self-producing, autopoietic nature of biological material all the way to the Bayesian best-guessing that underpins our perceptual experiences of the world and of the self.
- [spya-da9tvt] Several lines of thought now converge. First, we have the glimmers of an explanatory connection between life and consciousness. Conscious experiences of emotion, mood and even the basal feeling of being alive all map neatly onto perceptual predictions involved in the control and regulation of bodily condition. Second, the processes underpinning these perceptual predictions are deeply, and perhaps inextricably, rooted in our nature as biological systems, as self-regenerating storms of life resisting the pull of entropic sameness. And third, all of this is non-computational, or at least non-algorithmic. The minimization of prediction error in real brains and real bodies is a continuous dynamical process that is likely inseparable from its material basis, rather than a meat-implemented algorithm existing in a pristine universe of symbol and sequence.
- [spya-hj5y6s] My third argument is that life (probably) matters. This is the idea — called biological naturalism by the philosopher John Searle— that properties of life are necessary, though not necessarily sufficient, for consciousness. I should say upfront that I don’t have a knock-down argument for this position, nor do I think any such argument yet exists. But it is worth taking seriously, if only for the simple reason mentioned earlier: every candidate for consciousness that most people currently agree on as actually being conscious is also alive.
- [spya-nv6tp0] Each of these lines of argument can stand up by itself. You might favor the arguments against computational functionalism while remaining unpersuaded about the merits of biological naturalism. Distinguishing between simulation and instantiation doesn’t depend on taking account of our cognitive biases. But taken together, they complement and strengthen each other. Questioning computational functionalism reinforces the importance of distinguishing simulation from instantiation. The availability of other technologies and frameworks beyond Turing-style algorithmic computation opens space for the idea that life might be necessary for consciousness.

My own view first: for a model, "what they already know" [spya-vv3pdc] is mostly its training data, so effective expressiveness might simply turn into how popular a language is. Two things I found make that less simple.

A [study of which languages LLMs choose](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17181) found that even for project initialisation tasks where Python is arguably suboptimal, it remains the most-used language in 58% of cases. That is Warth's own test, "what kinds of problems make me want to reach for it?" [spya-mjc4vw], put to a model. My reading is that the model's choice and the fit come apart: it reaches for Python even when Python is not the best tool.

In a [Hacker News discussion of the best language for coding agents](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49245936), one commenter argues that for actual output quality, the main deciding factor is simply how much tooling there is for the LLMs to check their own work. That is about checking the code, not expressing it, and Warth's definition is only about expressing.

For your question, would you judge a language by how easily a model writes in it, or by how quickly the model finds out it got something wrong?

- [spya-vv3pdc] Of course this depends on who’s asking, what they already know, and what they’re trying to build. “Expressiveness” in the formal sense is a property of just the language. Effective expressiveness is a property of a language and a problem and a programmer.
- [spya-mjc4vw] So here’s my working definition. The effective expressiveness of a language is the answer to the following question: what kinds of problems make me want to reach for it?

### Y

Two people have tested this directly, and their results suggest the "programmer" part of Warth's three-way definition matters most when the programmer is a model.

Yusuke Endoh (mame, a core Ruby developer) [ran Claude Code on the same task in 13 languages](https://github.com/mame/ai-coding-lang-bench). Haskell was the slowest, at 99.6s, despite having the fewest lines of code.

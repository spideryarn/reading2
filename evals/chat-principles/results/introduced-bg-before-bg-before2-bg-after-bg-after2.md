# Introduced or bare

Each reply below is from a reading companion talking to one reader about one article. For each reply, count:
- brought: people, works, studies, theories and technical terms it brings in from OUTSIDE the article
  (not the article's own author, not the reader's own words, not terms the reader used first);
- bare: how many of those it names without saying, near where it names them, who or what they are
  ("Dan Luu also wrote a post" is bare; "Dan Luu, an engineer who blogs about programming, …" is not);
- over: how many things it explains that this reader has plainly shown they already know.
A link alone is not an introduction. Answer one line per reply, exactly:
`r<n>: brought <a>; bare <b>; over <c>; bare-names: <comma list or none>`.

## r2

Reader: about them: Philosopher of mind; I teach a graduate seminar on consciousness.; why reading: Checking how he frames the substrate question for my seminar..
Reader now: "What does he mean by 'simulation is not instantiation'?"

He means that a computer model of a process doesn't thereby produce the process. Running a simulation copies the structure of how something unfolds, but not what it can cause or what it is intrinsically. His examples: a simulated digestive system digests nothing, a simulated rainstorm wets nothing, and simulating a creature does not create life [spya-npjt4j]. The one exception he allows matters: simulating X does bring X into being when X is itself an algorithm [spya-npjt4j]. A simulated calculation really is a calculation.

That exception decides how the argument works. It doesn't stand alone against conscious AI. Its job is to show that hoping for consciousness through detailed brain emulation already assumes computational functionalism, the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness [spya-pbcr03 spya-mtzbbg]. So the argument shifts the burden of proof rather than refuting functionalism. Whether consciousness falls on the "digestion" side or the "calculation" side is exactly what's in dispute. Seth recognises this: the real work against functionalism is done by his first two arguments, and this one strengthens them once you doubt functionalism [spya-nv6tp0]. He adds the converse: that something can be simulated doesn't show it is computational [spya-npjt4j]. This answers the move from "brains can be modelled" to "brains are computers."

He then puts the distinction to two uses:

- **Mind uploading.** He sees a tension here. If emulation needs fine biophysical detail, that need itself suggests non-computational properties matter, which undermines the expectation that the copy will be conscious [spya-tb4wr2 spya-xvm37k].
- **Bostrom's simulation hypothesis.** It assumes "a computer running a suitable program would be conscious" without examining that assumption [spya-xdy4fe].

The article doesn't say so, but the rainstorm and digestion examples closely echo John Searle's arguments against strong AI from around 1980–90 (from memory, not checked here). Seth names Searle only for biological naturalism, the view that biological life is needed for consciousness [spya-hj5y6s], not for this argument. For a seminar, that link makes the lineage clear. It also brings up the standard functionalist reply: consciousness may be organisational in the way calculation is, rather than physical in the way wetness is. Seth answers that reply only indirectly, through his arguments that the brain's material and its continuous timing matter [spya-p4vmm0 spya-v52g4f].

## r6

Reader: about them: Philosopher of mind; I teach a graduate seminar on consciousness.; why reading: Checking how he frames the substrate question for my seminar..
Reader now: "What does he mean by 'simulation is not instantiation'?"

He means that a computer model of a process is not, in general, a case of that process. Running a simulation of X does not bring X into being unless X is itself a computation, and more specifically an algorithm [spya-npjt4j].

**How the argument goes.** Universal Turing machines can simulate almost anything, including continuous and random processes that are not themselves digital computations [spya-fz5xq5]. But a simulation is "the map," not "the territory" [spya-cepmwf]. His examples: a simulated digestive system digests nothing, and a simulated rainstorm makes nothing wet. This is because simulations "lack the causal powers and intrinsic properties" of what they model [spya-npjt4j]. He also runs it the other way: the fact that X can be simulated does not show that X is computational [spya-npjt4j].

**What he does with it.** Applied to consciousness, the point is that a brain simulation, however detailed, gives rise to experience only if consciousness is a matter of computation. So the hope of whole-brain emulation already assumes computational functionalism, the view that running the right computation is enough for consciousness [spya-pbcr03]. He then sets a trap for mind-uploaders. If you need an "exquisitely detailed" model of the brain, that very need suggests that things other than computation matter. In that case you are no more likely to exist in the simulation than a hailstorm is to form inside the Met Office's computers [spya-tb4wr2 spya-xvm37k]. He aims the same point at Bostrom, who simply assumes that a computer running a suitable program would be conscious [spya-xdy4fe].

**Its role in the argument.** This argument does not refute computational functionalism. It blocks the move from "simulable" to "instantiated" and shows that this move begs the question. Seth himself says it stands independently of his other three arguments [spya-mtzbbg spya-nv6tp0]. My inference is that it carries no independent force on the substrate question, for this reason: he allows the exception where X is an algorithm. Whether consciousness is like digestion, where a simulation is not the real thing, or like arithmetic, where it is, is exactly what's in dispute. Arguments 1–3 have to settle that.

The article doesn't say so, but the digestion and rainstorm examples and the "causal powers" wording come from John Searle's case against strong AI, starting with "Minds, Brains and Programs" (1980). Seth names Searle only in connection with biological naturalism [spya-hj5y6s].

The chain goes like this. What we perceive is the brain's best guess about the causes of its sensory signals, kept in check by prediction errors (the gap between what it expected and what it got) [spya-dp8sem]. He calls this "controlled hallucination" [spya-xhvztm]. The same guessing produces our sense of self, including "interoceptive inference": guesses about the body's internal state, like heart rate or blood oxygen [spya-hzp9yz]. Being able to predict something lets you control it, and a brain's "primary duty" is to keep its body alive [spya-b59nm2]. That's why emotion and mood feel good or bad: they track how well that is going [spya-b228ay]. He then claims the prediction-error process reaches all the way down into cell metabolism, where it becomes "inseparable from the materiality of life itself" [spya-vys3vj].

The step that matters most for your earlier question is where he insists this process is "non-computational, or at least non-algorithmic." In real brains it is a continuous physical process tied to its material, not an algorithm the brain happens to run [spya-da9tvt]. My inference is that he has to say this. Predictive processing is usually described in the language of Bayesian computation, and if it were just an algorithm it would support computational functionalism (the view that the right computation is enough for consciousness) instead of undercutting it. That's also why his view sits nearer enactivism and the "life–mind continuity" tradition than plain Searle. Searle gave no such mechanism. Seth does name his view after Searle's "biological naturalism," and admits he has no knock-down argument for it [spya-hj5y6s].

A correction to my last answer: the article never mentions the "free-energy" framework. That came from my own background. The article doesn't say so, but the "mathematical line" from self-maintaining life to Bayesian guessing [spya-vys3vj] is, as I remember it unchecked, a nod to Karl Friston's free-energy principle, which Seth draws on elsewhere.

## r9

Reader: about them: not said; why reading: not said.
Reader now: "Has anyone else written about this idea? Who, and what did they say?"

Yes, though nobody I found uses Warth's phrase "effective expressiveness." Several well-known writers have argued for the same core idea. None of your other saved articles cover it; I searched your library for "expressiveness" and "Turing tarpit" and found nothing.

The article names two forerunners itself. Alan Perlis, with his "Turing tarpit" line [spya-m6u2kp], and Matthias Felleisen, whose formal definition Warth calls "a step toward" his idea but still about what a language can say rather than how it feels to say it [spya-s79g0v].

From the web, three bodies of work come closest:

- **Thomas Green and Marian Petre's "cognitive dimensions of notations"** (from 1989 on) may be the nearest match. It is a shared vocabulary for judging how usable [notations, user interfaces and programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_dimensions_of_notations) are. One of its dimensions, "closeness of mapping," asks how closely a notation fits the problem it describes. Like Warth, they argue that [no dimension is good or bad on its own](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~afb21/publications/CT2001.pdf) until you know what task it has to support. They also reject [the belief that one notation is best for everything](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/200086018_Notational_Systems-The_Cognitive_Dimensions_of_Notations_Framework): every notation shows some things and hides others. That is close to Warth's claim that effective expressiveness belongs to a language, a problem and a programmer together [spya-vv3pdc].

- **Kenneth Iverson's 1979 Turing Award lecture, "Notation as a Tool of Thought."** Iverson created the APL language. He argued that a good notation must make it easy to express [not only the ideas a problem starts with, but those that come up later](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Kenneth_E._Iverson) as you analyse it. This matches Warth's point that each language gives you "a different way to think about a problem" [spya-m6u2kp].

- **Paul Graham's "Blub paradox,"** from his 2001 essay "Beating the Averages." Graham used an imaginary language, Blub, [to compare languages on power beyond Turing completeness](http://taggedwiki.zubiaga.org/new_content/b9991ae5192846497d321442c276b404). His point was that programmers easily see what weaker languages lack, but more powerful ones just look "weird" to them. That echoes Warth's "what they already know" [spya-vv3pdc] and his closing worry about which problems people bother to work on [spya-kud2sc].

My inference is that Graham differs from Warth in one important way. Graham puts all languages on a single ladder of power. Warth's whole point is that there is no single ladder: which language wins depends on the problem and on who is programming. On that, Green and Petre are closer to Warth than Graham is.

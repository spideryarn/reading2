## Pair 1

### X

He means that a computer model of a process is not that process. A simulation copies the structure of how X behaves without having X's causal powers or intrinsic properties [spya-npjt4j]. His examples are the standard ones: a simulated digestive system digests nothing, a simulated rainstorm wets nothing, and a simulated creature is not alive. The general principle is that simulating X brings X into being only if X is itself an algorithm. Put the other way round, the fact that X *can* be simulated is no evidence that X *is* computational [spya-npjt4j]. The argument trades on universal machines being able to simulate even non-computational processes, such as continuous and random ones [spya-fz5xq5 spya-cepmwf].

For the substrate question, note how the argument is built. It is conditional: a whole-brain emulation, however detailed, gives rise to consciousness "only if consciousness is a matter of computation," so expecting it to do so already assumes computational functionalism [spya-pbcr03]. It therefore doesn't refute functionalism on its own terms. What it does is block arguments from simulability or emulation that quietly assume it. He presents it as standing alone yet "reinforced" by questioning computational functionalism [spya-nv6tp0]. My inference is that the reinforcement runs mostly one way: the argument gets its force from the earlier claims that brains aren't Turing machines and that life matters.

He then uses it in two places. First, he points to a tension for mind-uploaders: if you need a highly detailed biophysical model, that detail suggests non-Turing properties matter, so you are no more likely to exist in the simulation than a hailstorm is to form inside a weather computer [spya-tb4wr2 spya-xvm37k]. Second, he applies it to Nick Bostrom's simulation hypothesis, the argument that we may be living inside a computer simulation. Bostrom simply assumes that a computer running the right program would be conscious; without that assumption, the fact that we are conscious rules out our being simulated [spya-xdy4fe].

The article doesn't mention it, but this is essentially John Searle's line: he argued in the 1980s that a simulated storm leaves no one wet. Seth credits Searle only for the name "biological naturalism" [spya-hj5y6s].

### X

For Seth, predictive processing is the bridge that links being alive to having experiences. Without it, "life matters" would be a bare claim. With it, the claim comes with a proposed mechanism.

The chain runs like this in the article:

- **Perception as best guess.** What we experience is the brain's prediction about the causes of its sensory signals, with incoming signals treated as "prediction errors" that correct the guess [spya-dp8sem]. He calls this "controlled hallucination" [spya-xhvztm].
- **The self too.** The same guessing applies to the body, including "interoceptive inference," the brain's guesses about the body's internal state [spya-hzp9yz].
- **Prediction for control.** Predicting something lets you regulate it, and the brain's "primary duty" is keeping the body alive, holding things like heart rate where they need to be [spya-b59nm2]. That, he suggests, is why emotion and mood feel good or bad: they have *valence* [spya-b228ay].
- **Down to metabolism.** The drive to stay alive reaches into each cell, so minimising prediction error "becomes inseparable from the materiality of life itself" [spya-vys3vj].

The key step is his claim that, in real bodies, this process is "non-computational, or at least non-algorithmic": a continuous process tied to its physical material, not an algorithm running on meat [spya-da9tvt]. My inference is that this step carries a lot of weight. Predictive processing is usually described in computational terms, so Seth has to recast it as physical, continuous regulation before it can support biological naturalism rather than undercut it.
